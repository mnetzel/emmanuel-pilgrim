Hello everyone,

Three months without news—I’m overdoing it a bit.

Here’s the update:

Since last time, many good things have happened, and no bad things. That’s a decent balance.

Still, I feel like complaining. Firstly, because it’s a basic human need, and secondly, because I’m French. As the cliché goes, the French love to complain, followed by a good protest.

My chosen subject of complaint: the temperature. For the past two and a half months, it’s been consistently 35⁰C, with spikes to 38⁰C and even 43⁰C on three occasions.

It’s unbearable. Walking in this sun and heat is no longer just challenging; it’s becoming dangerous. I’ve had to adjust my walking schedule to avoid the midday extremes.

I now wake up 30 minutes to an hour before sunrise to pack my things. When daylight breaks, I fold the tent and start walking early, continuing until around 11 a.m. or noon—longer if I haven’t found a suitable resting spot. Romania is an hour ahead of Central Europe, so the timing feels slightly off.

I stop in the shade of forests, caves, or villages—often meeting new people in cafés or restaurants. These breaks allow me to recharge my batteries—both electrical and social. Sometimes, I’m invited to stay, which I often accept, drawn by the promise of a shower and good company.

I set out again around 4:30 or 5 p.m., walking until I find a welcoming place to stop or until the sun sets, usually around 8 p.m.

I don’t always pitch the tent. In this summer weather, sleeping under the stars is comfortable, with the Milky Way serving as my sleeping bag. It also saves time tidying up in the morning.

Sleeping outdoors has taught me to tolerate the local fauna. Insects often crawl over me as I sleep, but I’ve learned not to react. An earwig running across my skin or a beetle tangled in my hair no longer bothers me as much. I used to think they got caught intentionally, but after observing, I realized their shell design is to blame. If I don’t carefully untangle them, they risk losing legs or antennae—or worse, pulling out my hair.

Some beetles pinch, likely to express their discomfort, creating mine. When that happens, I wake up, fully aware of the incident—unlike many other encounters that don’t disturb my sleep. It’s like how we notice a sore knee only when we bump it, even though we probably hit it just as often when it doesn’t hurt. A beetle in your hair is a similar, unforgettable experience.

Larger creatures, from shrews and hedgehogs to grass snakes, foxes, and weasels, tend to keep their distance. Without the tent obscuring their view or smell, they quickly recognize me as human—the ultimate nightmare—and flee without hesitation.

If they are sane, everyone flees. So when that doesn’t happen, it’s a notable and often touching event.

For example, one morning during this pilgrimage, I was lying on my mattress (it’s still necessary to isolate yourself from the dampness of the ground to sleep better), my head resting on my hand, against my bag, and leaning against a tree at the edge of a wood. Like a hunting dog curled up and asleep, I faced a small grassy clearing. I was awakened by a presence.

It didn’t feel alarming. I stayed calm, listening. I believe the brain, even while asleep, can hear and analyze sounds in the immediate environment, alerting us if necessary. I’ve experienced this four times now during nocturnal visits from bears. But this time, there was no urgency, no danger.

I opened my eyes. About fifteen meters away, an enormous doe was grazing nonchalantly. I say enormous because it was not a deer but an adult doe—and an adult doe is large. She grazed noisily on the grass. Well, noisy for someone like me, now awake and trying to be still. In the calm morning silence of nature, after a good night’s sleep, I lay motionless, amazed by the spectacle. Usually, such encounters are fleeting, as frightened animals quickly flee.

I savored the moment, staying as still as possible while observing her. Then, about ten meters away, I noticed a small fawn, likely old enough to have lost its spots. I wanted to lift my head slightly to see it better but didn’t dare move.

Eventually, I had to adjust—or perhaps my breathing changed—because the doe suddenly raised her angular head. Her whole body tensed, motionless, on alert.

That’s when we locked eyes. We stared at each other, directly into each other’s gaze—a moment suspended in time. It felt so real that it was almost unreal. Probably just a fraction of a second, but it felt eternal. Both of us were frozen in attentive silence.

And then, as nothing happened, she seemed to decide that I wasn’t a threat and resumed grazing.

That’s what moved me the most. The moment of eye contact was intense, but her choice to start grazing again, despite knowing the worst of predators—the most destructive of creatures—was nearby, truly touched me. It felt as though she had decided to trust me.

I felt accepted, as if I had become part of her world. You can’t imagine how proud this made me. It’s a moment etched in my memory, one that strengthens me when I recall it. Hindus have a term for such experiences: “Darshan.”

We stayed like that for a few minutes. She tore at the tender grass, taking a step between each bite, while I remained still, listening to my heart pound as if I were receiving a kiss for the first time.

These are the kinds of moments that can happen when you sleep under the stars.

I’m nearing the Serbian-Romanian border. My journey continues toward the sanctuary of Medjugorje in Bosnia-Herzegovina, which means crossing Serbia.

I hope the summer won’t be as hot as the spring, though I’m not optimistic.

I hope everything is going well for you and wish you health and happiness.

See you soon.

P.S.:
     I have a good excuse for being late with updates. I’ve overexposed myself to phone screens while working on putting a blog online. I’ll share the address once it’s ready.

Writing and sending these updates, attaching photos, and sending them to all my contacts has become tedious. Platforms like Signal or WhatsApp only allow sending to five contacts at a time. Between each batch, I have to reselect text and photos, then sift through my contact list to choose the next five while remembering who hasn’t received it yet.

With several hundred contacts—and the list growing—the process isn’t getting any easier.

The blog should simplify things while improving practicality. It might even reach others who haven’t met me but could appreciate the content.

What content? Nearly all the updates I’ve sent since starting this pilgrimage in September 2020, plus a selection of the best photos, and complete answers to frequently asked questions.

Voila. A good excuse, right?
