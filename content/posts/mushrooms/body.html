Porcini mushrooms (Boletus edulis) everywhere! My God, I can't pick them all. It’s no use—my stove is broken, so I only take those I can eat raw. And of course, I limit myself to one kilo, because carrying more gets heavy, and I know I'll find more tomorrow...

Anyway, it feels strange to leave behind nice, big Corks (in France, we call them "Corks" because they resemble the shape of champagne corks). I already have 4 or 5 in my portable pantry...

Some very young porcini mushrooms (the ones in the photos were a bit too mature to eat raw without seasoning, but they were majestic. One of them must have weighed nearly a kilo—very firm), some Pinkys (our nickname in France for Agaricus campestris because of the lovely pink color under the cap), and some Airy Coprins (Coprinus comatus). It’s been a long time since I last ate those. They’re really, really good... in fricassee or soup (considering how much water they release when cooking, a bit of butter and garlic turns them into a lovely evening soup).

But since my stove is damaged, I eat them raw too. They’re tender and melt in your mouth, but of course, they don’t last long—they quickly turn into a thick black paste, almost like ink...

Also, I’ve never eaten so many nuts! All the walnut trees I come across are laden with fruit, and the ground beneath them is scattered with fallen nuts. People in the region leave them on the ground, so I take full advantage.

And the apples! I collect some every day. Apparently, no one picks them, even though they’re absolutely delicious. Far better than the dull, floury ones you find in supermarkets...

Luckily, I come across all this bounty along the way—it really eases the food stress when passing through these deserted villages. Sometimes there’s not a single grocery store for four days. As a result, my pantry empties out, only to refill with nuts, apples, and occasionally pears, as well as mushrooms.

Often, when I’m about to admit—almost ashamed—that “there’s nothing left in our villages these days,” a local will kindly offer me something to eat. How touching. Sometimes it’s a sandwich, a slice of cake, or some fruit...

I’m happy to see and experience exactly the opposite of what the media say. The usual, “My God, you have to be careful these days! Anything can happen! You can be attacked at any crossroads, robbed, raped, or even beheaded!” Instead, all I encounter is kindness, goodness, and benevolence wherever I go and whoever I meet.

Imagine this: on several occasions, total strangers have invited me to their homes to take a shower! I’m invited to come in, eat, drink coffee, wash… It really doesn’t make me want to like television or newspapers, spreading these ideas...

But it’s funny sometimes to see how even those same people—kind and welcoming—have reflexes conditioned by that mainstream, vague, and formless fear hovering over everyone:

“But aren’t you afraid?”
     “Afraid of what?”
     “Well… everything, bad encounters, all that...”
     “Well, no. Look, I’m meeting you, and it’s going well.”
     “Yes, but it’s not the same. We can see you’re a nice guy...”
     Honestly, I don’t think there are that many bad people out there. But hey, let’s stay careful—you never know... 😜

Oh, the other day, a gentleman who was vaguely part of my conversation with a mechanic couple left for ten minutes to go home. When he came back, he handed me a bag with two bananas, four clementines, two yogurts, and a packet of biscuits. I didn’t ask him for anything—I never ask—but he told me, “There’s no grocery store in the next villages you’ll pass through.”

It’s moments like these that reconcile you with society, with humanity… well, if you were ever dis-conciled. 😁

That’s all I wanted to say.

I’m doing fine. Slowly but surely, I walk the trails as much as possible. Sometimes it doesn’t rain, and even when it does, it rarely rains all day—it’s only happened three times so far.

Today, I’m staying in Orval Abbey. It’s in Belgium, right on the border. It’s nice—shower and hot meal on the agenda. Feels so good. Their beer is great.

See you soon.
