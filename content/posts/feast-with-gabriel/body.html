I would like to drive the point home, because some nails deserve to be nailed, and the subject of the last message is one of them. I hope this expression makes sense in English, as it does in French.

So, coming back to my last message: I wrote it as I was leaving a pretty little village to head toward the mountains (or rather hills—no offense, but I spent several years in the high Himalayan altitudes, so...) of the Carpathians. To be precise, sub-Carpathian Transylvania! Yeah, I know, it sounds professional. It was a history/geography teacher friend of mine who tipped me off to the full designation.

In short, coming back (and by coming back so much, I’m sure I’ll finally get there) to these nails that deserve to be driven in: after spending three days in these hills—yes, yes, these magnificent mountains, which are truly beautiful—I descended on the other side to a small village. I had the same hope as always: to find a grocery store, preferably one that accepts credit cards, to recharge my batteries, and to wash myself. The pilgrim classics.

Upon entering the village, a welcoming concrete wall seemed to have been built just for my backpack and me to rest against. It’s 36 degrees Celsius in the shade... and I’m not in the shade. So, I’m soaked in sweat and happy to breathe while sitting, almost comfortably, with my bag supporting my back.

A car stops. The gentleman, speaking through the open window, says something to me. After a few gestures on my part to explain “French/Pilgrim/Not understanding,” he points to a spot further up one of the streets at the intersection where we are. In English that’s as good as my Hungarian, he says: “Vateur. Oundrund méteur.” Which translates as: “If you want, there’s a source of drinking water a hundred meters higher up this street.”

Yes, I know, it’s just a few words, but I include the non-verbal gestures and the tone in my translation—it adds something.

This is great news, I think to myself as the man drives away, because all my water bottles are empty, and I’m thirsty. It’s funny how the moment you realize you’re out of water, thirst immediately kicks in, as if by magic. The mind is strange, isn’t it? In short, thirst is real.

The information is tempting, even though it’s uphill and not on my route. Spring water is often very fresh—if not outright cold—and frequently very tasty, which fits my situation perfectly. It’s the kind of water you drink even when you’re not thirsty, until your stomach feels tight because you’ve overdone it. Yes, I assure you... I speak from experience. Anyway, subjectively, I estimate I’ll drink at least three liters.

So, I get up and walk.

The place does not disappoint. It’s a small spring, like many I’ve come across: two rectangular, interconnected basins, with a pipe coming out of the rock, pouring clear liquid. And the water is really good... and really fresh.

And I drink. Not three liters, but at least one, then half of another shortly after. I splash water on my head and wet my hat (which prolongs the cooling effect while it dries—about five minutes).

As I lounge, wash my hands, re-wet my face, re-re-wet my head, and soak my arms, a gentleman comes to do much the same as me.

We greet each other, and the conversation begins. After a few moments, comes the comment to which I am now accustomed—common to my situation—delivered with a handshake: Respect! Yes, many people use this word after I play them the brief message recorded in my translator, which succinctly summarizes my situation over the past three years, listing, among other things, the countries I’ve crossed. Respect.

So, Tibor and I talk, and I go into a little more detail... The landscapes, the silence, the encounters, the freedom, the joy of living, the happiness, the kindness received, the welcomes... All of it, you see.

Then, two women pass by on foot. They greet Tibor and exchange a few words, without stopping completely. That is, until he seems to speak of me, because I recognize the words “French” and “kilometers”. I translate this in my mind, with some detailed extrapolation, as: “This is a French pilgrim who has walked 7,500 km to get here. Do you know where he could sleep tonight?”

The women stop for a moment, then leave. Tibor tells me via the translator that I should follow them because they might ask their boss about getting me a room for the night. They work in a sort of hotel or motel, he explains—they are in the kitchen.

I finish filling my water bottles, say goodbye to Tibor, take my backpack, and leave at a rapid pace (fortunately, it’s downhill) to catch up with the women and follow them to the establishment. It’s just opposite where I had been sitting previously... Small world. Though, admittedly, the village is small too—but that’s not the point.

They invite me inside, and while they try to reach the boss, they offer me a meal: a good vegetable soup, some sautéed potatoes, and pan-fried cheese. In the end, they can’t get me a room at the hotel because everything is booked—apparently, there’s a wedding.

But they tell me a friend of theirs is inviting me to her house. I finish eating, and she comes to pick me up to take me there.

She’s a petite lady, around 75 years old (72, in fact). Very kind, she walks with me up the street where the spring is located but further along, to her house. She shows me a sofa bed in the living room, which she immediately begins to prepare while I still have my backpack on my back.

Once I un-shoulder my backpack and take off my shoes, she shows me the bathroom. A shower! My God! Have I already said how good a shower feels? Because, my God, a shower feels amazing...

So, here I am, lying down now. It’s 7:45 p.m., and I’ll sleep soon. My batteries are recharging. Earlier, I went shopping at the store, knowing it would be closed tomorrow, Sunday. Margret—that’s her name—cooked the hard-boiled eggs I bought for the road, just to add some variety. We shared a little aperitif under the pergola in her garden, a ‘little pear.’

She invited me to dinner, but as I had a late and rather heavy lunch (cheese in the pan...), I preferred to lie down. And write to you, to tell you... A little tap on the nail’s head, because I think it deserves it.

And since you have to strike the nail while it’s hot (I’m sure this is a saying—maybe not as common as the one about iron, but still), I might as well continue.

This morning, on my way down to the church, I filled all my bottles and canteen at the spring I visited the day before. I filled up completely because my plan was to set up my tent in the forest 4 km past the village and rest there for two days and three nights. You know the drill.

But one of the first lessons of the road is learning to easily let go of your plans to be guided by others—plans that often turn out to be much better... Certainly in my case...

Margret tells me to wait for her after mass so we can say goodbye. And, as planned, at the end of the mass, just outside the church, I see her as well as the gentleman from the day before, Tibor.

He is a teacher here and would like to introduce me to a friend who speaks French: Gabriel. Contrary to his habit, Tibor sat especially next to Gabriel during the service this morning to inform him of my presence and the opportunity to practice a language that is otherwise hard to use in the area.

Gabriel does indeed speak very good French, occasionally using English for words that escape him due to lack of practice. Thanks to him, my "goodbyes" to Margret and Mr. Teacher are smooth and direct.

When it’s time for me to get back on my way, Gabriel asks if I’m in a hurry or if I have time for a coffee at his place, just five minutes away on foot. After "checking my schedule", I assure him that I have all the time necessary to meet and answer any questions. This is one of the rules I’ve set for myself: to remain available to share what I receive. It also helps me avoid drying out, which is always a risk for a hermit.

So, I spend the morning chatting in French with him and his wife. Then, because he has errands to run, he invites me to accompany him. Together, we feed the farm animals a few kilometers from the village, visit a miraculous spring whose water has been renowned since the Middle Ages, and stop at a recently rebuilt chapel next to the ruins of the one built by King "Machin" after the spring water below cured his army of the infection that was decimating his ranks. We also visit a small wildlife reserve hidden deep in the countryside, where a pond fed by another spring—delicious and fresh water again—houses many rainbow trout that Gabriel regularly feeds.

Along the way, he shows me numerous signs of plantigrade activity. Apparently, there are a few bears in the immediate vicinity, five if I understood correctly. (I know this is a long parenthesis, but I’ll close it now.)

During all this, Gabriel invites me to stay and rest at a small chalet on the farm where we fed the sheep, pigs, chickens, and dogs. In addition to having the renowned spring nearby, the place is special, he says. Certainly bucolic, but more than that. It seems conducive to healing.

And I have to admit, without fully understanding why, it’s one of those places where you feel good—immediately and simply. Gabriel suggests I stay more than two days. In fact, he says, I could stay as long as I want to enjoy the place.

He also offers that, if I want, I could see the family doctor, a general practitioner with extensive experience, who could prescribe more thorough examinations than those done at the hospital last time. I could wait for the results and decide on the next steps accordingly.

He will regularly drive by to feed the animals, so if I need anything, I could benefit from a trip by car to the village to resupply. But, as he showed me while taking me around the place, here in the courtyard set up for hosting family or friends (equipped with a wood-burning oven, a wood-burning grill, and a wood-burning stove, all built from bricks—perfect for enjoying good times at the table with good company), there is a fully stocked fridge where I can help myself.

As far as vegetables are concerned, the organic vegetable garden is just behind the chalet. Everything I see here is available to me, if I wish. And to reach the spring for bathing, he showed me his quad parked in the barn, explaining how to start and use it.

He wished me a good evening and a good night when he left. Through the window of the SUV he was driving, he told me that the herdsman, whose herd’s bells are ringing all around the area, would come this evening to bring me fresh milk. He suggested I invite him to have a Palinka (a “little plum!”) or whatever he prefers and to insist if he hesitates out of politeness or fear of disturbing me. Since it’s outside of his schedule, it’s acceptable—and it would help him feel welcome to have a little drink.

And that’s precisely the feeling Gabriel creates: feeling good. I am welcomed, well-received. His concern is exactly that: for me to feel good. And it happens easily.

He offers me his trust without hesitation and with abundance, sharing the treasures of his life, the enjoyment of this place and its resources, his warm hospitality... and I just met him for the first time this morning.

I’m finishing this message, comfortably seated in the armchair of a small chalet in a stunning corner of nature in the middle of the Carpathian Mountains. One of those dangerously comfortable armchairs that’s hard to leave without a very, very good reason.

On the table, I have peppers and capsicums, apples and plums from his garden, tomatoes from his dad’s garden, cucumbers from the same source, bread from his bakery, and croissants. Two fresh honeycombs from his beekeeper friend. Cheese in the fridge, along with some pastries, French butter, olives in brine, Greek-style dried olives, hard-boiled quail eggs in addition to those I already had, porcini mushrooms, and homemade canned chanterelles.

And there’s the fresh sheep’s cheese that Margret gave me before I left her house—for the road, she said, for the road.

I just uncorked a bottle of red wine that Gabriel took out for me, likely because he suspected I wouldn’t have helped myself. And because he knows I enjoy a small glass of red wine with cheese. I let the bottle breathe after removing the cork, waiting for the herdsman, who should arrive soon.

Isn’t this a nail that deserved to be driven in a little further?

Wow...

I wish you the best.

See you soon...

97% of my messages were opened, and 68% received a response, either with a small emoji or a message. Only 42% included a code in the reply. This doesn’t mean the remaining 58% weren’t read, as some people told me they didn’t understand the secret code thing. Still, it gives an idea.

On the other hand, almost all of the 42% of confirmed readers insisted I continue writing—even long messages—emphasizing how much they were appreciated, which I appreciate just as much. I’m writing this because I want to share a “top ten” list of less secret codes.

Re-re P.S., but now from the morning: I just remembered an important detail. There’s a small Italian coffee maker next to the gas stove. Gabriel took out the can of coffee that goes with it, and it’s full. So, after a glass of real milk—milk that smells and tastes of cows and the flowers they eat—I’ll have an espresso to go with my croissant.

If I add that this is one of those places where you sleep exceptionally well... you’ll probably understand. You’ve likely come across such places yourself. As someone who changes locations regularly, I assure you there are some places that are clearly better than others for sleep, and it’s not due to external conditions, decor, or anything obvious. It’s a kind of intrinsic atmosphere.

This place is one of them. You sleep deeply here. Even disturbances during the night, like a majestic storm or the dogs, didn’t compromise the restorative effect of a good night’s sleep. Just that—sleeping well—is so good. And a hot coffee with a croissant afterward... how to describe it? It’s like drinking a good coffee in the morning after having slept well.

One last P.S. for the road:

There are four dogs here, each with their own personality.

There’s a huge grandmother, about the size of half a bear, but she’s very gentle—except with the youngest. He’s utterly sassy and annoying, like a two-year-old dog. He runs around, licks, nibbles, plays, or wants to play, and steals the dishcloth every time I leave it too close to the boundary he’s not supposed to cross under the courtyard. She regularly growls and snaps her jaws at him, trying to calm him or keep his boundless energy at bay, so she can resume the nap she started a year ago... in vain.

The insolent little one rolls onto his back, pretends to be submissive, and lets out fake barks that no one believes. He wags his tail in all directions while squirming on the ground, sending completely contradictory signals that no one pays attention to anymore.

Then there’s the very small one with curly hair, who seeks cuddles even more than the scatterbrain. She runs sideways, muzzle to the ground, twisting her back as if she’s already stretching toward a hand even before reaching the potential caresser. Her light-colored eyes give her a slightly “quirky” look, but it suits her well.

Lastly, there’s the big dad, who’s massive too, but he’s a soft caramel. He stays discreet in his corner, but when approached, he’s clearly happy.

There’s no blood relation among them, but they share a very good bond.

A band of good companions—joyful and friendly.
