Arrival in Lithuania

10/6/2021

0 Comments

 
     ​So, how to say this... I’m a bit shy to share my arrival in Lithuania. I’m afraid you might think I’m exaggerating or embellishing things, or maybe that I ate the wrong mushroom, or even that I’m having some kind of psychological breakdown—I don’t know. But honestly, even I have difficulty believing I’m not dreaming all of this.

      It’s been a week now since I arrived in Lithuania, and I have to say, the place I came through is stunning. The northern part of Poland was already breathtaking, with its national parks, wetlands, and all. But once I crossed the border, I’ve been in a kind of ecstasy.

     The landscape is overwhelmingly beautiful: vast forests, crystal-clear lakes, and charming little rivers. Seriously, wait until you see the pictures—you’ll understand!

     As the weather is clearly turning to summer, the walking can get sweaty. The sun shines brightly from 3:30 a.m. to 10:30 p.m., and it’s around 28°C. So yes, it feels hot while walking. But can you imagine? So far, I’ve been able to wash, swim, or splash myself every single day in places so idyllic you’d think they were taken from a summer vacation brochure or a postcard—no, better than postcards! The water is crystal clear and wonderfully refreshing, cool enough to take all the heat out of your body but not so cold as to leave you shivering.

     The ground here is mainly sandy, whether on the roads or forest trails. Taking a swim, washing, or rinsing clothes (without soap, of course, to protect the environment—but with fine, soft sand that scrubs gently) has been such a pure pleasure that it fills me with joy. At least, it does for me. These stunning spots are everywhere, as lakes range from several kilometers wide to just a few hundred meters, and I come across one every 5 km at most.

     You see?

     Now, to complete the picture, imagine the air filled with the scent of pine resin, mixed with the intoxicating fragrances of the season. Every day, I pass dozens of blooming lilacs, carpets of lily of the valley with a scent so strong it’s almost overwhelming, and countless flowering trees, bushes, and meadows. Every open field between forests is covered in flowers!

     You might think I’m being overly enthusiastic. But I grew up in the countryside; I know what flowered meadows are. And yet, I’m amazed by what I’m seeing here. Just wait until you see the pictures—really, the whole picture! No wonder the place where I entered Lithuania is a national park and a popular tourist destination for Russians, Belarusians, and Ukrainians.

     And the wildlife! The mosquitoes are so in love with French cuisine—utterly enthusiastic about French gastronomy! 

    And the birds! My God, the birds! There are still swallows here, you know. And robins. And cuckoos. And... well, I’ll stop now. You get the idea.

     Now, about the people!
    You might think that no country can have it all—the beauty, the landscapes—and also have kind, welcoming people. That would be too much, wouldn’t it? Unfair! Unrealistic! But you’d be completely wrong. Totally wrong.

     At first, even I didn’t believe it. After a few encounters, I thought, “This is too much—it can’t be real.” I half expected a hidden camera to pop out or something. But let me give you some examples...

     The first village I crossed was a small one, just a few kilometers past the border. I was walking along the road/path out of the forest, taking in the charm of a new country, with a touristy air about me, my nose in the air, admiring the scenery. Most of the houses are made of wood. Many don’t even have fences, and if they do, they’re often beautifully crafted from wood.

  The roads are mostly dirt, which means sand—brown sand, yellow sand, black sand... Comfortable for walking, let’s say, but dusty. Only a few are paved, and honestly, that’s nice because cars drive slower (though dustier, too—I just step aside and position myself with the wind to avoid being coated in it).

     At a crossroads, a couple about my parents' age called out to me with enthusiastic gestures and started walking through their garden toward me. At that point, I honestly thought I was about to be told I was on a private path or trespassing. But when they reached me, I realized—almost strangely—that they just wanted to say hello, ask who I was, and what I was doing.

     I had barely finished saying "Emmanuel" when the woman immediately invited me to come to the veranda for a coffee! Seriously? The whole thing felt surreal—pleasant, but a bit beyond reality in how fast and simply it all happened, completely out of the blue.

   I ended up staying with them for maybe two hours, first over coffee, which turned into breakfast, and eventually lunch. But wait—the best part? For the first hour, we couldn’t even communicate properly! They didn’t speak any English or French, and my phone was painfully slow downloading the Lithuanian language set for Google Translate. So you can imagine how tenuous our conversations were. But still, they showed me warm hospitality and genuine kindness. The woman even called her son to help translate a bit. And once the language pack was downloaded, everything became much easier, of course.

     When it was time to leave and they were going back to their day, I took the opportunity to sit at their table and repair my shoe. The zipper on the right one had broken an hour earlier, and it took me nearly an hour to sew it securely while my phone charged nearby.

     See? A cool arrival and a perfect first contact with the place. I left feeling happy, well-fed, caffeinated—and with a fully charged phone!

     Before continuing, please check out the pictures of their place. I mean, come on—what a gorgeous spot! A wooden house flanked by two buildings: one a cute barn and the other a series of stables, chicken coops, and workshops you’d expect to find on a farm. In front of it all was a tiny, picturesque house, and behind, down their backyard, lay a lake. The view was just breathtaking. God... It was a beautiful arrival.

     And then I went on my way again, with freshly repaired shoes and a big smile on my face. I walked around the lake for maybe two hours, though I was a little too far to actually see it—remember, the forest?

     Suddenly, I heard someone running behind me and calling out. It was a man who had seen me on the road from his house and had run out after me just to chat! We tried to communicate as best as we could, at least enough to establish the basics: that I was a Frenchman who had arrived here on foot, and for him to tell me that it was great! We shook hands, and then he left. I stayed there for a moment, wondering what had just happened.

     That day, I kept walking until I arrived at a place that felt like one of those spots where you just drop your backpack, sit down, and stay—quiet and happy. Maybe not for life, but definitely for the rest of the day and for the night!

     See the picture! A lovely flat clearing with lush grass, a shore of very fine, smooth sand, a small pontoon, and the lake. It was a free outdoor shower, free laundry, and pure relaxation—all in one spot.

     Some people were there too, doing the same as I: enjoying the place. They had come by bike, on foot, or by car from nearby villages or even farther away. A quick splash in the water, a short swim, a bit of friendly chatting—and then, before I knew it, evening came, and they were all gone.

     When I arrived, I asked if it was a public spot, free to access, free of charge, and whether it was okay to camp and swim. Of course, they said! "Feel free," they said. "You are welcome." It felt so nice to feel welcome. Really, it felt so nice.

     I set up my tent, swam for maybe an hour, washed all my clothes for another hour, hung my clean laundry on a string someone else had stretched between two trees, and then went into my tent and fell asleep like a baby in his loving mother’s arms.

     What is wonderful about those places where you sleep is that they are still there in the morning when you wake up!

     And the early mornings in those places are spectacular. The loud concert of birds, the soft and warm sunlight filtering through the trees and reflecting on the water, the gentle lapping of the water—it all rocks you and invites you to linger a bit longer in your half-awake dream.

     I got up a little later than usual, feeling fully rested and completely relaxed, thinking that place and the last few days had been just perfect. I wished for more, craved more. And I wasn’t disappointed. In fact, it was so perfect that I even began to doubt my experience. I started to think it was just too perfect, that the beautiful encounter I’m about to describe was too good to be real. I mean it—so good, so nice, so human, so rare.

    I walked about 3 km to reach the next village and went straight to its center, looking for a church and a shop. I arrived at the church just before ten o’clock and went inside, only to discover it was packed and crowded. Mass was about to start in 20 minutes, so I decided to stay.

     It was my first Lithuanian celebration, and I have to say, the priest was really special to me. He smiled a lot, joked with people, and moved from one person to another, seeming genuinely happy to say hello and exchange a few words with each and every one. His way of being was something I found truly rare.

     After the mass—though you can imagine how much of it I was actually able to follow—I waited a bit for the crowd to thin out because I needed some space to put my backpack back on. As I was getting ready, a beautiful young lady came straight to me with a preoccupied smile, as though she were deep in thought, making calculations.

     She asked if I was a pilgrim and if I knew where I would sleep that night, but before I could say more than "yes," she gently pushed me aside to let someone pass and told me to join her outside. Kornelija—that’s her name—was with her older sister and her mother. She was elegantly dressed in a beautiful summer dress, which she had chosen especially for church that day to please her mother.

     I immediately thought of an old French song that goes: "Salade de fruits, jolie, jolie, tu plais a mere, tu plais a mon pere..." But that reference might not mean much to you if you’re not French...

    She struck me as a dynamic, beautiful, slender, tall young woman of 28, who could easily be mistaken for 20 when she smiled or for 35 when she thought seriously. She had particularly beautiful, delicate, and slender hands. Her blue eyes sparkled with intelligence, playfulness, and a gentle, soft rebellion. Even though I sensed she could be stern or severe, her warm openness and enthusiasm made it nearly impossible to say anything but "yes" to whatever she asked.

     We planned to meet 12 kilometers away, which she estimated would take me about four hours, at her grandmother’s old farm where I could stay, rest, and meet her family, including her brothers. And with that, she said, "See you later," and she was gone...

     I went shopping and started my way, which, as she said, would be on the same path leading to her place. I thought of that meeting as another beautiful one.

     About half an hour later, as I was leaving the village, a car stopped beside me. She popped out, hugged me, and asked if I wanted them to take my luggage in their car. I politely declined, explaining that I preferred to carry it myself—it’s part of my journey, my karma. She introduced me to her mother, who was driving, got back in the car, and left, waving enthusiastically as they drove off.

     I stayed two nights in a beautiful place. The entire family is just incredibly lovely. They are all beautiful—not just physically, but their souls seem to shine a little through them. They have a brilliance about them. And I’m not just talking about Kornelija studying pure mathematics, or her youngest brother studying political science, or the fact that they are all fully fluent in English, Norwegian, and Russian. No, I’m talking about their shiny, smiling eyes that radiate kindness and intelligence.

     You know me well enough to understand that I have very little experience with close family dynamics, so I might be easily surprised and impressed—and yes, I absolutely was! But it felt completely legitimate:

     They talk to each other! It’s like they genuinely care about what the other says! They hold hands and close their eyes to pray before meals. They smile at each other and hug to say hello or goodbye. They ask their mother for blessings, which she gives by tracing a small cross on their foreheads with her thumb. They care for each other—it’s obvious.

     This isn’t my world. I come from somewhere else entirely. But it’s a wonderful world, and they invited me into it so completely, so trustingly, so gently. It felt surreal. It couldn’t be real. And yet it was—for two magical days.

     I met everyone I could. We ate flowers and raw food, bathed in the river, and talked for hours. Then we cooked over a wood fire before enjoying a sauna until midnight. A traditional sauna—jumping into the river behind the garden to cool off when it got too hot and sweaty. We covered our bodies in a half-clay, half-vase paste. Only after I’d happily plastered my entire face with it did they tell me how hard it would be to wash off!

     And you know what? When it was time for me to leave—remember, I have a rule of staying no more than three days in one place—they half-seriously, half-jokingly invited me to bend my rule and move to the next village to their father’s old house and stay there instead.

    When I was finally ready to go, Ignas, the youngest brother, decided to come with me. He packed his camping gear, as well as Kornelija’s, who had left in the morning to meet another brother in the next town. Ignas assured me we absolutely had to meet him before I left. That brother was walking toward us, planning to join us at our campsite for the night.

     We met him in the late afternoon and set up camp together. Later that night, Kornelija joined us on her bike after finishing whatever had kept her in town for so long.

     We spent the evening and part of the night sitting around a fire, sharing stories and laughter after a lovely dinner.

     In the morning, we bathed together in a nearby lake, went to see a farmer to get some "real cheese" for the French guy (me), and said our goodbyes.

     Ignas stayed with me the whole day again so we could walk together to the next town and talk along the way.

     Now, if I tell you that the town we’re in is one of the most beautiful and peaceful towns I’ve ever seen, would you believe me? It’s surrounded by forest, with forests even in the town center, along with lakes and a river... It’s beautiful and green. You can swim in the lake if you want—it's allowed, and the municipality keeps it clean for that purpose.

     We spent the night in his grandmother’s flat since she wasn’t there. And we stayed the next day as well, except for the morning when I got up early to run some errands: getting money, having coffee, taking a little tour of this beautiful town, and shopping for new shorts or trousers because my constant walking had worn them out really fast.

    Later, we met again in town, had more coffee, visited a special second-hand shop, and then went to the library to make a kind of paper to show people along my way, introducing myself in Lithuanian. Afterward, we met his cousin and went to a restaurant together, then took a long walk through the town park, talking the entire way. Finally, we returned to the flat, where I am now in bed, ready to sleep and writing this message.

     Ignas went out for the evening. I wanted to go to bed early to start my journey early tomorrow. He had some plans to figure out for his next move and the coming weeks. So we said our goodbyes in case I leave tomorrow morning without seeing him again.

     Before I leave, I need to switch off the flat’s utilities: turn off the main water tap, take out the trash, close the windows, lock the door, and put the key in the mailbox. Then I’ll go.

     I’m alone in the flat of people I only met this past Sunday. And you know what? I already miss them...

     Am I just that lucky? Or blessed? Or maybe both? But seriously, think about it: how many people do you know who would give their home for the night to a total stranger who doesn’t even speak their language?

     I feel like I’m living in a dream. A beautiful dream. The only way it could get better would be if the mosquitoes became vegetarian!

     And honestly, I’m starting to believe it could happen. Why not?
