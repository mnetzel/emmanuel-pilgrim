The Bird and the Journey to Serbia

1/9/2024

0 Comments

 
    ​ Today, I crossed a small village just before Timisoara. After stocking up at the small grocery store at the crossroads of the main roads, I chatted a bit with the owner, who spoke French, having spent around fifteen years in Lausanne, Switzerland. He suggested I recharge my batteries in the back room, set up as a bar. I plugged them in. All were completely empty, so I had an hour to spare. As I sat down to eat something, three regulars came in. One of them, seeing me cut the freshly bought bread with cheese beside it, came straight to me and handed me a jar of chili pickles, assuring me it would be much better with them. Nice. And yes, it was much better with them.

     Leaving the place, I headed toward the church, hoping to get inside and recharge my internal batteries somehow. I passed a man selling watermelons, sitting next to his small trailer full of fruit. I greeted him, crossed the pedestrian crossing, and climbed the stairs to the church’s main entrance. The door was locked, as so often. I went back down the steps to road level, placed my bag on a low wall, and sat down to check the map.

     The gentleman selling watermelons joined me with one of them in his hands, which he offered, telling me it was ripe and good. I gladly accepted the gift. In the heat, a watermelon is incredibly refreshing, better than drinking water—a matter of electrolytes and avoiding cramps at the end of the day. Of course, it’s heavy to carry, but we can’t have everything.

     He asked if I wanted to go into the church. I told him it was locked. Before I could say more, he was already on the phone. After a few words, he told me to wait two minutes—the local priest was coming to open it for me. He left me there, saying I should join him afterward.

     It was a pretty little village church with a lovely atmosphere. I stayed there for just over an hour, eyes closed, breathing calmly. These moments of depth in places like this are truly a haven. In summer, it’s cool; in winter, not too cold. The priest’s wife invited me for a meal, but I was already full. It was kind of her, though.

   Then I rejoined the gentleman selling watermelons on the side of the road to answer a few questions he had. While eating a fruit he had just opened to share with me, I explained a little about my journey and my path. It was a pleasant moment. He asked me about the weight of my bag and offered to weigh it on the large scale he used for his fruits.

    I had just stocked up on food (about a kilo and a half of very red tomatoes, two loaves of bread—well, one and a half now—a bottle of olive oil since butter doesn’t fare well in this heat) and water. I carry a lot of water when preparing to camp for a day off. It’s so hot that I drink 5 to 6 liters a day to avoid dehydration and tendonitis. Plus, there was the free watermelon—a generous but "poisoned" gift, weighing at least 6 kilos. I expected my bag to be excessively heavy. It weighed 47 kilos! That’s definitely too much. I’ll have to make some decisions to reduce this. I plan to do that soon, once I reach the town where I can send items back to France that I no longer need.

    Before I left to resume my journey, he handed me a small bundle of notes he had prepared by quietly emptying his cash counter. His gesture and genuine desire to contribute moved me deeply. I initially refused several times but finally accepted. We greeted each other warmly, and I continued on my way.

    I set up camp 3 kilometers after the village, in the bushes under a walnut tree, to have some shade for the next day when I’d enjoy my watermelon. It had been another beautiful day.

     Upon arriving in Timisoara, I headed straight to the guesthouse I had spotted on my map and called the day before. On the way, I stopped at a supermarket to buy some food. As I neared the guesthouse, about a kilometer away, the owner approached me on his bike. His wife had spotted me at the store and guessed I was the Frenchman, Emmanuel, they were expecting. We went to the guesthouse together.

     The guesthouse was an old bourgeois house set in a small town garden with an enormous, fruit-laden fig tree and a few plum trees. I was invited to help myself to as much fruit as I wanted—and I did, every morning. The thick walls of the old house retained the coolness of the night during the day and the warmth of the day during the night, thanks to its thermal inertia. Each floor had a few simple but well-appointed rooms and dormitories.

    After settling in, I rushed to the sports store on the other side of town to buy or order the items I frequently need to replace. The bus station was right at the crossroads, and I only had to wait a few minutes for the right bus. While waiting, I watched two energetic little girls dancing to pass the time, their mother sitting calmly next to me on the shaded bench of the bus shelter.

    When I entered the bus through the front door to purchase a ticket, my plans were foiled. There was no access to the driver from whom I expected to buy a ticket—not even a hygiaphone. The driver gestured at me through the window, pointing backward with his thumb. Following the direction indicated, I saw boxes attached to the poles. Payment was only possible via contactless credit card or by scanning a QR code and using a payment app on a phone—none of which suited me.

     It was the mother of the two little girls who helped. Heading to the same place as me, she used her phone to pay for my ticket and refused to let me reimburse her. She invited me to sit next to her and her children so we could chat. She assured me she’d let me know when it was time to get off. “We’re going to almost the same place,” she said and promised to point me in the right direction when our paths diverged. It was a lovely encounter, full of kindness and warmth.

    The same situation happened the next day. Unable to pay for my ticket again, and with it being Sunday, I couldn’t find an open kiosk to buy one in person. Boarding another bus to reach the store, I offered the equivalent change for the fare to anyone who could pay using their phone or contactless card. A kind woman in her seventies used her card and also refused the banknote I offered her in return. We spent the entire ride chatting in a mix of French and Italian.

     All of this is to say that the atmosphere in the city is remarkably relaxed and friendly. My stay was genuinely pleasant. My host was one of those people who run a guesthouse for the love of it rather than as a business. He half-jokingly told me that if you want to make money, this isn’t the way to do it. I believe him, as the rate for a night at his establishment was incredibly low, about half of what his competitors charge.

     It was clear he found joy in meeting his guests. He went out of his way to make sure everyone had a comfortable stay, doing everything possible (and then some) to help. His sincerity and dedication created a truly welcoming environment. It was a real pleasure to be his guest.

     I leave the city with a big backpack. The trip to a major supermarket didn’t help; the two lunch boxes are now filled with items I can’t find elsewhere, especially tofu—protein for the vegetarian. I even found a real ladled Brie.

     Before leaving, I stop at the post office to send a package to France. Once again, I’ve skimmed through the items I carry to lighten the load and allow more room for water. I managed to lose 6 kg—huge! That’s an extra 6 liters of capacity, which is necessary. I can now carry 12 liters, enough for two days of water in summer.

    I decide to follow the river for a few kilometers before the border, then head toward the customs post I must cross. The route is longer but much more pleasant. The map shows good vegetation offering shade along both sides of the watercourse. On one side, there’s a cycle path; on the other, a dirt road atop the dike that protects nearby villages. I can choose whichever side seems more advantageous.

    The riverbanks are lined with willows, aspens, acacias (robinia), walnut trees, and a few scattered species. There’s plenty of shade for walking and bivouacking, making it a pleasant path.

     After a few kilometers, I arrive at a lock where a father and son are fishing near their car. As I approach, the father speaks to me, and I take the opportunity to set my bag down, catch my breath, and answer their questions about my large pack. I introduce myself, explain my journey, and talk about Expiation (my backpack). I mention that since it’s getting late, I plan to find a spot to bivouac among the trees and bushes nearby.

     We talk for about half an hour. As I prepare to leave, the son hands me a note from the car, at his father’s request. Although I politely refuse, they both insist, explaining that the grocery stores in the next villages don’t accept credit cards.

   The father also tells me that the path I’m following ends at a gate protecting the lock installations. However, he knows the attendant living in the house nearby and offers to accompany me, sparing me from navigating through bushes and crops.

     We walk together, leaving my bag at the gate, as I’ve already spotted a suitable grove 20 meters back where I’ll pitch my tent.

    The attendant is washing his car when we arrive. He’s pleasant and friendly, and assures me there’s no issue with staying in the spot I’ve chosen for the night—and possibly the next. He even offers an alternative in his garden, but after inspecting it, I realize there won’t be enough shade there for the day. Without shade, the tent becomes a sauna under the sun.

     As we return to the house, the attendant shows us his vegetable garden, neatly filled with rows of vibrant fruits and vegetables. I find it remarkably aesthetic. He heads down into the garden and returns with three large, beautiful tomatoes, weighing over a kilo and a half in total. They’re enormous, sun-split, and incredibly juicy, with the rich taste and aroma of proper tomatoes.

     Before we leave, he steps inside, speaking aloud the whole time, and comes back with a large bottle of very fresh sparkling water—cold even—a can of red beans, and a packet of biscuits. I politely refuse the can of tuna and pâté, explaining my vegetarianism...

     Leaving this place where I stayed for two nights, I passed through a small village nearby to refill my water bottles. Spotting a restaurant, the idea of a cooked meal tempted me, so I stopped at the café next door to wait for the restaurant to open in an hour. The café owner, also the bartender, offered me a drink and a smile. He suggested putting my water bottles in his refrigerator while I waited, a kind gesture but unnecessary since the bottles would heat up quickly once I left. I chatted with him and the regular patrons playing cards at a nearby table. Then I picked up my bag, said my goodbyes, and set off, knowing I’d pass by again as the river and the nearby bridge were the only crossings in the area.

    I chose to walk along the bank without the asphalt cycle path, which is hard underfoot and radiates the sun’s heat. Satellite views showed that side had more vegetation, offering shade for my route today and tomorrow.

    At the restaurant, I had a meal while chatting with the young owner and her mother, who cooked the food. She invited me to recharge my devices at the outlets behind the checkout and suggested I shop at the nearby grocery store while leaving my things safely there. She seemed taken aback by my pilgrimage story and thanked me for coming to her restaurant and sharing it.

    As I left and passed the café again, an outdoor table called me over. The story of the French pilgrim had spread, and I was easily recognizable. I happily stopped to answer questions while enjoying a drink they offered. The café exuded a warm, village atmosphere. I don’t mind spending time like that. The only thing I can offer in exchange for the kindness I receive is sharing my experiences, and I do so gladly. By the time I left, the sun had lowered, making the heat less oppressive.

    I stopped a kilometer past the village, with about 20 kilometers ahead before reaching the next village where I could get water. The map showed a leisure camp halfway along the route. I figured I could ask for water there. At 5–6 liters a day, my dependence on water is clear.

    The camp turned out to be a leisure center with an entry fee, offering a massive swimming pool, children’s play areas, a terrace, deck chairs, a restaurant, and two bars—one inside the pool. It was clearly a popular spot for families from nearby areas.

     I arrived through an unguarded back entrance, the overgrown grass suggesting it wasn’t a usual path. Heading toward the building, I sought a manager to explain my entry and ask for tap water. Once inside, I thought I might pay the fee, eat, and use the pool. I don’t particularly enjoy crowded, noisy pools filled with chlorine, but a pool likely meant a nearby shower, which interested me.

     One of the owners greeted me and handed me a bottle of chilled mineral water from the fridge. He said I couldn’t use the pool shower but would instead have access to a private one in a room he’d open for me. He explained that the room wasn’t yet cleaned but would be soon. He called over a young employee, around 25 and fluent in English, to tell me I was welcome to a shower, food, and even a night in the room if needed. Additionally, his wife could do my laundry if I wanted. I was stunned but accepted the invitation, as everything felt genuine.

     After a shower and meal, I lay in the room near an outlet, charging my phone and writing these notes. A couple of hours later, the young man knocked on the door. When I opened it, he handed me a bag of food that his brother had gone to buy for me in the village, along with some money. I began to refuse, but he insisted, saying it was a local tradition.

    “My father passed away some time ago,” he explained, “I’m doing this in his name.” I asked for his father’s name—it was Sergio.

     Sergio made good boys, I think...

    He then asked if I’d agree to take a photo with one of the employees who wanted a souvenir of the Frenchman who came on foot from France. Then, of course, he wanted one as well, followed by the bosses. We took the first few pictures straight away, while the group photos with the bosses and all the staff were planned for the next morning at my departure, with everyone gathered, my equipment, and the sun just right.

    Another beautiful day ended with a good night’s rest. In the morning, I woke up early—four years of living by the solar rhythm does that. I wandered over to the swimming pool, which was calm and serene. One of the brothers who owns the camp was already there, tending to the water. A swimming pool in full sun with hundreds of daily bathers requires a lot of maintenance, he explained, before showing me the water pumping station in the basement and the filtration and cleaning systems. A whole building was dedicated to it. With my technical background, I found the visit fascinating. Even more so when I discovered that they didn’t use any chemicals for water treatment—just salt and filtration. Impressive. Afterward, I went for a swim, and when he returned with coffee, I joined him for a break. Morning bliss: a dip in cool water followed by a coffee break.

    When I finally left the camp, it was already late. I knew because it was 32°C, and I was struggling. Stocked up on drinking water and food, my bag was heavy again, as always. After walking just 800 meters, I was drenched in sweat and my heart was racing. I stopped for half an hour to cool down, dry off, and let my body recover. I drank water, rested, and set off again. Another kilometer passed, and I had to stop once more—it must have been 34°C by then. They’d warned me at the camp that the temperature would rise to 38°C today. I knew I’d have to stop by midday, likely around 11 a.m., and wait for the sun to dip and the temperature to drop before continuing.

    I decided to push forward for another stretch. After just 400 meters, I spotted a place that seemed perfect for waiting out the hottest hours.

    I was walking along the top of a riverside embankment. A few meters below, there was a flat area, maybe a few meters wide, followed by another slope down to the riverbank. There, a small, cleared beach appeared, likely the work of local fishermen. Fishing is a common pastime in Romania. The area was lush with vegetation, shaded by enormous willow trees. I was certain the sun wouldn’t reach the spot until it was setting, long after I would have left.

     I hadn’t planned to stop yet—I still had enough energy for another stretch or two before resting. But something about the place called to me. Its idyllic setting, with easy access to the water and none of the mud or dense plants that often make getting in and out difficult, was irresistible. Though I knew I might find a similar spot later, this one felt right. So, I went down.

    The place was magnificent. I set my bag against a tree stump by the small beach and leaned back, stretching my legs toward the water. The wildlife I’d disturbed upon arrival—moorhens diving, herons and ducks taking flight, frogs disappearing into the depths—had all scattered. I let the sweat finish dripping off as I relaxed.

    The river was calm, about fifteen meters wide, flowing slowly but powerfully due to its depth—three to four meters at the center. The water was turbid but not dirty or unappealing. Perhaps my perception was influenced by the heat and the sweat stinging my eyes, making the idea of a swim all the more tempting.

   I stayed put, letting the water flow and time pass. Ten minutes went by without a single movement. The local wildlife began to resume its activity, likely forgetting I was there.

   Then, suddenly, off to the side among the water plants about two meters from the bank, something started thrashing wildly. Powerful, noisy strikes—perhaps paw knocks, though they didn’t make sense to me. There were about ten blows, strong and loud, but they didn’t seem to indicate movement or predation. It was as if the creature, still unseen, was trying to push the vegetation around it with great force. It was baffling.

     I watched, motionless, trying to identify it. My first guess was a frog, but it would have to be enormous to create such commotion. Perhaps a bullfrog—they can weigh up to a kilo—but the behavior didn’t align. A turtle, maybe? No, turtles are slow and deliberate. Could it be a coypu? It certainly wasn’t a moorhen—the tightly packed rushes would act as bars, and a moorhen is too small to make such a racket. Whatever it was, its powerful display didn’t match anything I’d encountered before. Strange.

    I remain still, immobile, concentrating. I can't see the animal, but I am overwhelmed by an unsettling feeling that something is deeply wrong.

     It begins again, violently, with bursts of energy, and I feel worse. An inexplicable worry grows within me, as if a foreign emotion has invaded my mind. I try to observe myself, analyze what is happening both outside and within. The rushes move with each blow, water splashes, then calm returns—but inside me, the turmoil persists. The calm feels deceptive. It's as though... as though what I'm feeling doesn't belong to me.

     I sense despair.

   The thought is irrational, even absurd. How could I be feeling something that isn't mine? It seems delusional, yet the sensation is so vivid that I can't push it away. I try reasoning with myself, but introspection can mislead.

    Before I realize it, I am already moving, gripped by urgency. My shoes come off quickly; I force myself to remove my socks, though I feel an almost compulsive need to step into the water immediately. First one foot, sinking 10 cm into water, 10 into mud. Then the other, deeper now—20 cm of water, 30 of silt. With a third step, I reach the area where I saw the movement. I still can't identify the creature, but I see a dark mass. It must be it.

     I hesitate only briefly before stepping further. The mud is treacherous, and I could easily sink, but I try to distribute my weight by stepping on the rush stems and vegetation, hoping they act like snowshoes on the surface. My arms stretch toward the submerged mass.

     It works. I sink, but slowly. My presence triggers another violent commotion, and suddenly, I understand. It’s a trapped wader. The bird is lying in the water, its body submerged, one wing resting on the vegetation, and its head barely emerging among the thick stems. Its legs are hopelessly tangled in fishing line, wrapped tightly around plants and limbs—a careless trap left by fishermen who didn’t bother to clean up.

     The bird is exhausted, almost lifeless, held up only by the vegetation. It barely has the energy to keep its head above water. When I carefully grasp it, speaking softly to reassure it, the bird doesn’t even struggle. Its frailty is heartbreaking.

     I must act quickly. The water now reaches my thighs, and there's no firm ground beneath me. I shift my weight to one foot and pull out my pocketknife. The tangle of wire, plants, and legs is so dense that I cut through everything preventing me from returning to the bank with the bird. I make rough, decisive cuts, freeing it just enough to retreat.

    Pulling my leg out of the mud is arduous, the sticky silt clinging to me stubbornly. When I finally succeed, I shake off the muck in the water, noting its lack of odor—perhaps a sign of the river's health.

     Back on the bank, I sit cross-legged, leaning against my backpack with the bird cradled in my lap. Its legs stretch out before me, and I begin untangling the mess. The tightest loops dig into its flesh, too snug to fit my blade underneath. I start with the easier knots, gradually loosening the rest. It takes ten painstaking minutes. Throughout, the bird barely moves.

    I speak gently, tenderly, trying to soothe its instincts and fears. Whether it’s my tone, its exhaustion, or resignation, the bird remains calm, only watching me with quiet intensity.

     When the last thread is cut and I confirm it has no injuries, I place the bird on the ground in front of me. Weak but free, it pinches my fingers with its beak. I can’t tell if it’s a gesture of gratitude or the feeble defense of a creature still wary of me. I choose to believe it’s the former.

     Releasing it, I watch as it slowly rises, unsteadily finding its footing. After a moment, it runs toward the bushes and disappears into the greenery.

     I stay seated, motionless. Time passes. Everything is quiet. I realize after these serene moments that I experience a pleasant feeling of intense satisfaction, of deep contentment. And yet I remain calm and still. Everything has been so calm. Like in a dream, as if detached. Executed without agitation, without even emotions I believe, and the result was a succession of moments of accuracy, of sure and precise movements. What remains is this deep feeling of calm and contentment. I feel like I’m on drugs.

     When I get up to go rinse, and then bathe while I'm at it, I hear it fly away. It emerges from the thicket, climbs the small slope leading to the dike, and takes off. It seems to be doing well. Today, I tell myself, I saved a life. It makes me smile. I feel good.

     I bathe. That feels good too.

    I leave once dried, carrying this feeling of having done something—not necessarily important, though from the bird's perspective it certainly was—but something right. And that’s what gives me a real, deep, and strangely persistent contentment. I then walk with a light step despite a heavy backpack. The sun has gone down, and so has the temperature.

     I am now approaching the border with Serbia. I know this all the more because I am stopped by a border police patrol as soon as I leave the path running along the river. They check my passport, ask where I'm going, and inquire about my route to ensure I plan to pass through an official border post. Then they let me go, mentioning the village grocery store I’m about to pass. Over the next two days, it will take me to reach the border, I encounter 12 patrols in total.

     Each patrol checks me, sometimes barely an hour apart. The closer I get, the more I sense their tension. They seem worried I might cross illegally. This strikes me as illogical since they’ve already checked my passport, confirmed I’m French, and thus know I don’t need any special authorization, papers, or visas to leave Romania or enter Serbia. I’ve "shown white paws" (proven innocence) at every check.

     Now, however, these checks take up to an hour because the patrols are perplexed by my case and call for a superior to ensure no mistakes are made. Each time, I recount a bit of my story, answer trick questions designed to uncover any possible deception, and then respond to their personal curiosity while we wait for their superior. They scrutinize my passport meticulously, examining each seam, feeling the thickness of the pages, and watching my reactions as much as they inspect the document itself. If the passport were fake, I would surely betray tension or anxiety, their demeanor seems to suggest.

    After inspecting the passport, they go to the vehicle’s onboard computer, typing away. They return, acting as though something is amiss, then scan the passport cover to access the biometric chip. But they lack a network connection to verify it, which makes me smile. I remain patient. They ask again where I’m going.

     "Uh, where I’m going tonight, or in general?" I respond, seeing their puzzled faces. "Tonight, I’m going here," I say, pointing to a spot on my phone map. "But I’m heading to this customs post," I add, marking the pin from my planned itinerary, "and, in general, to Serbia. And more broadly, I’m going to Bosnia and Herzegovina, to the sanctuary of Medjugorje."

     I try to be precise, knowing every word seems scrutinized as if they’re determined to uncover some hidden motive. After all the fuss, they hand back my passport and tell me I can proceed, but warn me not to approach the border.

     I smile at them, repeating once again that my planned itinerary is clear and shows exactly where I’ll pass and how close I’ll get to the border. "Yes, okay," they reply, "but don’t go near the border, okay?"

     An exchange of deaf, almost.

     And then come the last kilometers. I leave the bushes where I spent the night. Barely 300 meters later, I hear a 4x4 SUV quickly approaching behind me. I don’t even turn around. I know it’s a border police patrol, and I know they lack internal communication, as the previous six patrols have already informed me. So, this seventh patrol has no idea about my presence yet and rushes toward me on the small dirt road as if they’re about to catch me with no chance of escape.

    The same scenario unfolds: passport control, questions, answers, doubts, a call to the chief. After 15 minutes, the chief arrives, inquires with his subordinates, then with me, then back to the others. After lengthy discussions, he authorizes me to continue (so generous!)—but only if I take a detour back to the main road. Ahead lies a village on my chosen route, where I plan to eat at a restaurant, shop, and refill my bottles. We can see the village, about 2 km away, at the end of the road. The main road, however, is 600 meters to my left, requiring me to retrace my steps a few hundred meters to join a dirt road leading to it.

     I have no desire to comply. The detour adds nearly 3 km to the remaining 8 I have, forcing me onto a busy road with no shade or safe resting spots. Walking straight to the village makes far more sense, even without the shade. This is an agricultural region: flat fields stretching to the horizon, with sparse vegetation barely reaching the size of bushes.

     I ask if there’s a specific reason for this demand. He replies with a rhetorical question: "Do you know where you are?" Of course, I know—I’ve just spent 15 minutes showing my route on my topographic app. With condescension, he tells me I’m near the border, in a restricted area where I’m not allowed to walk. This sounds nonsensical, though I’m not entirely sure. I ask the size of the restricted area. He claims it extends 30 km from the border, which is utterly implausible. If true, it would mean I can’t walk anywhere off main roads leading to border posts—even those on smaller roads, like the cycle path I used since Timisoara!

    The chief seems intent on forcing me to take a route that suits him better than my planned itinerary. I demonstrate on the map that my route is much more logical for a pedestrian, avoiding the long zigzags of the main road, which adds 3 km and runs through villages. I also explain that the main road is far more dangerous—long straight stretches where vehicles speed recklessly.

     Now he gets angry. He refuses to let me continue. He orders me into the patrol vehicle, offering to drop me at the border post—not to help, but to rid himself of the "problem." I refuse, not to antagonize but to preserve the integrity of my pilgrimage, done entirely on foot, as traditional pilgrims would. It would frustrate me deeply to admit that, among 8,912 km, I had to take a police car for a dozen kilometers to appease an anxious, authoritarian customs officer.

     The chief then claims the dirt road I’m following is prohibited to the public, even pedestrians, as it’s reserved for agricultural vehicles. At this point, he loses all credibility. His uniform is tainted by his dishonest, authoritarian attitude, prioritizing personal convenience over proper conduct. I firmly refuse his orders, pointing out that the only "problem" is my presence on camera, triggering alarms for patrols unaware that I’ve already been checked seven times. This is their problem, not mine—it’s simply their job to manage it.

     As the situation escalates, I call the customs officers I met upon entering Romania. By chance, they’re hiking with their families on the Via Transylvanica. They confirm my rights: the restricted area is only 20 meters from the border, and tractor paths are entirely permissible for pedestrians. They even express frustration that I’ve been harassed over something so straightforward.

     I hand my phone to the chief. His expression falls as he realizes he’s speaking to a colleague—someone fluent in Romanian and familiar with the law. Caught abusing his authority and lying, he loses face—not just personally but for his uniform. This saddens me, not for him, but for the respect his position should command.

    Stanley Milgram’s experiments on authority should be studied more, I think. It might reduce such unnecessary abuses of power and the resulting erosion of respect for authority, regardless of the uniform. Romania hasn’t yet reached France’s level, where authority often lacks respect, but encounters like this suggest it might someday.

     After hanging up, the situation resolves quickly. I’m "authorized" to proceed and leave without delay, having already wasted an hour in this absurd exchange. The chief drives off, but the patrol car trails me, alternating between crawling at my speed and stopping 100 meters ahead, waiting for me to catch up, then speeding past again. By the time I reach the village, I’m covered in dust, clinging to my sweat-soaked clothes. It seems that, having embarrassed himself, the chief sought to dirty me in return.

     I would be checked five more times, but each interaction would be professional. Still, better internal communication could have spared a lot of unnecessary trips and tension for the patrols.

     Later that evening, I’d meet two teams in vans equipped with telescopic thermal cameras. These officers were friendly and, after chatting by the roadside, gave me a tour of their setup. Half the van housed detection equipment; the other half contained an office space with two gamer-like seats, joysticks, keyboards, and four screens. They spent nights in this cramped space, spotting shrews from 3 km away in total darkness.

     They were really nice guys. After the visit, they asked me many questions to which I answered with pleasure. Their interest and questioning were genuine, sincere, and personal. Before leaving, they advised me to ask the watchman of the nearby silo for permission to pitch my tent on the property. They explained that while I could continue to the spot I had planned, a bit further along the dirt road leading to the border, I might be woken up multiple times during the night by patrols. Sleeping under the watchman’s surveillance would likely ensure a full night of rest. When I asked if they could send a radio message to notify all patrols of my presence, they explained that due to shift changes, the safest option was to ask the watchman. They were confident he would agree.

     So, I went to see the watchman at the gate and asked if I could stay on the cooperative grounds. "No worries, put yourself wherever you want on this side," he said, gesturing to an area near the path I’d take in the morning.

     I pitched my tent there, a simple setup without the rainfly due to the heat but still enclosed to protect against mosquitoes. I took a "bottle shower" after sunset to rinse off sweat and dust. Over time, I’ve learned to manage a full shower with just 1.5 liters of water, adding another liter if I wash my hair. Carrying water on my back has made me very economical with it. Despite the floodlights illuminating the area around the massive silo and the adjacent park of agricultural vehicles, I had a radiant night.

     In the morning, the watchman kindly offered me fresh mineral water from an office fountain. After a handshake and a wish for good luck, I resumed my journey.

    Customs control stopped me barely 100 meters later. I was 5 km in a straight line from the border post I intended to cross and 8.5 km by the main road. Given the past two days, I thought this would be a quick check—just a confirmation that I was the "French guy" they’d already heard about. Perhaps a brief exchange, a reminder not to cross illegally, and a "have a nice day."

     But no, the same procedure unfolded: a full hour-long check, calls to superiors, and eventually, a personal escort. I couldn’t help but wonder how this compared to approaching the Iron Curtain during its time.

     This time, the escort vehicle followed me at a distance of 200–300 meters. The front passenger used binoculars, which made me smile internally, as I had planned to cross a small stretch of wasteland the car couldn’t navigate. They would need to find another path, losing sight of me for a few minutes.

     Unfortunately, nature called, and the sparse, flat terrain offered little privacy. I had hoped the tall grass of the wasteland would suffice, sparing the binoculars operator any traumatic sights. However, they anticipated my route and were already waiting on the other side. I’ll spare the details of my necessary stop, but let’s say my training in India has diminished any lingering embarrassment.

     The second patrol escorted me to within 1 km of the border post, where we parted ways after several friendly exchanges. One officer seemed genuinely interested in the spiritual aspect of my pilgrimage, while both maintained a professional yet relaxed attitude. They seemed to share my sense of the absurdity of the situation.

   During my final break before reaching the post, a surveillance drone appeared overhead. Hovering about 50 meters up, it fixed its camera on me, scanning the surroundings. Leaning against my bag, I raised a hand in greeting to help it locate me. After a few minutes, the drone moved, scanned the area again, then left.

     Shortly afterward, another patrol arrived. The officer, one of the morning’s chiefs, asked if I was okay. I said I was resting from the sun and the heavy load. "Was that your drone?" I asked. "Of course," she replied, then asked, "You’ll pass the border at the post, right?" I didn’t answer verbally, but my expression must have spoken volumes. She wished me a good trip and left.

     Finally, I reached the border and crossed without issue. A Romanian officer even came out of the post to offer me a bottle of cold water and wish me safe travels.

     Whenever locals ask if I’m well-received in their region, I always say I’ve encountered kindness, generosity, and warm welcomes everywhere. Crossing into Serbia, I felt confident this would continue.

     I wasn’t disappointed. In the first village, I approached a man cycling to ask about getting a local SIM card. He accompanied me to exchange currency and then helped me buy a SIM card, ensuring it suited my needs.

     At a café where I stopped to activate the SIM and recharge, the owner offered me a coffee on the house, saying it was her way of welcoming me as a first-time visitor.

    Later, at a restaurant, the staff refused payment for my meal, saying it was their treat. The owner even helped me refill my water bottles at a hand pump outside, making the task easier by pumping while I filled.

     All of this happened within three hours of entering Serbia. It was a warm welcome to new territory.

     As I write these words, I’m by a river where I bathed with delight every two hours during the day. My tent is pitched 3 meters from the water, shaded and hidden by large trees. I’m seated comfortably on a chair left by regulars, likely fishermen or swimmers. My back rests against one chair, my feet on another—a classic "working position."

    A fisherman arrives and sets up nearby. We greet each other, exchange pleasantries, then fall into a companionable silence. I continue writing while he fishes. After a couple of hours, less successful for him than for me, a couple visits him with cold drinks. They offer me an iced coffee, insisting despite my polite refusal. We chat briefly as I give up my chair to the lady, gallantry obliges.

     When they leave, they hand me a bottle of ice water.

    The fisherman lingers a bit longer, hoping for a catch, then eventually packs up. Shaking my hand, he asks if I’ll be there tomorrow. "No, I’ll be back on the road in the morning," I reply. A fish splashes loudly in the water, as if mocking him.

     "So goodbye," he says, "and good trip."

     "Goodbye, thank you."

     He leaves, and I retreat to my tent. Darkness has almost fully fallen. Another great day.
