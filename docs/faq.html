​How many kilometers I walk per day?

     ​I am asked nearly every time I meet someone how many kilometers I walk per day.
     It's a really legitimate question, for sure. But I can't really answer it without some explanation. Or yes, I could, but I would have to say that it depends. 

     You might not think that's an answer, so I would then go into detail to explain. 

     You can understand that the number of kilometers I walk each day depends on many factors. 

     - The weather. Of course, outside conditions make a big difference. Rain slows me down a lot, as does snow. And if the sun is really strong, it doesn't help with long walks either. On the other hand, wind can be helpful if it's at your back... But you see my point here...

     - The weight. The backpack's weight is a very important variable. See the topic of the backpack for more details. It is obvious that the less heavy it is, the farther and longer I can walk... The weight is always fluctuating, depending on how much food and water I have with me, and whether I’m wearing my clothes or carrying them in the backpack. You might think it is the same weight either way, but no, it makes a difference. It's easier to have the clothes on you and the backpack lighter than to carry the same clothes inside the backpack... I wonder how much of this difference is psychological, but it is definitely there...

     - The road. This has a huge influence on walking. The easiest would be a paved road because it's flat and clean. You can walk on it without really taking care of where your feet are going, and you don't have to lift your feet very high for each step. That is not the case on paths through  fields or forests, where you have to take care of every step in order not to fall. Remember, the backpack increases the risk of falling and makes it more dangerous. Walking on grass slows you down significantly. It's like the fibers are trying to catch your feet with every step. So now just imagine when you have snow up of the knee. Progress becomes excruciatingly difficult and slow...

     - The slope. It's obvious. But still, the difference is really really huge whether you're climbing up or going down. However, it’s more complex than it seems because there’s a point where descending can also be slow, depending on the steepness. Again, the weight of the backpack makes things much more critical...

     - The physical condition. This must be carefully considered for long-term walking. There's the immediate factor of how you feel today, which influences your walk of the day. The night you had, what you ate or drank yesterday that you shouldn't have... But there are also long-term considerations. You quickly learn that forcing yourself to walk or pushing beyond your limits is really counterproductive. If you feel tired, you must stop and rest, or you’ll pay for it in the coming days. You risk developing tendonitis, for example, and if that happens, you could be facing 2 or 3 weeks of pain... If you don't listen to your body and keep pushing for more, you won’t be able to sustain years of walking. You might manage a few weeks, maybe a few months at most, but eventually, you’ll have to stop... So yeah, your physical condition is a major factor. 

     - The mental state is really an important thing. However, it's complex. For example, there are days when I feel like I won't walk far, yet I end up covering a long distance. And opposite, sometimes I start the day with the feeling I will have a big, long walk, but it doesn't happen at all. There is a certain state of mind that sometimes appears where you feel as if you're inside your head, and your body doesn't really need you to be in charge. The body is occupied with the simplest task of putting one foot in front of the other and repeating. In that moment, depending on where your mind is focused, you can become totally absorbed in your thoughts and forget about the physical task. When you finally reconnect with your body, you might realize you've covered a huge distance without noticing. It can be really really impressive. Sometimes 3 or 4 times what you would normally walk.

     - The encounters. One of the rules I follow in this practice is to be available to the people I meet along my way. It's the only thing I can give and, in some way, give back a bit of what I receive by sharing my experience with those who show interest. So, I take the time to answer all questions and satisfy the curiosity and interest of everyone I encounter...

     - The beauty. Yes, the beauty around me. That is a real slowing factor. There are places where I stop and simply look around for a long time, sometimes for hours. The panorama of the mountains, the flow of a river or stream, the wildlife I encounter... Some places are so beautiful that even if it's still morning, I decide to spend the rest of the day there and set up my bivouac.

     That brings me to the most important consideration. But as you can already see, it's difficult to answer the frequently asked question of how many kilometers I walk per day...

     What is that most important consideration I’m talking about?

     It's just that the distance has become totally irrelevant. So much non-important that the question does not appear anymore in my mind. The approach has become something else totally independent of the number of kilometers I walk, even though it's those kilometers that give me these experiences. It's a bit hard to express, but no matter how many kilometers I walk, I consider mainly the quality of the time I had during the day. That gives me a much more accurate measure of the day than the distance walked...

     You have to understand that the pilgrimage is a practice aimed at bringing the pilgrim inward. The path supports the transition from the external to the internal. In a way, it's an excuse for the quest to find oneself. The destination is a call that helps guide the pilgrim, pulling him through difficult times, inspiring and motivating him. The path itself isn’t inherently important. It becomes meaningful through the opportunities it offers for self-exploration and discovery. The way he walks teaches him to find himself. It is a support, perhaps even an excuse. But the true journey is inward, and that happens in complete stillness. In this context, the kilometers I walk each day are completely irrelevant...

     You see...

     Haha, but I’m sure you still wonder how many kilometers I actually walk per day. So, if you really need numbers, I’d say it varies between 10 and 30 km. This past year, as I've been feeling quite physically tired, it’s more often around 10 to 15 than anything else. 

     But I have discovered something.

     When everything is fine—the backpack isn’t unusually heavy and conditions aren’t extreme—the walking distance is around 25 to 30 km daily. And that’s about 7 leagues. That led me to think of the legend of the 7-league boots, if you know it. The ancients knew about this number, and for them, traveling mostly on foot, the concept of the 7-league boots was a way of imagining a journey—a distance to be covered on foot. Each step with the boots, covering 7 leagues, was like a full day of walking for a human. Interesting, isn’t it?​

​How heavy is my backpack?

     There is this one word, one of the first ones I learn in each country I cross. At the moment I am writing this, I am in Romania, and the word is 'greau'. That means 'heavy', and it always comes out of the mouths of people who touch my backpack or try to move it. It usually comes with an astonished face, a big open mouth, and a look full of doubt concerning my sanity.

     Because my bag is heavy. Like really heavy. And so, naturally, the question arises: how heavy?

    There is only one answer to that question. It is a universal answer that anyone, anywhere in the universe, carrying a backpack on a long walk would definitely agree with me to consider as TOA (The Only Answer): too much!

    I often add: 'too much and a half!' to make sure people understand. Because whatever you carry on your back during a walk, a trek, a pilgrimage—whatever it is—it's always, ALWAYS too much... To the point where you find yourself having mixed feelings, even to the point of hostility towards your backpack. Even when the latter ultimately has no role in this matter... a backpack is always too much, too much and a half heavy...

    Now, of course, you want to know; you want numbers. And now I'm going to have to frustrate you, and maybe annoy you a little, but to answer the question, I can only say that it depends... The weight of my backpack is never the same. However, it only depends on a few variables. Let me explain.

     The easy part is just about water and food. 

    Water is always an issue. Especially for the long-distance walker. The constant and repetitive movement of walking is really conducive to the development of tendonitis, which can appear very easily and very quickly. Just a few hours of inattention to the body's signals and you have three weeks of pain that is more or less intense and widespread, but always really annoying or even disabling. The only way to prevent this, or to treat it when mild, is by hydrating a lot. And when I say a lot, I mean more than you are used to, more than you feel you need. This means drinking even if you don't feel the need to, even if you don't feel thirsty. The point is to drink often. Very often, in small quantities. Thus, when you are about to cross unknown terrain, such as forests or mountains, and you have no idea where, when, or if you will find drinking water, you end up having to carry a lot. I have been in some places where I was forced to carry ten liters on my back... 10 kg just for water...

     Food is less problematic because it's ok not to eat for a day or two if you have to. I actually think it's healthy to fast for a few days now and then. So when it comes to choosing between carrying food or water, water always comes first. And if there is no room left for food, that's ok.

     As you can see, there are many fluctuations in the weight of the backpack. But somehow, there is a base weight—a weight that is mostly constant. I say 'mostly' because this weight is actually decreasing slowly over time. 

     This weight is the sum of the objects and materials you need, as well as those you think you need. As we walk, what we really need stays, and what we thought we needed disappears. We quickly realize that what is disappearing are our anxieties, our fears. Fear of lack, fear of being uncomfortable, fear of many things, fear of everything. So many fears inhabit us so deeply that we don't even realize them, at least not until their weight becomes unbearable. And in the backpack, it becomes unbearable very quickly. If you don't react, your body will complain fast: fatigue, shoulder pain from the backpack straps, the onset of tendonitis, and back pain...
​
     And slowly, you decide that some things are not really necessary, so you can drop them. This isn't really useful, so you can give it away. In this way, the journey teaches you to let go of your fears, and in doing so, to lighten the weight of your backpack until it reaches a limit.

    And this brings you to the irreducible limit. That is to say the limit defined by the weight of the essentials you really need to continue your way without putting yourself in danger or without experiencing difficulties or discomfort so great that it compromises your motivation to continue your path. If the path is to teach you, it is necessary to walk it for that to happen. I imagine that the more you have to learn, the longer you have to follow it. Immersing yourself in conditions that are too difficult could shorten the experience and hinder the learning process. We can always force ourselves, push through, but that is only possible for a short time. This is not possible over time. Especially in the long term... Logical, isn't it?

     So yes, there is a kind of minimal weight that you reach after some time. For me, considering full autonomy across all four seasons, it's about 20 kg. The tent, the mattress, toiletries, clothes (including winter clothes), the big sleeping bag, the external battery and cables, the cooking pot and small stove, the small box of replacement materials to repair the backpack, tent, or to stitch clothes or shoes, and the minimal pharmacy I carry (antiseptic, a little bottle of propolis, and a small bottle of grapefruit seed extract as a natural antibiotic)... nothing fancy, but all of this adds up to 20kg... 

     20 kg dry, meaning without food or water.

     20 kg...

     That's the weight of my fears.
