Decision

18/11/2023

0 Comments

 
     ​Again, it’s been a while since I shared my journey with you. I had nothing new or special to say. No change in the pilgrim’s experience of the path.

   But ultimately, I’m not required to be original or new all the time, especially when there is nothing new to share. So, I might as well pick up the nail where I left it last time—if you remember the metaphor.

     So, here it is.

    I came down from the forest through a small grassy meadow to reach a small road downhill. I had just spent three nights in this lovely forest, in a quiet and protected area. When I first arrived there, I was in a bit of a hurry. It was my birthday.

    I had stopped at a restaurant to enjoy a hot meal and a power outlet. When I left, I quickly felt the urgent need to find a bathroom—in real hurry. It happens. For the restaurant’s defense, I don’t think the food quality was to blame, but rather the possible presence of non-vegetarian elements in the preparation. Something like pork fat for cooking, perhaps. For many people who are unfamiliar with vegetarianism, inattentive, or simply mischievous, cooking vegetables with lard still counts as a vegetarian dish...

   For someone like me, who has abstained from meat products for more than 32 years, my digestive system reacts immediately—and violently.

     Of course, this isn’t ideal on a busy road where each side is occupied by buildings and fenced-off properties. The few kilometers that followed were epic: tiring, distressing, and punctuated by numerous “open toilet breaks.”

     Happy birthday, Emmanuel!

     So I didn’t really choose where to stop. As soon as I saw a perpendicular path leading to a forest, I followed it until I found a welcoming place. I came down from that spot well-rested after three nights there. As I arrived at this small road, intending to follow it to a larger one, a gentleman in his garden spotted me and called out from afar.

     He was far enough away that he had no idea who I was or what I was doing there, just that I had the look of a backpacker. Far enough, too, that I couldn’t really hear him, but I could see he was calling out. So I changed my direction and walked toward him. When I got within earshot, I said my usual line: "French/pilgrim/not understand." He replied, “Ah, okay! Coffee?”

    The proposition was clear and tempting. A hot coffee early in the morning is always welcome, especially with the chill of the season. So, getting one just as I was starting my day was unexpected but very pleasant.

     We sipped it together, joined by his wife on the terrace of their house, where he was finishing work on an outbuilding. It was a very nice encounter—simple and full of kindness. After an hour with them, I got back on the road. The main road wasn’t pleasant to walk along, as important roads often aren’t. The number of vehicles, and especially their speeds, made walking uncomfortable, stressful, and dangerous.

   But I had no alternative, so I carefully monitored the traffic coming toward me. I wore headphones to block out the noise—whether with music, an audiobook, or even just silence.

     At the intersection where I needed to take the left branch, a pedestrian crossed the road to meet me and asked what I was doing there. I showed him the short text recorded in my translator, summarizing the main ideas in one paragraph. After several handshakes and a few "very goud, very goud," I continued my walk toward the small grocery store located right at the intersection.

     With my backpack emptied of all food, and having not eaten the day before or that morning, I thought a stop for provisions would be welcome. I placed my bag on one of the benches on the small terrace near the entrance—village grocery stores often have a café/bar attached with a terrace—and went inside.

     Two cans of vegetable pâté, a block of cheese, five nicely red tomatoes, two bananas, and a loaf of sliced bread later, I stepped outside to grab the coins I had forgotten in the jacket on my bag, intending to add them to the 50 lei note I had already given. I wanted to simplify payment by offering a rounded amount. When I returned to the counter, the lady indicated that I owed nothing. “It’s for me,” she said. “Drum bun. Have a nice journey.”

     Isn’t that surprising? Isn’t it pleasant? Isn’t it touching?

     Apparently, the gentleman I had met earlier had entered after me, and while I was choosing my cans of pâté, he briefly introduced me to the lady. That’s how she ended up giving me back my money and saying, “It’s for me.”

     After carefully putting the entire donation into the appropriate boxes in my bag, I resumed my journey, only to discover a roadside restaurant just a little further along the way. A sort of container-turned-chip shop/French fry/kebab stand. The idea of a hot and different meal tempted me. After all, I could save what I had bought for later. Hot food isn’t an everyday luxury. So I stopped.

     Leaning on the counter, the gentleman ahead of me saw me, and we exchanged greetings. After my customary "French/pilgrim/not understanding" introduction, he began speaking in surprisingly good French—leftovers from high school, he said. It was not only practical but also pleasant to have a conversation and a translator for my order, just to ensure the hamburger I was ordering was truly vegetarian!

     When his order was ready, he shook my hand and went on his way. A very nice meeting with a local teacher—Romanian, of course. While waiting for my burger, wallet in hand and ready to pay, I noticed the lady preparing it was on her phone, while another finished assembling a bag of fries and another burger.

     I assumed it was a phone order—until she handed me her phone. On the screen was a message she had just written and translated for me: “The two burgers and fries are for you. It’s free. We wish you well. Have a nice journey.” I don’t want to sound melodramatic, but I nearly cried.

   It’s not just the gesture itself—it’s the repetition, the frequency. The consistency. It touches something deep inside. You see? So the nail sinks in...

    It was still only midday, and I had about ten kilometers left before reaching my destination: the Basilica of Cacica. I’m here now. Well, not precisely in the basilica as I write these words, but I’m right next door—maybe 200 meters away. I’m staying in a family home. It was one of the priests from the basilica who approached me as I was leaving. Night had already fallen; it was almost 5 p.m.

     “Do you know where to sleep?” he asked me.
     “No, not yet,” I replied.
     “Come with me,” he said.

     He led me to a neighbor’s house, and the neighbor then took me to another house, just behind his, on the other side of the garden.

    He showed me the kitchen as he plugged in the refrigerator. Then he led me upstairs to the bathroom, saying there would soon be hot water (he knew the exact words a pilgrim would appreciate—or perhaps my smell made it clear how necessary it was!). And then he showed me the bedroom. “You stay as long as you want,” he said.

     I’ve been experiencing this for three years. Three years!

    Maybe you don’t realize—I don’t know. You can only read what I can write, and many things cannot be put into words. But imagine living this...

     So.

     Today, I decided something important.

    Three years was the length of time I initially hoped to experience when I decided to go beyond southern Poland and the Czestochowa Monastery. It seemed enough—not to learn everything I wanted, but to establish something permanent.

     Certainly, the impression is profound. Certainly, the imprint is there, the trace is marked. But I feel its fragility. I intuitively sense that there is much more. The nails can go further, deeper, to ensure they don’t come out.

     I’m probably not a good student. I’m a very slow learner.

     But I want to continue. It’s not that I don’t want to stop—I want to continue.

     The nuance is very important.

     So.

    I’m heading back tomorrow morning. This may seem obvious to you, but it wasn’t. In recent months, I’ve experienced certain conditions that made me doubt—doubt the relevance of continuing this path, at least in this way. And the desire to continue is not a sufficient condition for a serious practitioner. Alone, it can lead to confusion, which is the opposite of what I’m seeking.

    So I stopped to reflect on the path—the one I’ve traveled and the one ahead. But neither is as eloquent as the silence and well-being I feel this morning, walking under the snow that has been falling heavily since yesterday.

     It’s beautiful. It’s good. I feel good. I feel free. I am happy.

     I think I’ll wait until I truly can’t continue before stopping.

   Put yourself in my place—wouldn’t you look further for what the nail fixes? What work it participates in and contributes to? Would you have better things to do?

     I wish you the best.

     See you soon.
