Vilnius

6/7/2021 0 Comments

I know, I should have told you about Vilnius, my second destination. It's already been a week since I left the place...
    I would have shared my arrival, and all the people I met on my way since my last update. So many nice people. Always. Only.

For example, I would have told you about this lovely elderly couple, Maceliai and Vingriu. I say "elderly," but honestly, they felt so young in spirit. He, for instance, had the energy and enthusiasm of a supercharged teenager, practically hopping as he spoke. Both of them had bright, keen eyes. It was such a nice meeting.

From the street, I asked them for water. He came straight to the gate, opened it, and invited me in—all smiles. And what a smile! You can see it in the photos—genuine happiness! They offered me tea, and soon fresh cheese appeared, pressed that very morning, along with honey (forest flowers), bread, and pickles.

We talked, though it wasn't easy since they didn’t speak a word of English, but were fluent in German and Russian. They were from that generation that doesn’t give a phone translator enough time to work—reading translations aloud as they appear, causing the phone to misunderstand and start translating again, erasing the previous text. After a few tries, we mostly gave up on the translation app, but we still managed to have a great conversation.

He showed me an ultralight plane he built himself—including the plans! Then he gave me a tour of his workshops: first the mechanical one, with a milling machine, lathe, and welding tools; then the woodshop, equipped with a router, planer, jointer, and bandsaw. Next came his greenhouse and his homemade sauna, heated with a wood stove. Everything at his home had his personal touch, including a visit to his beehives.

The man must be hyperactive, a genius, and incredibly skilled with his hands. Everything was simple yet impressive. He even offered me a shower in a small building in his garden, where the water tank was made from a repurposed Russian bomb shell that holds 3,000 liters, heated naturally by the sun. With the week’s temperatures at 35°C, you can imagine how welcome that shower was!

They were deeply connected to nature—everything organic and healing. He showed me a small wooden hut designed for naps. The "bed" was actually two beehives, with the roof made from two more. You’re surrounded by bees, enveloped in the soothing scents of wax, honey, pollen, propolis, and pinewood, while the buzzing sound of thousands of bees hums like a lullaby. You can even feel the subtle vibrations, as if the whole hut is purring. He even built a periscope so you can observe the activity inside the beehives while you drift off, counting bees instead of sheep! It's supposed to be healing.

I don’t know if it works, but I can say this: a nap there must be amazing, and I’d love to be as healthy as he is at 77 years old!

They also showed me a pagan ceremony they participate in—shamanic practices around a fire with songs and tambourines.

It was truly a beautiful encounter.

Or there’s Rasa—how I ended up in her garden is a story of its own. I had lost my track in the forest and, being too lazy to retrace my steps, I decided to just keep going, thinking, "There are no mistakes, after all—the path I take becomes my path." A bit of high philosophy that ultimately led to me crossing a river on a fallen tree trunk and battling my way up a steep riverbank through tall grass, nettles, and brambles. This adventure ended with me stumbling into the back of her property.

After asking for water, she offered me coffee, then lunch. Later, to make myself useful, I mowed her garden lawn with her mower, clearing it up nicely before we sat back down on her veranda to chat. I ended up staying the night, and since it poured rain the next day, she took me for a drive in her car to visit at least six beautiful spots I would have passed without noticing otherwise.

On the way back, we stopped at a wine shop where I managed to find some Côte du Rhône AOP, which paired perfectly with the “real cheese” you might remember from my previous update. That evening, we enjoyed the wine on her veranda while her neighbor joined us for a chat. It was a delightful French-Lithuanian evening.

Rasa and I stayed in touch, and we met again in Vilnius a week or so later. It turned out the countryside house where I met her was her holiday home. She normally lives in Vilnius, works in a bank, and is very connected—she even has one of those smartwatches that keeps her updated on everything happening on her phone. It’s impressive how efficient she is.

I’d also tell you about another couple who offered me dinner and a place to stay just a few days later, which turned out to be perfect timing because the weather forecast predicted a massive storm. And I mean massive—at one point, my phone, even in flight mode, emitted a warning buzz, displaying an alert directly from the government. That’s when you realize flight mode is just a software setting, not a physical switch!

Even though the alert was in Lithuanian, it was clear enough: bad weather was on the way. Camping in the forest during a storm isn’t exactly the safest idea—imagine what protection a tent offers against falling branches! So, staying in their beautiful garden was not only safer but also lovely. We spent the evening watching the lightning and talking, enjoying the storm from a safe and cozy vantage point.

So I finally arrived in Vilnius, and I could have told you about my first night camping near the Gate of Dawn, where a morning rainstorm turned into a river that flooded my tent, soaking everything—my belongings and me included. I could have told you how I spent four hours the next day drying everything out, especially my battery packs, which were literally dripping. I was lucky enough to find an air dryer and used it on low heat to avoid frying them. (The internet says it’s a terrible idea to let your battery packs get wet—it kills them, apparently, and should never happen. So, I guess I’m really lucky because all of mine survived! I would have been especially disappointed to lose the last one, a gift from someone on my way, with such excellent capacity and quality. This one might even be waterproof!)

I could have told you about Our Lady of the Gate, the Basilica, or the beautiful churches scattered throughout the town—or just about the town itself.

I could have also told you how I was invited to a convent near the Gate of Dawn, where I met some wonderful sisters and stayed for four days. They gave me the time and space I needed to take care of everything I had to do.

There are so many things I could have shared, and I guess I’ve mentioned a little of it now. But what I really want to tell you about is something I witnessed—something that left a strong impression on me.

One Saturday evening, I was coming back from a dinner I had prepared with Rasa at her place in Vilnius. It was a way to thank her for her warm hospitality and to enjoy a "French dinner" that we cooked together (though I really screwed it up). I also had the chance to meet her friends, which was another lovely experience.

So, it was quite late, around 11:30 p.m., and I was sitting on a tram in the suburbs of the capital, replaying in my head how the spinach tourte had gone so wrong, when I saw what I’m about to describe.

The tram had stopped, and more people were getting in than getting out. I was standing in the middle, with only a few seats still available—two of them right in front of me. A young guy, who had just boarded through the front door, spotted the seats and immediately picked up his pace, eager to claim them before anyone else. So far, it was a completely ordinary scene, the kind you see all the time. He was probably around 19 years old, chatting noisily but cheerfully with his friend, as young people often do on a Saturday night.

At first, I didn’t pay much attention and turned my gaze toward the rear of the tram, where the back door was letting in a few more passengers. And there, the same situation was unfolding. Two Afro-Lithuanian guys, dressed in the full rapper-style outfit—caps, T-shirts, gold chains, the whole look—had also spotted the same seats...

At that moment, it was obvious—I was about to witness a social tension event. You know, one of those moments you see coming. Both parties had that seat in mind, both sped up to claim it for themselves. They accelerated, and suddenly everything seemed to play out in slow motion. My head went from right to left, and back again, faster as they got closer, and then—bing! Of course, they reached the spot at the same time. Standing face to face, just a foot apart, one with his hand on the back of the seat, the other gripping the vertical bar in front of it.

This is the moment where you'd expect frustration to explode. You know the scenario: the closer someone gets to their goal, the higher the expectation; the higher the expectation, the worse the frustration when they’re deprived. Basic psychology! And obviously, one of them wasn’t going to sit. Someone was going to be frustrated!

It felt like such a pivotal moment to watch—something that could reveal so much about the social dynamics of the place... about human behavior. And, let’s be honest, maybe I’d learn a few colorful swear words in the process.

I projected my expectations, drawing parallels. Paris suburbs, Saturday night, young people on public transport... I braced myself for at least some incivility, if not outright violence. Verbal aggression seemed like the bare minimum I could expect.

But no.

They stood there, facing each other, realized the situation, and—together, at the same time—offered the seat to the other!
    "You first!"
    "No, you!"
    "Please, it's okay, take it!"
    "Are you sure?"
    "Yes, yes, go ahead!"

And then, one of them sat down with his friend, while the other held out his fist for a friendly bump. You know the gesture: Hey, give me five! Yeah, bro!

They smiled. They were relaxed. They were human.

I was impressed. How can I describe it? I felt comfortable, confident, and at ease on public transport in the suburbs of the capital, late on a Saturday night, surrounded by a big group of young people, some of whom were lightly tipsy.

Now, you might not think this is a big deal. I mean, sure, you could argue that this should just be normal. But in my experience—at least mine—for far less than this, I’ve seen middle fingers raised and unsavory comments about people’s mothers thrown around.

It’s hard for me to tell if I’m just hypersensitive to this now. Spending ten months mostly alone, following a discipline where most of my time is spent in meditation, contemplation, prayer, or the recitation of mantras as part of my yoga practice—all of it aimed at what we often call spirituality—might have changed how I see the world.

I’m aware of this shift, but I can’t measure its extent. I know I’m not objective, but I don’t know how much I’ve changed. So many things I witness now seem surreal at first, but because they happen every day, they’ve become my new normal. Meanwhile, what’s conventionally considered "normal" often feels unbearably sad in comparison.

I’m not sure what’s more significant here: the scene itself or my perception of it. What’s certain is that my experience of pilgrimage—my Sadhana—has altered me.

One thing I know for sure: it makes me happy. It makes me feel good. It gives me a sense of reconciliation with humanity, a stark contrast to the pervasive fear mainstream media loves to sell. The message of the world is always “Be careful. Outside is dangerous.”

But my experience?
    People are nice.

So be confident. Be trusting. Be grateful, be kind. And love… laugh… smile. Life is beautiful, in a beautiful world with beautiful people. Even if you sleep outside every day, cannot wash yourself every day, cannot wash your clothes every week, don’t have hot coffee in the morning, or cannot dry yourself when it’s raining… it’s still nice to live. (So imagine when you have a house, a car, a family around you, a hot shower, a coffee machine...).

Ok. I swear I didn’t drink. Or smoke. Or anything else…

Let me tell you another one!

Two days ago, as I was on my way to Aglona in Latvia (my next and third destination), I arrived in a small town and came across a cute church bordered by a small park with a little river running around it. A really beautiful place. I decided to stop for a while and splash myself in the river. So I went down the alley to get closer, and I saw a bank full of people sitting there.

No problem, I stopped about 20 meters away, dropped my backpack, and sat on it to take my shoes off. Before I could even do that, one of the three guys called out to me, waving a bottle of vodka and saying something that could have been “hey heu euuuu ah beee labbbeu heuuu”... though I can’t be entirely sure of the translation.

This is another good example of self-projection—where I, like many others, find myself assuming things. I thought, Ah, just a bunch of homeless, drunken guys squatting in the park. But I got over that quickly. I reminded myself that I, too, might look like someone who sleeps outside, doesn’t wash every day, and so on. You see what I mean…

So I said hello and accepted their offer to join them, figuring I could explain a bit about myself. They turned out to be really jovial, sympathetic, and friendly. I hadn’t even started introducing myself—my name or the fact that I was a pilgrim—when one of them handed me a shot of vodka.

Okay… I took it and began my explanation. Before I could finish—pretty quickly, I might add—I had to politely refuse the fourth shot.

It could have ended there: just some drunk people sharing drinks with a stranger who probably looked like one of them. But then, a woman passed by on the path, and they gave her the same warm welcome, offering her a drink as well.

I had serious doubts about this. I thought, This is a bad idea. There’s no way she’ll engage with them—she’ll just walk by with an air of disdain. But to my surprise, she stopped. She talked with them a bit and even found out that they were celebrating one of their birthdays. And then—they hugged.

No way. They must have known each other. Surely, I thought, they were longtime acquaintances.

But no. This same thing happened three more times while I stood there with them. Two more people joined and had a shot of vodka with them. Another woman even sat down with them for a while.

After splashing myself in the river (which they assured me was totally fine to do), one of the men offered to introduce me to the priest of the church. He knew where the priest lived, just a few hundred meters away, and thought maybe I could find accommodation there. He came with me, and that’s how I left this merry group of people—people I’m now ashamed to have initially judged as drunken homeless.

Okay, sure, they were drunk. And yes, they were drinking outside in a park. But the way they made me feel—like we were neighbors who had known each other for years—was humbling.

That’s it. Maybe it doesn’t seem like much to you. But for me, here, in my nearly ten months of walking now, I can assure you I’m taking these small, seemingly insignificant things as truly important—little everyday miracles. And even though I’m not particularly fond of vodka, I gladly drank with them from their glass. I was happy to answer whatever questions they asked, happy to share moments with total strangers I probably would have ignored in my “normal” life.

And when I left, I felt genuinely happy. A big smile on my face... (The vodka might have helped with that because, honestly, I’m really not used to it...).

Today, I was supposed to get moving since yesterday was already a day off! But it’s raining hard. Really hard. So I decided to stay in my tent again. I have enough water in my bottles, enough food, and enough battery to write a long message to give you an update!

And you know what? I just received a message from Rasa. She’s planning to take her car and meet me this weekend by a lake I’ll be passing on my way—just like that. Just to spend an afternoon on the shore with a guy she’s only met twice...

I hope you’re well and happy.
    And I hope my update isn’t too long...
    But I needed to say this, and I did...
