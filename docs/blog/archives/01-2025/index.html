Crossing the Balkans

11/1/2025

2 Comments

 
     A lot of time has passed since the last update. I had many opportunities to write, but I didn't really feel like I had anything particular to say. So, I didn't.

     I could have shared that over the past few months of autumn, I traveled across Serbia, passing through Belgrade and the mountains, receiving a warm welcome everywhere I went. I could have mentioned that this year wasn’t very good for mushroom hunting in the area I visited, as the summer was too hot and dry, and the autumn wasn’t rainy enough to sufficiently moisten the soil for the mycelium to grow. But I didn’t...

     I could have used the fourth anniversary of my departure on September 26, 2020, as an occasion to send an update...

     Or the milestone of 9,000 km that I reached upon entering Bosnia-Herzegovina, although we all know how imprecise this calculation can be.

     I could have mentioned the Christmas celebrations, celebrated at least three times depending on the church, of course...

    But no, I wasn't really in the mood, I guess... and I felt it was better not to share than to force myself to write you irrelevant or uninteresting news. Though it doesn't mean that the one I am writing now will be relevant and interesting, but at least it comes from a genuine desire to share, strong enough to make me break my comfortable silence.

     I am now in Bosnia and Herzegovina, between the capital of Sarajevo and the town of Mostar, where I am headed. I found a nice route on my maps to get there. A path through the mountains, avoiding the major roads in the valley. I even found a water source along the way, so I could easily get water without having to carry too much. It was perfect—a solid plan! So I set off on that route. It was nice because it took me through all the places where the Olympic Games had been held before. It was strange to cross through them.

     The area was beautiful, albeit a bit tough, something like a 1500 m positive elevation change from the capital... You have to know that the mountains are directly around Sarajevo. The Olympic village is maybe 25 km from the center... I had just left this lovely capital when the first snowstorm hit... beautiful.... I had to stop and find a place to pitch my tent because the snowfall was intense. I'm not sure, but I think I wasn't even technically out of the Sarajevo municipality when I pitched my tent on a tiny hill, in the woods, under a heavily cloudy sky and during a thick snow episode... I stayed the next day because it was still heavily snowing, and because why not? 😂.

     The next morning, the entire area was covered with a good 50 cm of snow, with all the tree branches around bent under the weight... It was beautiful, magical as only snow can be... It was my Christmas present!

     Packing the tent became a bit harder than usual as I first had to clear all the snow around, and also remove the ice from the edge of the tent roof... And then roll the frozen fabric into the bubble I had fixed under my backpack. But I managed it; this wasn’t my first rodeo. I continued on my way uphill... As I was walking on the main road, I wasn't in too much snow. There was about 5 to 10 cm depending on when the last snowplow had passed... It was still snowing, but only lightly. Nothing compared to the past two days... So cars and trucks were going slow. I felt comfortable... My plan through the mountains was good. The entire way was through roads... No paths... So I would be clear to go, as the snow plowing seemed very efficient in the area... And that is the keyword: in the area... Efficient indeed...

     So I walked up and up and decided to find a place to pitch the tent again as night falls fast around 4 PM. Knowing that when there's so much snow, you have to dig the place you choose for the tent to fit, but also to have enough room to walk around, you need to count about 1 hour to build your camp... 1 hour of intense work because once the backpack is off your back and on the ground, you don't want to get cold, you don't want to start working in the night, and there is a lot of work to do. First, it's about 3 or 4 m³ of snow to shovel out... without a shovel... I use one of my food containers to do so. That was about when the second snowstorm hit me... which I found totally okay because I was about to finish and ready to get inside. And I did...

     The next day, it snowed again and I stayed. The spot I had found was next to the main road. It wasn't really pleasant because of the traffic, but I didn’t have much choice. About 20 meters to the side, there was a flat area, free of trees and just big enough for my tent, but not much more. I think my tent was visible from the road, but only slightly, through some branches covered with snow and only from one specific spot on the road. Motorists would have to be looking sharply in that exact direction and not focusing on their way, and this for about 2 seconds before their view was obstructed by bushes, trees, and snow. But still, at least one person saw me, probably when I was setting up the tent. How do I know? Because at one point, while I was comfortably settled in, a policeman knocked on my tent door. He might have called me from the road, but I had my audio earplugs in as I was listening to a book to drown out the traffic noise. The guy was tense, annoyed... probably because he had to walk the 35-meter path in 60 cm of snow, slalom around trees, following my footsteps to my camp. When I opened the tent and looked out at him, he wasn't really smiling. He was a bit loud, like someone issuing authority orders, and asked me to come to his car. I greeted him with a nice smile (I assume it was nice...), but I added: "Sir, I am in my underwear in my sleeping bag, can't you check my passport here?" He refused. "Get dressed and come down to the road"...

     So I did. I took it easy, but really, it’s not that simple when you're in a tent in the cold, dressing again in clothes that are still wet from your day, and getting out. I mean, it’s a pain. You lose the calories you had been conserving in the sleeping bag. You have to gear up again... It's not like just jumping into clothes in a dressing room and going. But I took it easy and went down to the road to meet the guy, managing to keep an authentic smile. I put myself in his shoes, thinking, “What the heck is that?!”

    He, with an authoritarian and borderline angry tone, demanded, "Your passport!"

    After examining it, he asked, "You are French?"

     "Yes, sir," I replied.

     "What are you doing here?"

    "I am resting, I was tired... I needed to sleep... But when you arrived, I was also listening to an audiobook, so I didn't hear you..."

     He cut me off, "Why?"

     "Why am I tired?" I asked.

     "No, why are you here?" he clarified, slightly annoyed.

    "Because it's a reasonably flat and discreet enough place to put the tent. I don't disturb anyone here, it's public, so it's good enough for me... I couldn't find a better spot on the way."

     He then cooled down a bit, "It is dangerous, sir..."

     "Dangerous? No, it's a safe place... Why? What danger?"

     "Sir, it is minus 15°C..."

      I smiled, "Exactly! It's not even really cold yet..."

     There was a moment of silence.

     He looked at me, half amused and half in disbelief. "There are bears and wolves in this area..."

     It's possible, but I doubt it... Not many, I'm sure. The bear and wolf threat is often exaggerated to scare tourists. The place isn't really interesting for a bear, even less for a pack of wolves. And even so, I've had my share of bear visits in Romania, Slovakia, and Hungary... I'm not really worried anymore.

     "I'll do my best not to disturb them, sir..." But then I smiled and added, "I have bear spray with me."

     He handed me back my passport.

     "How did you find me? Did you see me from the road? Because I'm pretty well hidden, especially now that the tent is covered by snow..."

     "Someone phoned the police station..."

    "Really? Phoned for what? I'm doing nothing wrong, I'm respectful of the place and collect all my rubbish... Why would someone complain?"

     "Are you used to this?"

  I smiled, pretending not to acknowledge that it's kind of uncommon, perhaps even unconventional: "To sleep?"

     "To sleep in these conditions?"

     "You mean comfortably set up in my tent? Yes, I am. A lot..."

     Finally acknowledging that I was not totally naive, incompetent, or unaware, he said, "Ok... You can stay... Have a good night, sir."

     "Thank you, sir. And please tell your team that if another one calls to say there is a guy here, just answer: 'Yeah, there is a guy there...'" I continued, mimicking a jaded voice, and pretended to make a distressed call in a panicked voice: "'Yes, but he is in a tent!'" Then, miming the phone to my ear, I responded in the same nonchalant jaded voice: "'Yeah, he's in a tent!' 'But sir, it is highly unusual!' 'Yes, it is. Anything else?'"

     At that moment, he laughed... I made him laugh, and it made me very happy... A small success, sometimes delicious!

    I walked back to my tent, he returned to his patrol... I am still smiling at that exchange today while writing it...

   I slept very well. The morning was sunny, blindingly so, because of the pure white snow everywhere. But it was really comfortable... I packed everything and continued on my way. That day, I planned to cross through a ski resort, perhaps have lunch there in a restaurant to charge my batteries while enjoying a hot meal, and then proceed on the upper mountain road to cross the mountain range. That was the plan... and it went well up until the lunch, the battery recharge, and the hot meal. After that, things got tricky because I learned there was about 2 meters of snow up there. Walking was out of the question, of course, but maybe the roads were okay.

     So I went looking for information about the snow conditions on the roads in the upper part and I especially wanted to be sure that all the roads on my itinerary were cleared of snow. But it was really difficult to find anyone who knew. Most people I asked were guessing, answering generally, or not at all. I went to the police, the ski resort staff... I tried anyone I could think of to extract solid information. But I ended up with nothing reliable enough for me to attempt the mountain pass. And it was getting late. So I decided to abandon my plans and head back downhill, on the main road. Not really pleasant, but I had no other choice. Night was falling at 4 PM, and I had to find a hotel for the night. Also, because I would use a lot of internet data to make new plans and chart a new itinerary... and it was not a cheap place, I can tell you that... But again, I didn't have many options.

     So, I spent the night in a glorious hotel just for an eye and half a leg (they gave me a generous discount). Refreshed, I set out in the morning, easily heading downhill, pondering a new itinerary that would make me backtrack just a bit before turning west to take a road that would showcase the 1984 Olympic Games installations.

     It was a lovely day. The sun shone brightly throughout. The path gently sloped downwards. It was a comfortable walk... The Olympic Games installations were a bit disappointing as most of them had simply been left to decay. But I guess it costs a lot to maintain those, and there probably aren't enough people using them... Even the large hotels built to host all the athletes and staff are now crumbling into ruins...

   I was truly impressed by the ski jumps. On TV, we're used to seeing those long ski jumps plunging downhill into the void. Watching athletes, half jumping and half flying with their skis slightly opened, bodies straight as ash tree planks, arms at their sides trying to gain aerodynamic lift... it's not that impressive on the screen.

    But once you see the installation they're flying from, it's a different story. Just the thought of them jumping, the visualization of them flying—it's genuinely awe-inspiring. It gives you chills... Gone is the thought, "Yeah, I could do that." No. A big no. I wouldn't jump that even if I were paid for it, not even to reclaim the eye and half a leg I'd spent previously.

    In the afternoon, around 3 PM, I stumbled upon a small restaurant on the side of the road. There was just a trench in the snow to access it; the snow in the place was about 1.8 meters deep. I paid for a hot meal, charged my batteries for the little I had used during the day, and continued on my way after leaving a few coins and a note from my wallet. The kind owner insisted on giving me two beers to take with me, which he slipped into the pocket of my backpack.

    It was getting late, so I walked quickly, my eyes carefully scanning both sides of the road for a potential place to pitch my tent. Roads on mountain slopes aren’t the best for finding camp sites, but I eventually managed one after 3.5 km.

    While walking down the main road, I noticed a small road branching off uphill. I followed it and about 100 meters later, in the area between the two roads, I found a flat, tree-free spot big enough to accommodate my tent. It was eight meters from this small road, and the main road was about 40 meters down, mainly below me—so the spot was really quiet and out of view. That was ideal because I wanted to stay two nights there. The second night would be New Year's Eve, and I didn’t want to be in the town 12km down during that time. I don’t enjoy the noisy, hysterical behaviors of New Year's celebrations, especially the crackers and domestic fireworks. The idea of staying comfortably and quietly in my tent was a far better prospect.

    I ventured off the small road toward the campsite I had chosen. There was a 1.4-meter-high embankment completely filled with snow, followed by a relatively flat area with about a meter of snow up to the campsite. As I descended the embankment, I slipped and ended up half-sitting in the snow and leaning against the embankment. The snow was fresh and very powdery, and it quickly filled the space where I had just been. The 35 meters to the site were tough to navigate, but I made it. I set my bag down in the snow and sat on it to rest a bit after the strenuous crossing. After choosing the exact spot, I began the laborious task of digging out the snow to clear the ground, moving about 4 cubic meters in total.

   This exercise kept me warm as the sun had set for the day, and the snow on my clothes constantly melted, leaving me quite wet. But I successfully cleared the area, ensuring there was enough space to maintain access all around the tent. You know the drill...

    Thus, I secured a beautiful and comfortable place to stay for two nights. As expected, I could hear the distant thunderous noise from the town downhill the next night without being disturbed by it.

    On my way down after packing up, I stopped at a charming guest house, hoping they had a restaurant—and therefore, hot coffee. It was about 2.5 km from the campsite. It was another very sunny morning, and a couple was outside drinking tea. I joined them at a nearby table and greeted them. Assuming the man was in charge, I asked him for coffee, but it turned out he wasn't the host. They were on holiday with a group of friends from a club, spending a few days celebrating and walking the passable trails. My coffee arrived, and so did the man, Adnan, who sat at the next table. We started chatting about my adventures. After a few questions, he offered me breakfast, and I eagerly accepted. We talked more as I ate.

     Then, we moved inside the guest house to use the WiFi and to show him my route on the map. It was so bright outside that it was difficult to see anything on the screen.

    Inside, the guest house was cozy and decorated like a museum with old alpinist gear. That's when I realized they were a club of alpinists. And guess what their last destination was? Nepal! Specifically, the Solukhumbu Valley—an area I'm very familiar with, where I've spent a lot of time, made many friends, and created fond memories. So, we had plenty to discuss. Sometimes, the world feels incredibly small...

    I spent about an hour and a half at the guest house, chatting and meeting people who were equally interested in my adventure and my spiritual journey.

     When it came time to leave, I approached the owner to settle my bill for the coffee and food. As my hand reached for my wallet in my back pocket, Adnan intervened, insisting on covering the cost. His generosity was deeply appreciated, yet little did I know, this gesture would soon be a source of regret. I accepted his kindness graciously.

    With my backpack, hat, and gloves, I bid farewell to everyone and resumed my journey. I still had about 8 km to descend into town, and another 2 km to reach a spot I had identified on the map as suitable for setting up camp for the night. My plan was to grab a hot lunch and a few groceries in town before heading straight to the campsite. But things were about to deviate from my plan.

   The descent was relatively easy. Going downhill is always easier when the slope is gentle. However, steep descents can be more challenging and hazardous than ascending. Fortunately, the elevation change here was mild, and I reached the town around 1:30 PM. With nightfall not until 4 PM, I felt I had ample time. At the restaurant, the limited vegetarian options led me to choose a vegetable pizza. I sat at a table next to a power outlet, stood up to ask if I could use it to charge my battery, and also inquired if they accepted Visa or only cash. "Cash only," he replied, as was typical in many shops in the country.

     Having recently left the capital with enough cash for my mountain crossing—where I expected no ATMs—I reflexively reached for my wallet to confirm I had enough. But my hand met nothing. My wallet wasn't there.

    Time seemed to freeze in that instant. Silence enveloped both inside and outside. I was paralyzed by a sudden stillness.

    Then, my mind raced wildly, bombarded by a flurry of thoughts—regrets, assumptions, plans, realizations—all at once. Tumultuous.

     A bit bordélique, as we would say in France:

   Empty back pocket! No wallet. I had no memory of moving the wallet from that pocket to another. How was this possible? Irrelevant! I checked the pocket thoroughly, only to discover a hole at the bottom...

     What exactly did I lose? What was inside and how much? No, not now. First, cancel the pizza.

     It must have fallen out along my path, likely right after I tore the pocket. But I had no memory of tearing it, nor of any incident that could have caused such damage without my noticing.

   I needed to retrace my steps... First, find a place to leave my backpack so I could quickly backtrack...

     Cancel the pizza because, as of now, I had no money and no way of getting any for several days...

     The embankment, the embankment next to the campsite where I sank into it both ways... My wallet might be there.

     Would I have realized if it fell out when I undressed in the tent? Not sure...

   When had I last used my wallet? This morning at the guest house, no—they offered me breakfast... But did I reach for my wallet? No, I hadn’t completed the motion.

     Cancel the pizza now, Emmanuel! It would only complicate things to order food without the means to pay.

      I couldn’t believe it. I had really lost my wallet.

      The pocket, the treacherous pocket of my brand-new pants! Traitorous pants!

      I needed to go to the police. If I explained everything, they should be able to help me.

      But first, stop the pizza order!

      Actions, Emmanuel, actions!

      I rushed to the counter, called the waiter over, and asked him to cancel the pizza order because I had just realized I lost my wallet. I asked if I could leave my backpack here while I went to the police station. "No problem," he told me, giving me directions to the station just 300 meters away, on the right. And off I went.

     I walked quickly, doing two things simultaneously: first, trying to recall all the places I had stopped and sat—a rock, a safety barrier, on my backpack—any spot where I might have cracked my pocket without realizing it. Second, I strategized about the steps I needed to take—blocking my Visa card, phoning France to arrange a money transfer, and reporting the loss of my ID and driver’s license.

     I arrived at the police station, which was exactly as you might expect on New Year's Day—minimally staffed with four policemen. They couldn’t help me find a place to sleep that night, nor could I leave my bag there while I searched for my wallet, and they wouldn’t dispatch a patrol to take me uphill to look for it. However, they mentioned that a patrol had been informed there was a missing wallet somewhere in the mountains. That made me laugh—it was so absurd it was almost funny, though I’m pretty sure the officer was serious.

     Of course, these answers didn’t come easily. I had to explain everything from the beginning to an officer who seemed to listen attentively until he abruptly summarized, “So you are French!” and then asked for my passport.

     Eventually, they found someone who spoke better English, and we managed to communicate more effectively. The best they could do was give me access to the WiFi at the station. That was helpful; I called Adnan, the man I had met at the guest house that morning, and explained my situation. He was busy but sent a friend to pick me up at the station. After retrieving my backpack from the restaurant, we headed back to the guest house.

     Leaving my backpack at the guest house, I took my headlamp and torch, and set out on foot to retrace my steps from the morning and the previous day's evening walk from the restaurant—where I had last used my wallet. I ended up covering about 10 km, including the campsite, but found nothing resembling my wallet.

     When I returned to the restaurant, slightly out of breath, the owner invited me to rest by the wood stove. It was already night, and the temperature outside was dropping. He then offered me a glass of hot tea and asked if I’d like him to prepare dinner for me, the same as last time. Accepting his kind offer seemed like a good idea—I could definitely use some hot food. So, I enjoyed a warm meal before heading downhill.

      At the campsite, 3.5 km down, I quickly realized that I couldn't do much without a shovel. The day had been sunny, and the snow had melted slightly, only to harden as evening approached. This was nothing like the fluffy, powdery snow of the morning. So, I returned to the guest house to retrieve my backpack, with a plan to find a place to sleep and start again in the morning.

      Upon my arrival at the guest house, Adnan and his group were in full celebration mode for the New Year—music, dancing, eating... exactly what you'd expect from a group of friends enjoying their time together. The room was filled with the sounds of folklore music and the sight of folklore dancing, a lively scene that warmed the heart. The energetic group dance went on and on, with everyone sweating and smiling.

      Adnan found me a room next to the entrance where I could wait and have a drink. When he returned, he offered me to stay the night. It was too dark and cold to venture out again, and I could sleep on the sofa in the room. The guest house was full, but this was the best he could offer. I accepted right away, quickly concluding that it was much better to stay here than to search for a spot to pitch my tent in the middle of the night.

      I occasionally joined the party, but mostly I wasn't in the mood and didn't want to intrude. So, I mostly kept to myself, even though many guests, breathless and sweaty from dancing, would sit with me for a while to chat about mountains, pilgrimage, and various topics. The room I was in served as a common multi-purpose area, strewn with everyone's coats and bags, and fridges full of drinks. The kitchen was just opposite. Whenever the owner-cook found a moment for himself, he would lie down on the sofa opposite mine and close his eyes. He didn't speak English, so our exchanges were silent but comforting.

     It was a pleasant evening. At one point, Adnan approached me and revealed that they had organized a collection of money for me without my knowledge. He handed me the collected funds to help me in the coming days. I was taken aback and overwhelmed by such a kind gesture. It was not only unexpected but profoundly helpful.

     Now that I was a little rested and no longer in immediate distress, I began making plans. I would walk down to the town early the next morning, retracing my steps carefully all the way. If I didn’t find the wallet, I planned to rent a hotel room, leave my stuff there, and then try to find a shovel. The next day, I would return to the campsite to move all the snow necessary. I wasn’t ready to consider the wallet lost until I had done that. If it was still missing after that, I would accept defeat. Meanwhile, my French support team (Franz) informed me that nowadays, you can temporarily block a bank card without canceling it. You can make a temporary opposition for a few days and then call to have the card reactivated—great news! I was safe to continue my search.

     I spent a good night sleeping on the sofa. In the morning, I was probably the first awake. I repacked my backpack—I had opened it the previous evening to take a hot shower. Just when I finished, the guest house owner came to me with a platter of coffee and breakfast. That was very welcome. I warmly thanked him and set off.

      Unfortunately, my walk back was fruitless. No sign of the lost wallet. But I harbored a big hope of finding it at the campsite. I had half slipped, half fallen on a small slope there, and it seemed possible, even logical, that this was where I might have torn open my back pocket. Since it was in at least one meter of fresh, powdery snow, the wallet could easily have fallen there without my noticing and might be buried under the snow.

     Undeterred and as planned, once I reached town, I headed straight for a hotel, the cheapest possible. After searching unsuccessfully for a shovel elsewhere, I returned to the hotel, hoping they might have one. Fortunately, they did have a shovel and allowed me to take it up to the mountains the next day. Filled with renewed hope, I prepared for another attempt to retrieve my lost wallet.

     It was too late to venture out, so I decided to take advantage of the comfortable room to perform some maintenance on my tent. I also harbored a faint, tiny hope that perhaps the wallet had fallen out in the tent while I was dressing or undressing and that I might have overlooked it when packing. It was highly improbable, but hope tends to cling to such possibilities. After unpacking and thoroughly searching the tent—and not finding the wallet—I spent almost four hours stitching and reinforcing parts that needed attention.

    I went to bed satisfied and woke up early, fresh and ready to face the day. It was a cloudy morning. After breakfast, I picked up the shovel and started my trek back to the campsite, 12 km uphill. I tried hitchhiking, but it wasn’t easy for cars to stop for a big guy carrying a shovel. So, I walked while trying to hitch a ride, turning around whenever I heard a car. About 3 km into my journey, it started to snow. Normally, I would have my backpack with me, which not only keeps my back warm but also carries an umbrella protected in a PVC pipe. Regrettably, I hadn’t thought to bring the umbrella, so I was at the mercy of the elements.

      I had walked nearly all the way up, close to the guest house, when a car finally stopped. A fellow Muslim brother. He drove me the remaining 2 km to the campsite and, as he was early for a prayer meeting in a small mosque in the forest, he offered to help me dig.

     For an hour, we dug together, taking turns with the shovel. His kindness was invaluable. After he left, I continued alone for three more hours, shoveling snow and breaking it down with small jabs of the shovel to ensure the wallet wasn’t hidden in a big chunk. The snow was formidable; 20 cm had fallen since the start of my search. Now, my woolen hat (I had brought only my woolen hat for the cold, not my walking hat), my jacket, trousers, shoes, and socks were getting really wet. In such conditions, things deteriorate quickly. Snow sticks to the wet fabric, melts, and wets it further, causing more snow to stick and soak through until you're completely wet and chilled.

      Despite the worsening weather, the intense physical effort kept me from feeling cold. I felt the chill of the wet clothes, but I wasn't cold—yet.

     I had cleared all the area where I thought my wallet could be. I don't know exactly how many cubic meters of snow I shoveled, but I wouldn’t be surprised if it was around eight. Finding nothing, I had to finally accept that I had truly lost my wallet. Without further delay, I grabbed the shovel and headed downhill.

    The snow on the road was thick, with visibility limited to about 10 meters. No cars were passing, so I took out my bike flashing lights to signal my presence should any vehicle approach. The first to appear wasn’t a car but a snowplow, followed by a long line of cars taking advantage of the freshly cleared path. They were all heading uphill, while I was going down. After walking about 1 km, cars began to descend. The snow was already 12 to 15 cm deep, and the cars moved slowly. I stopped to help some tourists from the Netherlands fit chains to their van, then continued walking. About 1 km later, a car stopped and gave me a ride down. I was thoroughly wet and starting to feel cold, something that rarely happens to me.

      Upon reaching the town center, I still had another kilometer and a half to walk to my hotel. By then, I could truly feel the cold biting. Once at the hotel, all I wanted was a steaming hot shower, but to my dismay, there was no hot water left. Exhausted, I went straight to bed, trembling and shivering, trying to warm up, which I eventually did. Unfortunately, by the next morning, I had developed a significant fever—it had been a long time since I last had one like this. Now, three days later, the fever is subsiding, and I'll be on my way tomorrow.

      So there you have it, my New Year's adventures. All is well now. I lost some money, spent more than usual, and expended a lot of time and energy, only to end up right back where I was 12 days ago had I not taken the mountain path. But that's life, and I still have a big smile on my face.

     I walked fine yesterday, and I'm back in my tent now, eager to finish this news update. It's snowing lightly... I'll try to send this today, or tomorrow at the latest.

     I wish you a beautiful New Year. But most of all, I wish you happiness and well-being, no matter what.

     See you soon,

     Emmanuel.

     P.S.: The promised blog will soon be available. There were some delays, but my Polish support team tells me we're nearly there... I'll keep you updated on that too.
