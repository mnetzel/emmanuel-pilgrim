Pilgrim's Winter Trial

31/1/2024

0 Comments

 
      Attention, long (very) message:

   The plan is simple: I draw an almost straight line on the map to cross a mountain range. Alternatively, I could walk along a major road (national type) with unpleasant and dangerous traffic (many trucks). The choice is quickly made, especially since the straight line is significantly shorter.

     There are approximately 25 km of small country roads, 45 km of mountain trails (mostly forest paths), followed by paths leading to a pass at about 2,000 meters altitude, then more paths and forest trails until I arrive on the other side—about 85 km in total.

    The road option isn’t really an alternative. Sure, walking on a clear road with flat, tarmac ground, easy slopes (if there are any), and frequent villages for food supplies, electric charging points for my phone and power banks sounds convenient. But these advantages don’t outweigh the noise of vehicles passing by at 90 km/h, sometimes brushing too close, or the smell of exhaust fumes—especially from heavily loaded trucks crawling at walking pace uphill. Worst of all is the burnt tire smell, which gives me the awful feeling I’m inhaling micro-particles of the tire materials. Did you know tire pollution is ten times greater than exhaust emissions?

    In short, I prefer the mountains. And in this case, the difference in distance is more than double.

    So, I head toward the small mountain range from the charming little town of Vatra Dornei. From what I can see on the map, the ascent is gentle, with a steeper climb only at the pass. There also appear to be water sources along my planned route, which is great because it means I won’t need to carry much water.

     Instead, I’ll just carry the provisions I bought yesterday from the small grocery store in the last village before the massif. I hope the springs aren’t frozen. It’s starting to get cold, and temperatures remain well below zero during the day, even when it’s sunny. For a spring to avoid freezing, it either needs a significant flow rate or natural or man-made protection. Still, the water that flows from a spring usually maintains a constant temperature since it comes from underground.

     In summer, it emerges very cool—between 8 and 12 degrees, depending on the depth of the water table that feeds it. In winter, it feels warm, even though it’s the same temperature. At worst, I’ll melt snow if I don’t have access to liquid water.

     I spent the night in a guesthouse at the end of the village of Gura Haitii. My batteries are fully charged, my clothes and shoes are dry, my route is marked on the topographical map on my phone, I have the landmarks identified, the pre-determined stages planned, and my backpack is packed and well-organized. The cold-weather gear is easily accessible, and my tent is dry and neatly folded. Everything is ready.

     I finish sipping my morning coffee in the guesthouse dining room. The owner comes to ask me about my plans. Last night, he told me that two bodies were found last spring—tourists caught in an avalanche on the path I’m about to take. It’s his way of warning me about the risks.

     But I am no tourist—I am a pilgrim.

     There are always risks. The mountain is an unforgiving place. It’s important to be aware and to protect yourself, but there are no guarantees. Nothing is ever 100% safe. For me, that’s what makes the experience so valuable. The mountain doesn’t pretend, and that forces a certain humility. Approaching it with arrogance or overconfidence is dangerous, especially in winter. The mountain dominates—in every sense of the word. (See what I did there?)

     So, I studied the map carefully to identify the most dangerous sections. I believe the risks on my planned route are limited to a few specific portions. Another path would be much riskier, but it leads southeast, and I’m heading southwest.

     So, I leave.

     The first day is really pleasant. I walk along a motorable path used by logging vehicles. In fact, at least one vehicle passed recently, leaving tire tracks in the snow. The tracks are covered with 20 cm of powdery snow, but it’s far better than the 40–50 cm of untouched snow on the ground between and around them. Progress is easier—or at least less difficult. But it doesn’t last.

     After a few kilometers, I see that the tracks end. The snow was too deep for the vehicle, whose underbody left a large imprint where it turned around. Now, I’m walking on a path with no tracks, through powder snow that’s a good 50–60 cm thick.

     Of course, it’s harder. It’s slower. It’s tedious. I tire quickly. After only about 2 km, I decide to pitch my tent at the first suitable spot.

     The advantage of this place is that it’s quiet and deserted. I can move to the side of the path without issue—once I find a flat, open surface large enough for my tent.

     After a few dozen meters, under some fir trees on the slope to the right, a spot reveals itself. It feels natural, like the place chose me, not the other way around.

     I like it when that happens.

    There is a stream below, maybe 5 meters away. I place my bag against a pine log lying nearby. I inspect the spot to determine the best way to arrange my tent. If there’s a slight slope, it’s better to position your head at the highest point for a more comfortable sleep. If there’s a bump, I flatten it as much as possible. Any uncomfortable objects—pine cones, thorny brambles, branches—must be moved away.

     Clearing the snow comes first. Under the fir trees, it’s about 45 cm deep—a real task. I need to clear an area large enough not only for the tent but also for space to move around while planting the stakes (sardines in French). It takes time and a lot of energy. I must hurry to avoid getting too cold. The sun is setting, with maybe 40 minutes of light left (sunset is around 4:40 p.m., and it gets dark by 5:20 p.m. Romanian time). That’s enough time, but the temperature is dropping quickly. With my backpack now on the ground, it no longer shields my back from the cold. I need to work fast—both to warm up from the effort and to get “inside” as soon as possible.

     Finally, the ground appears. I deploy the tent, starting with the groundsheet. Then the tent itself, inserting the aluminum poles—not telescopic but nestable—with an elastic cord inside to simplify assembly. I slide the poles into the fabric housings designed for them. The tent stands, though still frail. Finally, I secure the flysheet over it.

     I anchor everything with aluminum pegs (sardines). Since it will freeze hard overnight, I plant the stakes carefully, shaking them in all directions once they’re in place to leave them free in the holes I’ve created. They must be angled opposite the direction of tension to avoid pulling out in gusts of wind. However, it’s crucial that they remain free; if buried completely, frost will lock them into the ground by morning.

    I’ve learned this the hard way. On a few occasions, either due to forgetting or poor weather predictions, I’ve made that mistake. Once, I had to go to a farm at the end of the road to borrow a crowbar. Even with the crowbar, it took over an hour of grueling effort to free the eleven pegs holding my tent. The ground was rock-solid, frozen to a depth of 10 cm. The crowbar, icy cold from minus 20 degrees Celsius temperatures, made my hands ache even with gloves. I had to take frequent breaks to warm my hands in my pockets when the pain became unbearable.

     I don’t let that happen anymore.

   Finally, the tent is pitched, the straps tightened, and the daily ritual ends as I sink inside, completely exhausted.

     Now the inner ritual begins. Everything has its place, precise and dictated by the experience of the last three years. Each object comes out of the backpack one by one, following the optimized order of storage. I lay out and open the sleeping bag, then finish undressing. The dry clothes stored just below the sleeping bag go into the backpack. Even if the clothes I’m wearing aren’t wet, they are damp from sweat, and they will dry quickly in the dry cold since they are still warm. Staying completely dry is crucial because moisture drains body heat. To warm up properly, it’s much better to be entirely dry.

     The temperature is around minus 12°C, maybe even minus 15°C. Before doing anything else, I must warm up. It’s only when I’m warm at the bottom of my sleeping bag that I can start preparing something to eat. The sleeping bag, no matter how good, doesn’t generate heat—it only slows down the dissipation of body heat. I am the radiator. So, I radiate as best I can...

     I’m utterly exhausted and start to doze off.

     Suddenly, I jolt awake. I must have fallen asleep—a significant mistake, as I didn’t finish my setup ritual. It’s completely dark now, after 8 p.m., and the temperature has dropped further, likely to -15°C or even -18°C. The tent ceiling sparkles like a mountain sky on a clear night. The humidity trapped inside has condensed into frost on the icy walls and ceiling. It’s beautiful when I turn on my headlamp.

    I finish setting up. Unfortunately, the shoes are already completely frozen—hard as wood. I know I won’t be able to put them on tomorrow morning, even if I try to force them on while hopping on one foot. There’s even a risk my foot could get stuck in them—frozen shoes are like cement blocks.

     The pre-moistened wipes are also frozen solid, just a single block of ice. No cleaning up tonight. I put the wipes into my sleeping bag to thaw for tomorrow morning. When you can’t wash with hot water and soap daily, it’s crucial to at least use wipes, especially after using the toilet. Luckily, I’m a morning person—that works in my favor.

     The portable batteries and the telephone also go into the backpack. Toothpaste as well, if I want to brush my teeth with it. The water bottle, too. It’s funny because the bottle of sparkling water I have with me wasn’t actually frozen—very surprising, while the water in my other bottle was completely solid. Does sparkling water freeze at a different temperature than still water? I was very pleased with the discovery. But as I opened the bottle to take a drink or two, the escaping gas hissed, and within seconds, all the water inside froze solid before my eyes. I assure you, it took only a few seconds.

    You have to remember, I tell myself, that the decompression of gas produces cold—that’s the very principle of refrigeration. That sudden drop in temperature was the last straw. The experience was amusing, but now the bottle had to sleep with me if I wanted to drink from it later.

     The shoes also had to come into the sleeping bag, stored in a plastic bag to avoid dirtying or wetting the interior. But now that they are entirely frozen, I tell myself it’s a little late. I’ve decided to stay here tomorrow to dry them in the sun. If my estimates are correct, the sun should reach my tent late in the morning and stay until mid-afternoon. This should be enough, if not to dry them completely, at least to melt and soften them. I’ll then place them inside the sleeping bag to ensure I can put them on the following morning.

     For now, I’m going to spend the night without these unpleasant sleeping companions, which, I assure you, give the impression of taking up all the space in the sleeping bag.

     Everything I’ve placed in the sleeping bag is like an ice cube that I’ll have to warm up and melt. You can understand why it’s essential to get very warm before attempting this.

     At these temperatures, I sleep with two pairs of socks, thermal tights, a long-sleeved thermal t-shirt, a second cotton t-shirt, a long-sleeved fleece jacket, and a thick hat.

     I also place a fleece around my feet on top of the sleeping bag, along with a thin sleeveless down jacket. My sleeping bag has lost a lot of its insulation over the years. I’ve used it intensively for over fifteen years. Additionally, my feet often touch the bottom of the tent. This area is made of mesh canvas to allow interior ventilation, making it very cold and prone to condensation. Ice forms there and clings to the end of my sleeping bag whenever it touches.

    Because my sleeping bag is "heated" by my body, the ice melts and gradually soaks the fabric, then the down. Perhaps you didn’t know, but wet down loses all its insulating properties. While it’s possible to sleep with cold feet, it’s much better to sleep with warm ones! By covering the sleeping bag with a fleece and down jacket, it’s the outer layers that get wet instead of the bag itself. I sleep much better this way.

     However, in the morning, I have to put on damp or wet clothes—often frozen and stiff. If it’s really cold, by the time I finish the morning tidying-up ritual, they’ve dried somewhat, and the tent’s ceiling drips with the humidity released during the night.

     Finally, I eat something: bread that has become very hard and cold, cheese that I break more than I cut, and two frozen tomatoes.

     Quickly, my arms outside the sleeping bag feel the sharp sting of the cold, so I pull them back under. I lie still. I fall asleep immediately. It must be 9 p.m. It’s very late for me—normally, I sleep around 7 p.m. But I’ve already completed one cycle. I’ll manage to catch up tomorrow morning.

     Everything is fine.

    I am awakened in the morning by the dull, slow sound of a heavy vehicle. A snowplow bulldozer is followed by a forestry machine for handling and transporting wooden logs. I did well to move out of the way. I hadn’t expected this, but it’s a pleasant surprise. Tomorrow, I’ll walk on a path with only a day’s worth of snow! I wonder how many kilometers these machines will clear.

     Two hours later, the vehicles return in the opposite direction. The first one honks. The second stops. I poke my head out of the tent, a big smile on my face, and wave hello. The response from the two passengers is very enthusiastic. One of them asks, with the window down, what I’m doing there. It’s a classic question, and I respond with the usual: French/Pilgrim/Not understanding/English? One of them speaks English. He asks me if I’m going up or down.

     I tell him I’m going up, aiming to cross the pass tomorrow or the day after, depending on whether I stop at the mountain refuge cabin near the pass. In Romania’s mountains, there are many mountain shelters: small wooden cabins with just a plank bunk bed, a window, and a bear-proof armored door, always left open to welcome travelers.

     He looks at me, and I can tell he’s assessing my credibility. The weather has worsened; heavy snow is falling. The silence in our conversation lingers a little longer than socially comfortable. He must be wondering whether I’m completely crazy, reckless, or superhuman. But then again, he’s meeting me just after I’ve spent a night here in minus 20°C. I’m clearly doing fine—smiling and everything. I must seem like I know what I’m doing. He suddenly wishes me good luck, gives me a thumbs-up, and drives off.

     The box of vegetable pâté is frozen solid, its contents iced. I slice it as thinly as possible to place on the sliced bread. Spreading it is impossible, even though the bread is hard enough to handle strong pressure. (This is cold bread.) The orange is frosty too.

     I quickly tuck my arms back under the sleeping bag and shiver for a moment. Getting out of the bag to say hello was enough to lose the accumulated warmth, but the sun will soon help.

    I wait until I’m warm again to place my shoes where they can best absorb the life-saving rays that will soon hit the entrance and side of the tent. This is an important detail to consider when setting up the tent: in winter, you seek the sun; in summer, you avoid it.

    The advantage of the tent, especially in a dark color, is that as soon as the sun hits it, the interior temperature rises immediately. Right now, this is a welcome benefit.

     So and very quickly it gets above zero inside. The entire canvas is dripping with condensation. I then open the entrance so that everything evaporates outside. That's less humidity inside. As it is always cold (you know the thing between real temperature and felt temperature...), drying is quick. In a few minutes, the condensation disappears and the tent fabric is dry.

     My shoes defrost quickly, and as soon as they are soft enough, I take off my socks so as not to wet them, immerse my warm feet in the very cold and still a little hard shoes, and leave the tent for the mandatory inspection tour.

     1: Remove the snow from the tent. Although I do it regularly from the inside by tapping and shaking the canvas, there is about ten cm on the top.
      2: Clear the snow around the tent so that the space between the flysheet and the ground is clear and air can circulate there. In addition to breathing, which is important in life, this also prevents too much snow from melting on the bottom of the flysheet and refreezing during the night, which makes it much more difficult to fold the tent in the morning because ice packs remain attached to the canvas.
    3: Loosen the sardines so that they come loose and move in their housings. Some are stuck by ice. They have to be forced. If it doesn't move, light kicks so as not to break the frozen metal, but strong enough to break the ice bond with the ground. Everything is in the right balance... Too strong and you cry about the breakage of a sardine. Too weak and you cry in the morning not being able to extract it.
     4: Finally, tighten the tie-downs, which soften a few times during the night.

    I finally return after a toilet detour. I then clean myself with the wipes that remained in the duvet, which is still a little warm.

    After this busy morning, a nap is called for, and I indulge in the gentle warmth that now reigns in the tent. About three hours later, I wake up feeling perky. The temperature has dropped, the sun no longer shines. It must be minus ten.

     I bring the shoes inside. They are almost dry; this can be seen in the color of the leather. I close the entrance. I surround the shoes with the long canvas bag that holds the tent, the groundsheet, and the inflatable mattress in the morning, to protect them a little from the frost. We put straw at the foot of the fruit or rose bushes so that it does not freeze.

     Because there are two forms of frozen shoes. The first is that they were soaked. It then freezes at -10C. The second is when the temperature drops to -20 and beyond. There the shoes freeze even if they were dry. Because in this state that we commonly call "dry," the leather retains a certain percentage of humidity, very low certainly, but sufficient to become very hard at -20C. By surrounding the shoes with thick canvas, as well as blocking the entrance to the shoe with a large sock rolled into a ball, I reduce the risk of freezing.

     The temperature continues to drop. The sun will set soon. There is something that I sense more and more. It's a little complicated to explain because it comes from feeling. I can tolerate the cold when it's there. -10C, -25C, that's no problem. I even experienced nights in -35C and once -43C in the Himalayas. On the other hand, the drop in temperature is painful. It's strange.

     To make an explanatory comparison, I would say that it is like speed.
    We tolerate being at a high speed very easily; however, acceleration is difficult for the body. Well, I feel a bit the same thing with the temperature. Acceleration (deceleration in this case) of temperature is significantly more painful than remaining at a constant temperature at the lowest. I am not sure whether I am clear in what I am expressing, nor whether this is a reality other than totally subjective. But the impression is strong...

     As I think about this and write it on my phone, I feel the bite of the cold. Fingers go numb. It stings... and suddenly the phone goes off. Too cold for him. I tuck it under the sleeping bag, and there, wrapped up in the sleeping bag, I notice that my breath has condensed on the sleeping bag right in front of my nose. There is now a frozen patch there, all hard and shiny. This is a sign that it must be in the -15C to -20C range.

    I fall asleep like a baby and stay with Morpheus until morning. It was cold, but I was warm. Everything is fine. The fleece around the bottom of my sleeping bag is hard. The bottom of my pants, which folded forms part of my pillow, too. It was really cold.

   I also realize this when I try to get out of the tent once all the morning rituals have been completed. The tent zipper is frozen and really hard to open. That of the double roof impossible. I have to heat it with a gas lighter, which itself has to be heated in my pocket for a few minutes for it to work. By passing the flame gently along the closure, gently, the ice on the other side melts and after about ten minutes I am able to open it. What happens is that the heat from inside the tent, coming from my body, escapes to the outside of course, but like everything else, it uses the path of least resistance to do so. The zipper is one of them. And therefore becomes a hot spot compared to the rest of the flysheet. So when the snow falls, the snow that arrives there tends to melt first, then to be covered with fresh snow which also begins to melt and so on until an equilibrium is achieved. What is melted ends up freezing and thus forms a more or less thick film of ice which completely blocks the zipper. I really wish the tent manufacturer had thought of making a small outer flap covering the zipper to prevent this. Maybe I'll send them the suggestion...

     When I finally manage to go out, I realize that it had snowed during the night. Approximately 20 cm. And it's still snowing. It's snowing well; I can't see 50 m away...

     I take out my backpack, the inflatable mattress, and begin the ritual of folding the tent.

   First, I clear the snow accumulated on and around it. I then extract the sardines which are obviously frozen and stuck in their holes. Fortunately, I planned this the day before because I would never have been able to extract them without it. A few calculated bumps and kicks later and they are removed and put away in their pouch.

     The tent is technically free now, but it's stuck to the ground. During my stay here, the snow and frozen ground I was standing on melted and then refroze, especially during the half hour I was already outside. So now I have to “peel” the tent from the groundsheet and the groundsheet from the ground. Slowly of course because as everything is frozen everything is fragile. When I can finally move the tent and groundsheet freely, it's time to dismantle the poles that give the tent its shape.

     First of all, the eyecups in which the end of the hoops are placed are a pile of snow, ice and plant debris that must be broken before the whole thing can be disassembled. I have to take a break to warm up my hands. Too cold. They are all numb and my fingers melt badly. I place them in my pockets, near the femoral artery which provides a lot of heat. I walk back and forth to the side of the tent, on the path which was cleared yesterday but which is already well covered with powder so as not to cool down too much.

     After a few minutes I find the digital functionality and only then can I really start dismantling the tent. The arches released from the eyecups do not move. They are stuck to the fabric through which they pass. These were humidified by condensation and as it constitutes a really cold point (aluminum and its derivatives are excellent thermal conductors...) they froze and the condensation stuck. You have to manipulate them cm by cm to break the ice and free them.

     Hand warming break...

     So I extract them from their housing and place them on the ground. I roll the flysheet into a ball and crush it all over to break up as much of the ice that is still attached to it as possible. Then I shake it to get as much out as possible.
     
     Hand warming break.
     
     I start the exercise again. The problem with ice sticking to fabric is twofold. The weight of the tent will already double because of this, but also its volume. So once rolled it is impossible to fit into its protective bag. I'm used to this and it makes sense to me. But it should not be too important either because then the fixing straps which hold it under the backpack will be too short to hold the whole thing together. That's really problematic...

     Complete warming break...

    This is because I use my thin gloves to fold the tent. Large gloves do not allow the necessary manipulations to be carried out. So the cooling comes quickly, especially since the rest of the body is rather static. I now take care of folding the tent in half lengthwise on the ground sheet from which I took care to remove the frozen plant debris on the ground side, and those appeared by my activity on the other side... I then place the flysheet on top, folded in half along its length too.

      Hand warming break.

     A few bends to warm up the thighs. The shoes are really cold and I feel it more and more. My toes are numb. I need to hurry up and start walking to generate calories and get warm blood flowing everywhere.

     Now the aluminum hoops. Impossible to unsocket them. They are frozen too. I have to lift them to my mouth and blow hot air while whispering a Haaaa on each of the sockets until they melt and release. It takes three soft, slow blows on each. There are 8 on the two longest hoops and 6 on the transverse. I have to be careful not to bring the cold metal too close to my lips. Otherwise, it sticks straight away and if the slightest movement is made, the lips bleed. This has happened to me several times, I'm careful now...
​
     I have to take a hand warming break every time. The frozen aluminum sucks the heat from my fingers really quickly, even with gloves on...

     When finally folded and stored in their pouch, I place them on top of all the folded fabrics on the ground. I kneel on the storage bag of the whole thing so that my knees don't freeze too much, and roll the whole thing up, using as much force as possible to reduce the diameter of the whole thing. When finished, and after a hand warming break, I roll everything into the inflatable mattress.

    It's so cold that the moisture inside it has frozen and created hard weights. It should be understood that the mattress, although self-inflating, requires a few breaths to be adjusted before lying on it. Each breath brings water vapor and after more than a year of daily use it is no longer humidity that is inside. It's water.

    When everything is rolled, it forms a cylindrical package of a little over sixty cm with a diameter of around twenty. I then place a piece of thick plastic found on the side of the road on top and tie the whole thing together with three small straps.

     Global warming break.

     The thick plastic is protection. As the package is placed and fixed under the backpack (lowering of the center of gravity...), each time I sit down or put the backpack down, it is the one that finds itself compressed between the ground and the backpack. The 4mm thick plastic prevents any thorns, brambles, spikes and others from perforating the mattress surrounding the tent. It happened to me twice, I don't get caught anymore either...

    In the end, packing up the tent takes me 1 hour 40 minutes instead of ten minutes in hot weather. In addition to being tiring, I lose a lot of heat. Not really time to breathe, you have to put on your backpack and start walking to warm up... Your feet are freezing. Fingers too...

    Since the vehicles passed, 35 cm of snow has fallen... It remains passable even if tiring. However, I am of course moving forward on this path. Fortunately because I don't have much walking time. It's already 10:30 a.m. and I have to plan to stop at 4:30 p.m. maximum to be able to set up the day tent. It's really risky to ride it at night. Loss of materials, location difficult to read which means bumps or holes in the ground which make lying down uncomfortable, but above all excessive cooling during assembly and installation...

     That leaves about 6 useful hours. Useful for walking first, but also for making a fire and melting snow to drink if I don't find liquid water on my way. I'm not really worried about that, the stream next door is frozen on its banks but not in its center. Around fifty cm let the current appear. I could always throw a water bottle attached to my string to collect the liquid.

     So I walk. The snow is falling. She falls hard. No wind is good. That's one less thing to have to deal with. 1 km... 2 km...

     The snow now stays on the top of my shoes... It stays there, melts and wets them. I anticipate that tomorrow will be difficult for this. This will be the stage where she will be wet and so will my socks. This is not cool because this will also be the time I plan to go over the pass. The pass being the least hospitable place during the crossing of the massif. I need to find a solution to dry my shoes properly tonight. I need to make a fire... 3 km... I'm already tired. I've been walking for 3 hours, it's rising slowly, 12% I think... 40 cm of snow now... And then the path stops. A sort of clearing, the gurgling stream on the left, a cabin on the right. This doesn't really seem to be a mountain lodge, but rather a forester's shelter. This is where the vehicles from the day before stopped. I can see in the falling snow a bridge over the stream. A very simple bridge, two tree trunks placed there, a vague guardrail on one side. Sixty cm of snow overall... This must be the start of the path that leads to the pass. I know where I am on the map, even if the cabin is not shown. It's 2 p.m....

   The door to the wooden hut is open.... I consider this as authorization and enter for a comfortable break outside the snow, with the possibility of placing my bag high up on a table (easier to get back on my back after ...), A dry bench to sit on, and a door to be inside rather than outside. Luxury...

     The cabin is classic. On one side a floor is 1 m high which serves as a collective bed and fills a third of the cabin. Above are three mattresses whose color, numerous stains and general appearance are revolting. A most rustic table made of thick, solid plank in front of it, just opposite the front door. And then the remaining third divided into a bed space again and a wood stove space. The floor is "clean" and has been swept recently. The broom is on the side of the front door, on the stove side. Behind the stove a pile of split wood. The stove is a rectangular parallelepiped of thick sheet metal, with a supply mouth on one side and the smoke evacuation tube on the other.

     It's almost 3 p.m. I could go on, but I find it extremely appropriate to spend the night here. A small fire will dry my shoes along with the room. The stream outside will provide me with water that I can heat at the same time. It's worth staying rather than pushing for an hour or so and setting up the tent. I don't hesitate for long.

    The wood is dry, and after rubbing two pieces against each other, smoke appears... No, I'm kidding. I bought fire starter blocks in the village below, on the good advice of a local. This allows you to be sure to light your fire effortlessly and quickly even in difficult conditions. Until now, when necessary, I used the hydroalcoholic gel which has become so common since COVID. It works well. But fire starters work even better. While the fire is burning, I go to get water from the stream. 3 liters should do it. One liter that I will drink straight away, the other two in my metal flasks to boil. One has been saved for tomorrow, the other will be for the evening and night. Both will serve as a hot water bottle while waiting, placed in the sleeping bag, the dream!

     The fire now gives its heat. It's nice. I now place my two water bottles and my metal mug on the hot stove. Very surprised, as I filled the latter with water from the stream. This turns into a thick, translucent slurry in a few moments. The water in the stream is less than zero degrees Celsius. It remains liquid because of the movement. Now that I have placed it in a container, a very cold metal container, it freezes in place within a few seconds. I really like observing the phenomenon... Like with the bottle of sparkling water... Quickly the heat from the stove warms everything up and soon the water in my cup is steaming. I drink it. How good is hot water! It immediately spreads everywhere. It feels good. In addition, my fingers fit the shape of the mug, sticking to it as best as possible as if to suck all the heat from it. It's good. I watch the snow fall outside. It doesn't weaken. I don't care, tonight I'm warm. I still hope that the sun will return and the snow will stop falling.

     I removed the large mattress from the bed area near the stove. I put my sleeping bag on the boards. And then, seeing the back of the mattress now vertical, I told myself that it wasn't much worse than sleeping on the ground as I often did. So I put the now turned mattress back down and place my sleeping bag on top.

      Then I braced my pilgrim's staff between the two walls of the corner of the wall above the stove in order to hang my shoes there without them being too close to it because leather doesn't like to heat up. I place everything that needs to be there to dry, the fleece and the sleeveless down jacket, the socks, the gloves...

     I'm eating now. The vegetable pâté is thawed, the orange almost. I place its bark peels in the stove to enjoy the scent.

      I finally lie down. I look at the ceiling. I am well.

     After a few moments savored like this, I take out the phone to study the map. I am 2.3 km from the campsite before the pass, 6.7 km from the mountain refuge beyond the pass. I won't be able to go to the shelter tomorrow. For sure. But I could make it to the camp site. 2 and a half km with an ascent of 350 meters in altitude is reasonably doable I think. Especially since I won't have to fold the tent in the morning, that means I could start walking early, around 7:30 or 8 a.m....

     One night on the site and then a little less than 4 and a half km to the refuge, with barely 250m of ascent. It's doable I think. This is the part where I should be careful not to trigger an avalanche. It's playable.

     I go to sleep feeling confident. I sleep well. How good is it to sleep well...

     In the morning I look outside just after stretching. It's still snowing... It's been three days non-stop. This does not bode well for crossing the pass. But I remain confident.

     I eat quickly. It's very cold in the cabin, I want to make a fire, but that would be excessive. And I don't want to use the wood that's there, I already felt a little guilty for using a few pieces last night...

    I set off quickly, and as planned, as soon as my bag is put away. All my stuff is dry. Awesome. I will approach the mountain in good conditions. I will go slowly. My plans are relevant and come together well as they say. I come out confident and determined.

     The exit is tough. Even though I already felt like it was cold in there. Outside is much worse. A little side wind, even if light, forces the snow to fall towards my face, under the hat. It whips around a bit. It cooled down a lot. Fortunately I spent a warm night. My shoes are comfortable. I am about to cross the small bridge over the stream.

    Two logs of wood, two trees in fact, placed there above the ice, approach each other with caution. Especially since they are covered in 60cm of snow. The snow is firm and crusty. I prefer to kick it free to cross by walking on the wood of the logs rather than perching higher on unstable snow. The prospect of icy water below is really not reassuring. Especially with a 30kg backpack... It takes me more than 10 minutes to cross the bridge and I'm already very tired just then. I still start walking on what I think is the path. There is a good 60 or 70 cm of snow. This doesn't help much in distinguishing which route to follow. The stick is very useful there, I test the waters under the snow with it, in front of me, whenever I have doubts. I check my phone to check that I am not straying too far from the marked path as soon as the clues that the configuration of the place, the vegetation, the topography, become too vague to be certain of the passage.

     Suffice to say that progress is slow. Very slow. And painful, really painful.

    I take a step, pause, a second, pause. Test the waters. One step closer. Break. One step again, pause. And I'm out of breath. It's hard. But in this way I advance to the point of arriving at a somewhat clear place. By clear you can also mean exposed. I expect the snow to be even deeper there.

    I am very surprised to realize that no. In fact it is even slightly shallower. 50cm I would say. Well I think.

     I'm still moving forward a little.

     And then a little more. I'm already tired. I haven't walked for a long time though. I haven't yet done a quarter of my planned stage. I'm even starting to wonder if I'll be able to do these 2.4km...

     I'm still moving forward a little.

     Stop me.

     A few more steps...

     Break.

     And I take one more step. There, my foot suddenly sinks much deeper. There is a lot more snow than my last few steps suggested. There are layers. Powder snow, an icy layer, another layer of snow and an icy crust again.

    In fact, the snow that falls is powdery because it's really cold. And then during the day, the sun warms its top a little, transforming the crystals. They grow and clump together. And then when night comes, it freezes while the snow continues to fall and covers it, powdery again, like flour.

     I have just gone through layers and am now down to mid-thigh. Obviously unbalanced, my bag carries me sideways and forward, and I fall. Well, half, because the snow is there. Getting back on my feet uses a lot, a lot of energy. And I can't take a break now; I have to dust the snow off my clothes as best I can so as not to get wet. I'm sweaty, and it's not good to be sweaty in -15C. I'm out of breath. Exhausted.

     The prospect of having to open a route alone is painful in itself. There is no place for me to sit and take stock. I am buried in almost 1m of snow, with one or two glazed crusts in mille-feuille. The degree of freedom of movement of my leg in the hole formed by my feet is the size of the flat of the foot. The crust of icy snow that I crossed on the way prevents further movement. The powdery snow enters the depression and then closes in on me. She's finished getting in my way.

     I change my mindset. I am no longer making progress; I am crossing over. The difference is important. The 2 km that remain to go disappear in front of me. When you open a roped trail, the first person advances like an icebreaker in the pack ice. After a few dozen meters at most, he stands aside and lets the second go ahead as well as the rest of the group. He finds himself last to advance on an open trail, that is to say, on a path whose snow has been trampled by the entire party. He “rests” like this, overtaking the next person, then the next until he finds himself in first position again.

     There I am alone. I open, stomp, and a hundred meters take me an hour. An hour at the end of which I am in a state of exhaustion which is unsuspected to anyone who has not experienced it. The snow holds you, sucks you in, grabs you. The pauses between each step become longer. I spend more time stopped than moving forward. Twice as long. Three times as long. I place my bag in the snow and sit on it every 15 meters, already dreading the effort it will take to put it back on my back.

     I'm already telling myself that I'm going to stop there for today. It's barely 9am! I can not stand it anymore.

     10 meters more.

     10 again.

     Yes, I could stop there. But that means my steps will be really, really short. Instead of three days to cross the pass, it would then take 8 days. I only have 2 days of food. 4 if I ration.

     10 meters again. The next step seems impossible to me. I put the bag down.

     Even if I decide to stop now, I need to find a place to pitch the tent. Nothing around me lends itself to it. And nothing seems to lend itself to it as far as the eye can see. Well, at the same time, my visibility is really limited. The snow has increased. I can see 30 meters away, then I can see shadows, no more. If I give it everything I have, I think I can do another 300 meters. Maybe 400. My phone annoys me, the cold just made it turn off unexpectedly. I still had time to check that there weren't really any places suitable for camping before the planned two km... Even the light wind bit me. I have to move. My sweat must not freeze. The prospect of moving forward is hard, and to think that if I ever manage to find a location, and I assure you that I will not be difficult in my choice, I will have to have retained enough energy to clear with the feet 3 cubic meters of snow before you can set up the tent.

     Doubt grips me. Helped by the evidence that the snow will get higher and higher as I climb, the fact that the avalanche risks will also become much greater if it takes me 3 days to reach the pass, what to cross the pass on an empty stomach will certainly not help, as my clothes and shoes will already be wet tomorrow, the question suddenly appears with clarity to my cold mind: but what the hell am I doing here!

     I gather my strength to carry on my shoulders the backpack whose name I find so well chosen at the moment: "expiation"... And with great courage, I decide to turn back. I'm going to go back down to the cabin. I'll see about the rest later, but I really think that going over the pass is too difficult, to the point of being dangerously difficult.

     It's hard to go back. But inevitability helps with choice.

     I'm going back down. It's normally less hard because it goes downhill, and I walk in a track not yet completely closed by the snow carried by the wind. The further down I go, the more I know I made the right choice. My legs are shaking with the effort. I finally arrive at the cabin. It's 11 a.m. I remain there haggard, sitting on the bench. My phone deigns to come back to life.

    I find it hard to give up. I'm trying to find solutions. Maybe I could stay here today to make walking snowshoes. There are fir trees around; I have some nylon twine which could be sufficient for making both in terms of strength and length. And then I have parachute-type cord which could be used to attach them to my shoes... In addition, I could use the stove to force the shape of the main branches into rings, to bend them without them breaking... how glad I am that my penknife has a saw blade!

   It's doable. Walking on snowshoes is extremely unpleasant (for me), we walk with my snowshoes apart... But we sink in much less. 30cm at most in powder...

     But that doesn't solve the avalanche problem, nor the food problem...

    No, I will have to retrace my steps for 24 km, then take a national road for the distance of 204 km to arrive at the same place where I would have been in 60 km if I had been able to pass these 6.5 km of snow-covered mountain...

    Good. Without regrets, I think I did my best. And then you have to look on the bright side: I didn't die stupidly in the mountain! That’s it already, right?

     And then it’s magnificent all around.

     SO...
