January 2022

Gietrzwald and Olsztyn

2/1/2022 0 Comments

I am walking, walking, walking, and somehow, now we are in winter. Of course, I had a few hints and clues—everything is white. Everything is frozen. Literally, outside or inside (which, to be honest, are very blurry concepts anyway). My food has turned into ice-cream: fruit ice-cream, tomato ice-cream, cheese ice-cream, hummus ice-cream—you get the idea. I can’t even cut a piece of butter; it’s too hard. So I eat frozen bread with frozen camembert (when I can find some)...

Ah yes, I’m back to sleeping with my bottle of water inside the sleeping bag. It’s the only way to have something drinkable by morning. Not much room in there, but that’s okay—she’s not a bad bed companion, except when the cork leaks. (And it’s absolutely necessary to avoid rolling onto it during the night... Otherwise, it’s fine.)

But you know what? It’s beautiful. Really beautiful. It’s so cold that the snow stays perfectly white, even after cars drive over it. Sounds are muffled, and the contrasts are sharper, making it much easier to spot wildlife. I see animals from far away or very close when they’re trying to hide. They stay completely still, totally immobile but highly alert, thinking they’re invisible, just waiting for me to pass. But on the white snow, I can see them clearly. I enjoy that a lot.

Oh, and my backpack feels lighter because I’m wearing almost all my clothes. I know it’s just a trick—after all, I’m still carrying the same weight—but I guess it’s a psychological thing. My backpack doesn’t feel so heavy!

Temperatures have dropped to minus 16°C at night, and now it’s even that cold during the day. I guess we’ll soon hit minus 20°C at night.

I’ll send photos this time, as I’ve managed to take and keep some. Unfortunately, the ones from my last message seem to be lost—or maybe they’re just hiding too well in my phone...

Today is the New Year, I’ve been told. That explains all the fireworks last night that woke me up in my tent in the small woods where I was camping. Well, to be honest, I knew it was coming—first, because it happens roughly every year at the same time! And secondly, someone reminded me of it a few days ago.

I chose to spend the night in the woods because I didn’t feel like being in the town that was the next stop on my journey. I wasn’t inclined toward big noises, excitement, or anything else that comes with New Year’s celebrations in town. I guess I’ve become more interested in quietness and silence. That’s the effect of spending all day in peace and sleeping in forests!

It’s strange, though. Even when I’m invited to stay indoors nowadays (which happens often), I find myself missing the cold. My hosts, out of kindness and worry—or maybe trying to compensate for the nights I’ve spent outside at minus 15°C—always propose to turn up the heating in the room I’m sleeping in, to make it cozy and warm. But I can’t. These days, I sleep best in the cold. Just being inside is enough—no heating required!

Now that you have a sense of the atmosphere of these days, I have to tell you about my journey. Since the last time I wrote, I’ve crossed the border and arrived in Poland. Thanks to the help of a Polish friend—whom I’ve officially declared my topographic planner-in-chief—I’ve joined one of the Jacobs Ways. (For my Indian or Nepalese friends who might not know, Jacobs Ways are part of a network of pilgrimage routes across Europe that lead to Santiago de Compostela in Spain.)

Following these routes is great for many reasons. First, they are usually very well signposted, making navigation easier. Second, they are designed to avoid big roads as much as possible. And third, they are planned by locals to guide you through beautiful places—old villages, historical sites, or cultural gems you might otherwise miss if you don’t know the area or can’t speak the language. (Speaking of which, did I mention that Polish is a nightmare to pronounce? I think I’ve sprained my tongue!)

And here’s the best part: the Camino I’m currently following passes through three places I really wanted to visit.

Święta Lipka, a very famous Baroque-style basilica dedicated to Mary, is located in a rather small village. It has a huge organ, and I tried to record it with my phone. Unfortunately, the phone isn’t the best tool for live recordings, and I’m afraid the result doesn’t do justice to the incredible concert I was lucky enough to stumble upon the day I arrived. Still, you can listen and let me know what you think.

Then there was Gietrzwałd. I have to say, it’s an incredibly beautiful place. I enjoyed my time there immensely.

Initially, I had planned to arrive in Gietrzwałd on Christmas Eve, but that morning, as I woke up in the forest just outside the town, I felt so comfortable, so at peace with the place where I had spent the night, that the idea of entering the town during the hustle and excitement of Christmas Eve made me a little anxious. So, I decided to stay in the woods for another day and night and only arrived in Gietrzwałd on the 25th. It was a wonderful decision. Honestly, if you ever take a walk and don’t know where to go, head there—it’s such a special place to be!

Now, I’m on my way to Licheń, about 350 km ahead. Licheń isn’t on the Jacobs Way, but I just have to leave the path at one point, and it’s only 40 km south.

One of the best parts of this journey has been crossing through beautiful old villages and towns. On the way, I’ve also encountered castles.

One castle I passed was open for visits, and it even had a restaurant inside—double jackpot! When I arrived at the restaurant, I met some very kind people visiting Poland with their Canadian friends. It was a lovely encounter, but by now, I guess you know that all the encounters I’ve had have been wonderful—always, everywhere. It’s crazy how true that is.

They invited me to share their table for lunch and were very curious about my walk. After lunch, we explored the castle together. At first, I was thrilled—walking inside a castle is like living a childhood dream, and I loved it. But then, I was disturbed by what I saw on display. I had expected swords, crossbows, armor, and chain mail—things that stir the imagination of an adventurer. Instead, 90% of the exhibition consisted of torture instruments and machines from the Middle Ages, used by the Inquisition and other authorities of the time.

It was overwhelming, even sickening, to see such an extensive collection. The sheer variety, complexity, and elaborateness of these devices left me feeling deeply unsettled. The contrast between the cruelty on display and the peaceful, meaningful journey I’ve been on for the past 15 months created a dissonance I found hard to bear.

Thankfully, shortly after that, I was warmly welcomed by a community of religious sisters. Their kindness and hospitality quickly helped erase the heavy feeling, restoring my sense of peace.

And then, sometime later, I arrived in the town with the castle where Copernicus lived for many years! That was a much nicer visit!

Yes, I am a pilgrim, not a tourist. I don’t go out of my way to visit many places, but when something is directly on my path, of course, I let the child in me enjoy it. What could be better than a drawbridge, a dungeon, a crenellated guardrail, a trebuchet, a mangonel, a ballista, or a catapult?

Voila. This whole journey feels like a dream I am living. Truly, it seems like my feet barely touch the ground these days. Maybe that’s why my backpack feels so heavy! Did I tell you I gave it a name? "Expiation"—I find it very fitting while making a Christian pilgrimage.

Even when something bad happens, I am so lucky that it often turns into something good. Let me give you another example because I’m not sure it’s easy to imagine how much, how often, and in what ways things just seem to work out.

One day, I was invited by a religious sister I met as I was leaving the church next to her congregation house. She welcomed me to stay the night, which meant I had a bed, but most importantly—a bathtub. A hot bath. I hadn’t taken a full hot bath in over three years! (I see you raising an eyebrow: yes, three years without a bath, but I take showers... sometimes.) But a bath! You know what I mean—being fully submerged in steaming hot water, getting clean, sure, but more than that, truly relaxing... until, of course, the phone rings or someone knocks on the door, because that’s a universal truth of bathtubs!

At the time, I was expecting a parcel from my friends in France for my birthday. I asked the sister to call my next destination to see if they could receive the parcel and hold it for me until I arrived. Unfortunately, it wasn’t possible. I wasn’t too worried because, these days, solutions always seem to appear.

And, sure enough, the next morning, Sister Alina (yes, I think it’s fine to share her name) came to me with a solution. Someone who overheard her story about the failed parcel plan, without knowing me at all, without ever having met me, offered to have the parcel sent to his home. Not only that, but he and his wife promised to bring it to me by car, wherever I might be!

I explained to him that I didn’t know exactly where I would be when the parcel arrived and that I was afraid I might be far away by then. But he simply said, “No problem, I’ll drive to you.” And that’s exactly what happened! Ten days later, he received the parcel, contacted me for my location, and after finishing his workday, he and his wife drove two hours to meet me. They handed over the package, chatted with me for a while, and then drove back home. Just like that.

And here’s the kicker: that very night, after I received the parcel—which included replacement parts for my tent—the temperature dropped so much that the aluminum structure of my tent froze and snapped. Like... well, like frozen aluminum. (A very apt analogy, wouldn’t you agree?) But because I had just received the new parts, I was able to repair the tent immediately.

This morning, another aluminum pole snapped... But it’s just that I walked exactly where I shouldn’t have. There are mornings like this. But hey, you know what? I arrived in a village just after that and got invited to stay for the night, even though it was still morning. So after spending a few hours talking, washing myself, and doing my laundry, I managed to repair my tent, so tomorrow will be fine! Great, no?

I used the extra time to write you this message. I could have done it while staying at the pilgrim center in Gietrzwald, but I wasn’t in the mood. I started writing it, but then thought I’d finish it in the tent... But guess what? It’s too cold to keep your arms out of the sleeping bag, and if you only keep your hands out, you get frostbite on your fingers in minutes. So I guess I had to meet these people. And this meeting could easily not have happened because, when I arrived in the village, I realized I had forgotten my hat on a bench about 1.5 km back. This has never happened before—first time. So I walked back to fetch it, then walked all the way back to the village where I met these people and got invited.

Now, another story—and the last.

I got sick in mid-December. I was invited by such kind people, really nice. A beautiful family. Then I was invited to stay a little longer to rest. And I was happy because it was such a nice place with such nice people. So I proposed to cook an apple tarte, Normandy style. I’m very easygoing when it comes to making tarts... Let’s call it cultural exchange!

So I was in the middle of preparing this tart, and I tried a piece of apple to get an idea of how much sugar I should use. And guess what? I had the worst, no-taste apple possible. You know those industrial tomatoes, the ones genetically modified by companies that want to save the world from hunger? One very famous one starts with "MON" and ends with "ENTO." I got exactly the same sensation tasting this apple as when eating their tomatoes. No smell, no taste... You only know it’s a tomato (or apple) because it looks like one before you bite into it. After that, it’s just your brain trying to fill in the blanks for those who know what a tomato or apple is supposed to smell and taste like. (I guess those who’ve never had a real one might get shocked when they finally taste one from a traditional seed-grown garden.) But I’m digressing—excuse me.

I was really disappointed with the apples! It’s basically very hard to make a good apple tarte if the apples aren’t tasty or sugary at all. I guess my disappointment was really obvious because Ania and Krzysztof (yes, I use names, but that’s between us!) asked me what was wrong. Then they tried the apples themselves and were very satisfied...

So either they had no taste in apples, or I had lost mine...

Do you see where this is going? No?

They explained to me, while handing me a bottle of essential oil to test if I could smell anything, that one of the effects of a certain virus going around these days (you’ve probably heard of it) is the total loss of taste and smell.

Imagine the situation: I was with people who had welcomed me very kindly and generously into their home, and I realized there that I was sick with the virus. Honestly, I felt really, really uncomfortable at the time. There’s so much being said about this virus that eventually, I felt guilty—not because of the disease itself, as my doctor had already warned me that I wasn’t part of the population at risk of developing a severe form, but because I was afraid of the fear this virus arouses in people. And, of course, I was afraid of transmitting it to others.

You can perhaps appreciate the delicacy of the situation. Guilt is the easiest emotion to arouse in humans. But I didn’t even have time to dwell on that because my hosts immediately asked me to stay with them until I got better. They tried to put me at ease straight away, reassuring me that I could and should stay with them.

They insisted I shouldn’t be afraid, and that’s when I told them, “I’m not afraid of the disease; I’m afraid of the fear of the disease.” They understood me completely and reassured me by saying, first, that she had already been sick and it hadn’t been a big deal for her. Second, he was in great health, and even when she was sick, he hadn’t caught the virus. Then she mentioned that her parents, who were coming to visit that weekend, were vaccinated.

So, all was okay. She promised to prepare me herbal teas and potions and assured me I had nothing to worry about. I could stay with them without any problem, doubt, or guilt. And I did. And it worked out. After five days of full rest—two of which I spent mostly sleeping—I got slightly better every day. By the fifth day, I was totally fine, and my taste and smell had returned. That part had been the worst because they cooked such nice food, and I couldn’t enjoy it. I mean, I couldn’t even tell the difference, except by texture, between raw garlic, peanuts, or a piece of chocolate! How horrible is that?

Once I got better, I proposed a new cultural exchange in the form of a chocolate pear tart and a spinach pie with Roquefort. They’re easy, fast, and good. I’ve made them dozens of times since leaving France, though I’ve succeeded in only a few of those attempts. Thankfully, this was one of the successful ones.

I stayed with them for about a dozen days, and honestly, I’m so grateful I fell sick while in their care. I had already been considering stocking up on food and water, heading into the woods, putting up my tent in a remote, hidden spot, and staying there for a week. But instead, I ended up with these wonderful people.

On top of being friendly and welcoming, their reaction was so cool, full of normality and reason, which seems rare these days. I was deeply touched. I felt at home, and they appreciated that I felt that way.

So basically, I got sick, but it turned out to be a nice experience! Cool, no?

And it brought me to Gietrzwałd for Christmas, which, in a way, is perfect timing for the pilgrim I am. (You know, Ania and Krzysztof insisted that I spend Christmas with them and their whole family. I know they would have really liked that, and they even offered to come pick me up from where I was. But, well, you know me and birthdays... I declined. Still, I feel so lucky. Truly.)

Voila. I think it’s time for me to stop writing. It’s getting really late, and I still have about an hour to go to send this along with pictures to everyone. So, yes, that’s it for today. I have no idea how long this message is. Hopefully, not too much. I’ll add lots of pictures to balance it out!

Anyway...

I wish you all a beautiful year.
     I wish you well and happy.

See you.
