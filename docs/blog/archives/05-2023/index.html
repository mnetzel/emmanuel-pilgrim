Romania

6/5/2023

0 Comments

 
​     Romania.

     I entered Romania today.
    And just where I was supposed to pass by, I got stopped by the police and stayed a little longer than expected. Just after crossing the border, when I had planned to put my tent on it (the border! Because I still enjoy doing that)—you know, in the middle of the line in a wild area with grass and a few trees—I told myself that I was going to walk a few more kilometers instead.

     A village was right there, and barely halfway through it, a car stopped at my level. A gentleman in stained overalls showed me a border police badge. I admit that at the time, I wondered if it was a hoax, or even a scam, because the guy looked like anything but a policeman on duty... But he called his colleagues, who came to pick me up and took me to the station a few kilometers away.

     So yes, Romania is in Europe, but it’s not in the Schengen area. So, while you can cross the border whenever you want with a French passport and stay as long as you want, the crossing must be done at a border customs post—not in the wild like I did...

     I have to confess that I hadn’t thought of that. This morning, I had the choice between a large, long, very busy road with fast vehicles, or a small country road ending in a dirt path, deserted and much shorter, but crossing grass fields and small woods.

     Guess which choice I made?

     I spent my afternoon waiting for my case to be processed. Lots of paperwork, with many copies of each. And after placing maybe twenty signatures, I was taken back to the Hungarian border, where I had to pay a fine.

    It’s intriguing to me because it wasn’t Romania that punished me for illegally entering their territory, but Hungary for leaving theirs...

     Anyway, I paid my dues. The customs officers were very nice. They did their job. I would have liked the law and its application to remain at their discretion because, yes, I made a mistake, but it was an honest mistake. I really didn’t know and wouldn’t have done it if I had known.

     After a few photos together and an exchange of telephone numbers, the customs officer who had called me in the morning was kind enough to accompany me back to the place where he had caught me so that I could resume my journey while continuing to be able to say, without lying, that I really did my entire journey on foot. Very nice of him.

     Why am I telling you this?

    Well, now I feel ashamed in addition to having been negligent. Because I think of all those people who often give me a coin or more to support my journey, many of whom are financially modest people, even very modest. People to whom I cannot refuse the money they give me—I assure you that I really try because it is very hard for me to receive or accept money. But in the face of their insistence, to avoid disappointing them, or just because sometimes I feel their genuine motivation to participate, I agree to receive the donation.

     But today, those donations went to pay a fine.

    It’s silly, maybe... Probably. But I feel ashamed that these pennies were spent this way. I feel as though I’ve betrayed the gift of these people, and this impression stays with me until the evening in my tent, where I am deeply moved.

     It’s 80 euros that I wasted...

     That’s how it is. But I think back to the 10-year-old boy who caught up with me on his bike as I was leaving his village after he heard in the grocery store who I was and what I was doing. Once he caught up with me, he got off his bike and asked if it was really true that I had walked all this way. While I was confirming it to him, he put his bike on the ground, took out the only coin his wallet contained, and very seriously—almost solemnly—offered it to me.

     It was important to him, and I feel ashamed to think that his gift foolishly went toward a fine...

    As I write these words, the Romanian officer (Marian), who now has my phone number, calls me to find out if I was finally able to cross the border. He tells me I can sleep at his place if I want.

     But as I’m already in my tent, he invites me to have a coffee with him in the morning near the post where I spent my afternoon.

     He also invites me to spend the Easter weekend at his house since it’s on my way, and we seem to have a lot to discuss.

     The next day, I resume my journey, which should bring me to the town where I’ll be hosted by Marian. Upon arriving in town, a car stops beside me, the driver gets out and approaches me—I recognize another customs officer. Shaking my hand, he tells me he’s happy to see me. He asks if I need anything, if he can help me, and even if I’d like to sleep at his place. I tell him I’ve already been invited by one of his colleagues, but we exchange phone numbers just in case, and he resumes his journey as I head to a restaurant for a late lunch.

     During my meal, while I’ve saved Mugurel’s number in my contacts to exchange messages via WhatsApp or Signal, he texts me to ask if I’d like to wash my clothes at his place. Washing my clothes is certainly a good idea—I’ll see how to organize myself for that.

     In the end, I will spend three nights at Marian’s, and during these three days, we will spend two at Mugurel’s house with his family, enjoying a good meal, engaging in nice discussions, and savoring a very pleasant little red wine.

     Well, that’s my arrival in Romania. And even though I felt a little bad (and very stupid) for crossing the border without checking out beforehand, I tell myself that I probably wouldn’t have met these people otherwise. So, it was a blessing in disguise, as we say...

     On my way back, I plan to stop at a small chapel on the outskirts of town. To get there, after leaving the road, you have to cross a garden/meadow adjoining a small house with a few chicken coops and sheepfold buildings nearby.

     A senior man with a wheelbarrow is busy there. We greet each other. He no longer has many teeth but keeps a beautiful smile. He leaves his wheelbarrow to accompany me to see the chapel, tells me where to put my bag, and then leaves me there. I stay for a few long moments, eyes closed in front of this small reconstructed cave, and then I climb the small hill, at the top of which stands the little chapel.

     When I come down the opposite side, I stop to greet a horse grazing there. And when I finally get back to my bag, the gentleman and his granddaughter are bringing me something to eat and drink: eggs tinted with red onion, as is the tradition for Easter here, cake, and fruit juice. He shows me around his house, his two lambs, his two goats, their kids, his beautiful hens, and his three piglets. I also greet his dogs, though I can’t get close to his two cats.

    He and his wife are interested in my little pilgrimage story—impressed, maybe—but really happy to welcome me.

     He cried twice as we spoke. The first time was when I told him how much the people along my path have welcomed me, listening with emotion to the kindness, attentions, and care that I described having been offered to me. His hand rested on his chest as I spoke, tapping it softly. His eyes grew wet, and a tear rolled down.

     When I got ready to leave, we shook hands for a long time, and I thanked him for his kindness. He had offered me food, a drink, the chance to charge my phone, and welcomed me with such warmth. He replied, full of emotion to the point of tears, the monotonous voice of the translator relaying his words to me: "This is the God I serve."

     And as I was about to let go of his hand to leave, he added: "Will you come back to see me? I'll welcome you again. You'll come back, huh?"

     This is how I entered Romania.

     Now that I’ve been walking here for a few weeks, I can say I am welcomed just as warmly as anywhere else.

     Today, as I was describing this hospitality on the phone to a friend in France—telling him how, the day before, I had been offered a phone battery recharge and a beer while waiting by the village bartender in a village I was crossing, and how, while I was sipping a local lager, a gentleman seated at the next table went out for a moment and returned with a full bag that he offered me: fresh grapes (about 1.5 kg), a large brioche bread (an Easter tradition for the Orthodox Church), and a good kilo of sausages, which I unfortunately could not accept because they were full of meat... So, then he offered me another beer.

     And then, when it was time to leave, as I shook hands to say goodbye to everyone, I felt a folded note slipped into my palm. When I refused to accept it, the bartender made big gestures to ensure I wouldn’t refuse it...

     There you go.

     I was telling my French friend all of this to describe the welcome I always receive. I was on the phone while walking when I arrived in a small village. I decided to stop, put my bag down, and sit under the porch of the church to finish chatting comfortably in the shade, on a bench. In the middle of my conversation with France, a lady entered the space, which sheltered some tombs around the church. After pulling out a few weeds here and there, she came toward me and, as though I spoke the language, said what I presume to be the following:

     "What are you doing here? Where do you come from?"
     Heu Francia, pilgrim, I said, pointing to my chest...

   She spoke kindly, and I didn’t understand much, but the principal message was full of benevolence. She ended up taking me by the arm, as ladies sometimes do with a man, and led me toward the low wall by the side of the road to point out a house. She gestured to me that I had to go there to eat. I understood the invitation. My friend was still on the phone and quietly shared in the spontaneous local kindness, listening to all this discreetly.

     I’ll skip the details because otherwise, it would take pages and pages, but that’s how I was offered a magnificent breakfast/lunch: fried eggs from the farm, bread with plum marmalade, and fresh morning milk (which, for those who don’t know, is exactly like the milk bought in cartons, but with a smell and taste... which is really good). All homemade.

     The nephew, who lived across the road, came to translate since he spoke English (which helped a lot, especially as I had no network for phone translation). All this very nice food had to be washed down with a superb little homemade plum spirit, double distilled to reach 53 degrees—just to help bring it all down. I left with a bottle of fresh milk, shortbread cake with chocolate between the layers, a jar of plum marmalade, and two small bottles of plum spirit and blackberry cream.

     There are a few things I’d like to point out here that I think are important. This lady came to see me and spontaneously invited me to her place to eat and drink. She took me by the arm to show me her house, and we went there together. She had never met me before and had only seen me briefly before approaching me.

     This, for me, tends to demonstrate that her act was dictated by her own qualities and not by anything about me. In other words, she was good and kind to me because she was good and kind, not because it was me. I was a total stranger to her (she had no way of knowing how wonderful I am...).

    I find that reassuring. There is something truly savory here, and I have the privilege of witnessing this expression of kindness. It’s precious, isn’t it?

     This lady was moved when I left, and so I was touched...
    I resumed my journey very briefly, as I was invited to the house opposite (the nephew’s, you know) for a little coffee, a little plum spirit that I refused, a galette of sheep’s cheese, and an Annette (a pastry folded like an envelope around the filling and pan-fried on both sides). It was really delicious, even when you’re completely full as I was. I’m attaching a photo so you can get an idea of the taste.

      All this to say that, even if it’s a shame you can’t experience these kindnesses live, I do my best to share them with you as authentically as possible...

    Ah yes, I forgot: in the next village, a gentleman in his yard saw me pass by, greeted me, and waved. I stopped because he offered me water, and since I was about to start looking for a bivouac spot, filling my bottles seemed like a good idea. I refused the little plum spirit... but I gladly accepted the homemade sheep’s cheese. A true delight, which I am savoring as I write these words.

     I’m under a beautiful tree that will give me shade tomorrow morning, so that waking up and packing my bag and tent won’t leave me sweating. I’m just a few meters from a small river where I bathed and rinsed my clothes. The lawn is a soft, vibrant green, dotted with flowers sprouting everywhere.

     This place was shown to me by two children who followed me for more than a kilometer and a half. Even though I would have passed by it on my own, they wanted to walk with me.

     That’s it, that’s all for this time. I’ll stop here. I hope that I’ve been able to convey, if not the flavors or the smells, then at least and above all, this wonderful feeling of being welcomed with kindness and generosity by strangers full of humanity...

     Voilà.
     Au revoir.

     P.S. 1: I came across bison! I’m attaching a picture.
     And water buffalo too, but they were too far away for me to take a photo...

     P.S. 2: The mosquitoes are starting to appear. But I don’t mind, because my tent has a mosquito net that works... Hehehe...
