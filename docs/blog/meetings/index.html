Meetings - Poland and Czech Republic

12/2/2022 0 Comments

Now we are sure—winter is definitely here. No need to comment on that, there are far more cold days than fresh ones. That’s how I recognize it!

I would like to describe my journey day by day, you know, as a journal for you to see and realize how crazy nice it is. Because it is—it’s just crazy nice. Every day. From the beginning until now. I mean, come on, 15 months! Non-stop!!

So I’m going to write, or at least try to write, a little summary of my day every day, to give you an idea of how things are going for the pilgrim, day by day.

Last night was nice. I slept in my tent in the forest. Usual and nice. My way is really easy nowadays, as I follow a very well-indicated path of Jacob (Compostela way/Santiago or St. James way). Most of it is on small woodland or rural paths. Only occasionally do I walk on roads, and through towns only when there’s something worth seeing. The route has been designed to showcase beautiful natural places and cross important historical, archaeological, and cultural landmarks. So it’s nice.

I don’t have to use my phone map much—the yellow symbol of a St. Jacques shell on a blue square is at every ambiguous crossing, waiting to guide me.

After packing my tent and backpack, I walked, rested, and walked some more. Then, suddenly, I realized I wasn’t wearing my hat anymore. First time this has happened! Strange, because normally I can feel it. But I understood why: at my last stop, I had been a little sweaty, so I sat on a bus stop bench to cool down and took off my hat. Somehow, I went on without it because I was still warm. Really unusual. But, let’s say it happens.

I realized this at the entrance of a village and felt like I had wasted my morning because I needed to go back to fetch my hat and then walk the same path a third time. I did, because even though this hat is a real disappointment (it was supposed to be high quality, but no—it’s really not. Yet it was expensive! And the slogan of the brand is “Be protected by a legend”—seriously, you should see this “legend”!), I still use it and need it. A hat is an essential part of a pilgrim’s outfit, after all.

So, I went back about 1.5 km, found it on the bench where I suspected I’d left it, and then returned to the village entrance. There, it seemed I had two options to get to the church whose steeple I could see. I chose the one on the right. It was a dead end—I walked half a kilometer for nothing!

I tried to find an alternative way that wouldn’t make me retrace my steps, maybe by crossing a field, you know... but after a while, I realized that wasn’t possible either because fences were all around. I even spent more time looking for an Indiana Jones-style solution than I would have by simply going back. Finally, I had to walk all the way back to the place where I had first realized I was missing my hat and take the straightforward path.

At that point, I really thought my morning hadn’t been productive at all! It felt like, somehow, I had done my best to waste time on my walk. Without intending to, I had worked hard not to make much progress this morning. It was as if I were delayed, as if I had to wait a little before going further.

I was probably too early! For what? No idea. But I was confident it was for something, and that I was there for that thing. It might sound a little crazy to you, but after walking for more than 400 days, I no longer follow my plans. In fact, I stopped making plans as much as possible very early on. “As much as possible,” because humans love to make plans—for everything, always! Try not to plan. If you really look and reflect honestly, you’ll see how hard it is not to.

But as a pilgrim—someone walking in the unknown but with a goal—planning my way, whether in time or space, becomes completely irrelevant, to the point of being a total waste of time and even counterproductive. Instead, to put it poetically or mystically, I let my way show me my way. And sometimes, it takes strange coincidences, meetings, or events (like this morning) to make me aware of what’s supposed to come next—if something is supposed to come next. This might sound strange to you, and I hope I’m not too confusing in my attempt to explain it.

Let’s just say something was unusual about my morning walk, and all of it seemed to result in nothing but a nice waste of time. My experience over the past 15 months, however, tells me that instead of focusing on the wasted time, I should stay aware of what’s coming next. That’s a relevant attitude to adopt even outside of pilgrimage, I’d say, but it becomes almost undeniably clear when you’re on one. There are no wrong ways. This concept and feeling of “wrongness” are, ultimately, very relative. One should strive to be more flexible. (The way trains you in that!) Finally, when you arrive, if you look back, everything that brought you to where you are is simply the way you used—your way. You might have used a lot of time, but you are here, aren’t you?

So I finally arrived at the church I wanted to stop by, and after taking off my backpack, plugging in my phone and power bank, I started to meditate a little, as I do anytime I can get inside one. Not for long, though, because not even five minutes had passed when a lady and her husband arrived. When they saw me, they came over very enthusiastically. And that’s how I met this very nice couple that I would definitely have missed if everything had "gone well." So, in the end, I was glad for my "wasted morning time."

Very soon, we were joined by the parish priest. After the mass, I was invited to have lunch at the parish house next door. It was a good opportunity to answer the many questions I was asked. I’m sure I’ll be in the next local newspaper. As we talked after lunch, night came fast—15:45! And so, I got to stay the night in the same place. After a kind farewell, I set up my sleeping mat on the floor next to an electric plug and started writing the summary of the day. Then the priest came in. He insisted that I feel free to use the kitchen, dig into the fridge, and cook myself dinner. And again, we talked—for at least two hours.

In the morning, I woke up early. The priest had to get ready for the mass, and I needed to pack all my stuff before he left. After the mass, I bid him farewell. To my surprise, a few people were waiting for me with gifts—chocolates, biscuits... With what the priest gave me, I was settled for the next few days!

Of course, for all of them, I collected prayer intentions for my next destination. Pilgrim duty, which I take very seriously. And then I went on my way.

Today, I passed through a town. Nothing particularly special, except that I crossed paths with a lady on the street at the entrance to the town. She saw the St. Jacques shell on my backpack and stopped to chat. It turned out she was one of the two organizers of the Jacob’s Way in this area. And guess who the second organizer was? The lady I had met the previous day, who invited me for lunch with her husband. Small world, isn’t it? The coincidence was nice.

My backpack is really heavy because I carry as much food as I can and all my water bottles are full. Just for those two needs, I carry around 10–12 kg! Now you understand why my backpack’s name is "Expiation." But that also means I can stop in a nice place and rest for a night—or even two if I want. I have enough supplies for that...

Today, I slept very well again. I was in my tent in the forest for the second night in the same spot, as it was a very nice place and I needed a day of rest. So, I just stayed for another night. This morning, I felt really good. Fully rested, and as you feel after a very good sleep. I woke up early and was ready to go after about an hour of packing up all my stuff and tent.

Beautiful forest. It seems this part of Poland is really nice for that—big, beautiful forests everywhere. Luckily, the Jacob’s Way goes through them. It’s so nice to walk there.

The only downside this morning was that I ran out of water. Water is heavy, and I carry only five liters with me. Since I stayed an extra day in the wild, I went through my reserve and ran dry by the morning. My map showed me a tiny village a few kilometers ahead, so I wasn’t desperate—just thirsty.

When I arrived at the first house, you know, the ones on the periphery of the village but still a good distance from its center, I stopped to rest on a big rock next to a canal, facing two houses and a superb bridge. A very nice place to stop and enjoy the water passing by—and I did. But thirst shortened my contemplation, and I went on my way. I crossed the bridge and stopped at a Y-bifurcation of the road, just to be sure of my direction and decide which of the two houses I’d ask for water. Exactly at that moment, someone called out to me from the other side of his garden fence, asking if I wanted a drink. The timing was so perfect. I did want one, and, even more, it was precisely what I was concerned about at that exact moment.

It turned out I was invited by a really cool guy—for coffee first, then lunch, and eventually to stay the night. Super cool for his warm welcome, but not only that. The guy is a passionate enthusiast of the Middle Ages and knights. To the point of belonging to a brotherhood whose members dedicate themselves to preserving medieval culture and organizing jousting games and duels as they were done back then. That means sword fighting, with real equipment and everything! Fully functional armors and weapons, made authentically like in those times. And yes, the swords are real (though not sharpened, to avoid cutting too heavily into either the brotherhood’s numbers or their limbs). It’s not just for show. How cool is that?

In the afternoon, we went on a quad bike ride through the forest because he wanted to show me a few things about the area. First, the canal—a massive and famous feat of hydraulic engineering designed to transport special pine wood from this region (and only here in the world!) that was perfect for creating aeronautical masts back when wood was still in use. Then, a grim place tied to the war, the post-war period, and Poland’s tortured and complex history—especially in this region.

It was the site of the totally senseless deaths of former deportees used for forced labor, French prisoners of war executed by communist forces, likely out of doubt and miscommunication, as none of them spoke the right language to explain their presence in the area.

I really enjoyed his kindness and the time he generously spent showing me around. Also, his wife was a great cook and had prepared homemade pizza with yeast dough she made herself for dinner...

I slept in his wooden bungalow. The next morning, after coffee, he walked with me for a good kilometer—just like that—until the entrance of the village. We parted there. Nice, no? My day was nice. Lots of forest. Small villages. Still plenty of food, a bit frozen, but it’s okay. All is well.

I found a beautiful spot between fir trees, with a soft carpet of moss. A perfect place to sleep—protected from the wind, with almost no risk of a tree falling if the weather decided to get a little wild during the night. It’s winter, and there’s a blizzard right now. Fir trees provide good protection against both wind and snow coming from the side. Looks like a good night in perspective...

In the morning, after the usual ritual of packing the backpack and folding the tent, I resumed my journey through these magnificent woods.

For the second time, I saw and observed a moose—another female. A superb moment when time seemed to stop, and everything was in suspension, alert.

In India, we use the word Darshan for such moments, often reserved for mystical encounters. But the concept can also extend to events like this—an encounter with the animal world. Because, obviously, there are very, very few moose in India, that’s for sure...

In the middle of the morning, I left the forest and arrived in a small village. I stopped at the first house that seemed occupied to ask for water. The lady who answered didn’t speak a word of English, and even less of French. Thanks to my few words of Polish vocabulary and a lot of gestures, I explained the basics: I am a pilgrim, I am French, I have walked a lot to get here. Could I have water to fill my bottles, please? As soon as she understood, she said many things that still elude me, but the essential and general part was clear: she had understood me well, said she regularly saw pilgrims but definitely not in this season, and was impressed that I had come from France on foot. She told me I must be totally crazy. She insisted I shouldn’t take tap water but bottled mineral water, which she said was much better. After that, we said goodbye.

Two hundred meters further, I came across a wayside crucifix with a bench for onlookers, which was perfect for me. Places to sit are scarce and highly sought after on the way. A stone, a stump, a fallen tree... The bench is technically the best.

In Poland, benches are often found at the foot of crucifixes and calvaries. They are practical and comfortable but usually located in villages or near roads with cars and noise. In Germany or Luxembourg, public benches are everywhere—even on remote forest paths. That was very surprising for me. But I have to admit, it’s nice to know you can sit comfortably without getting dirty or wet every 500 meters.

I was enjoying a well-earned sitting time when the neighbor from the house nearby went out for a firewood expedition. With a wheelbarrow, a saw, and a hatchet under his arm, he came over to talk when he saw me. He had much better rudiments of English than I have in Polish. He offered me hot tea, and as we chatted outside—me still sitting on the bench, sipping the hot liquid—the lady who had given me water earlier joined us. She was practically running, in her dressing gown and slippers, through the morning snow, holding a plate in her hand. She must have been preparing food when I came to her door, and when she saw me sitting on the bench, she brought me four pancakes with jam that she had just made. A few words were exchanged between her and the gentleman, and then she trotted off again. It must have been minus five degrees this morning, which cuts roadside conversations in dressing gowns short—that’s proven!

In any case, the gesture was touching. And the pancakes were super good. Pancakes in these conditions are always super good. But you see, it’s like this every day. It makes me cry sometimes.

I got back into the forest, found a nice place, and voilà...

Today I walked a good 15 km before meeting a couple of dynamic retirees who were on their weekly walk for him, daily for her.

I say walk, not promenade, because their pace was brisk. We crossed paths, and when we were level, stopped to chat. Then they invited me to their place—a newly built house in the next village, 6 km back from where we were. That was 6 km I had just walked. But nonetheless, I went back with them to their place.

We had a lunch/dinner... In Poland, meal schedules are a bit weird to grasp for a French guy. As I’ve been told, it’s probably because there are no meal breaks during working hours. So when your 8-hour shift is done, the first thing you do is eat. And then, obviously, everything gets messy. The schedules are jostled—lunch happens at 4 p.m., dinner is very late or skipped entirely, and snacks appear in between. We then arrive at situations worthy of hobbits, where we hear about first breakfast, second breakfast, the 11 a.m. snack...

Anyway, it was such a nice meeting with this couple. They were fluent in English because they had spent more than 15 years in Japan for his work.

There is always something common among people who have lived abroad—wherever it might be—for a period long enough to drop part of their own ways and open up to the local ones. A kind of open-mindedness emerges, where the habits and certainties specific to each place are put into perspective. I often feel this when meeting expatriates or returnees. I quite like it—knowing that we are right to do or see things as we do at home, while also putting into perspective the fact that elsewhere, "we" do things completely differently. It might even be the opposite way, but it also works. Putting my certainties into perspective has given me a lot of flexibility, that’s for sure. And tolerance too...

But let's come back to the narrative. After eating and talking, night fell, and they had to leave. They had plans that couldn’t be postponed. So they went, feeling a bit sorry for leaving me alone, but we decided to spend the next morning together in a "cultural exchange"—an apple one. Normandy-style apple cultural exchange, also known as tart. Cultural exchanges are great!

But see again: someone I didn’t know invited me to their home, offered me food, hot water, electricity, showed me interest, kindness, generosity, and even trust. They left me totally alone in their house for three hours.

I don’t know about you, but for me, it feels like a blessing. Like being cared for. You see what I mean? Every day. Every day...

I tried to wait for their return, to spend more time with them, but I probably stopped trying because I can’t recall anything after laying down on my mattress and starting to write my daily review.

The next thing I knew—it was morning. We had breakfast together, talking. Then we got into the apple pie. I always start with salted butter caramel, in which I brown some of the apples cut into very small pieces. That becomes my pie crust, or appareil in French, which I then spread on my shortbread dough (sandy dough? Like crumbles...)

When I got ready to leave, he offered me a foldable and portable solar charger. He said he didn’t really use it and thought it could be very useful for me. For sure it could! The last time I tried such a device was in Tibet, attempting to charge a satellite phone. It wasn’t very convincing. But that was a dozen years ago. Maybe progress has made small photovoltaic cells more efficient.

I was super happy—both for the gesture and for the object itself.

If it works, I could attach the cells to the top of my bag, run the cable along to one of the portable batteries that were given to me last year, near Gorzelec and in Warsaw. Even if I have no illusions about the charging capacity, it might be enough to recharge a little bit every day—or at least to make a phone call if necessary. I’ll see. It’s not really the sunny season, but I’ll try as soon as possible.

After all that, with the tart baking in the oven (I hope and wish it was a success!), he drove me back to the same location where we met, bid me farewell, and left. And just like that, it was one more nice meeting with total strangers.

The road I took this morning was really nice. I say morning, but actually, it was already late as the cooking had taken a while. The road skirted a lake, a small and scenic path. Since I hadn’t walked much, I wanted to push a little further in the afternoon. But that can sometimes get tricky when it comes to finding a good place to shelter or pitch the tent. Setting up in the dark is not a great idea.

I saw a sign for a guesthouse and thought it would be a good chance for another night inside. I arrived late, and the place wasn’t open. But someone told me another guesthouse a few kilometers further might be. It was getting tricky as it was getting dark, and the area had a higher population density. That meant a low chance of finding a good, clear spot for the tent.

The only good thing about that situation is that, because of the dark, I could pitch my tent in improbable places. If I got up early, no one would ever know someone had spent the night there. But I still don’t like doing that. Every time I’ve lost or broken tent equipment, it’s been in the dark...

So, I went directly to the second guesthouse. It was closed too! But the lady inside asked me to wait for her son, who spoke English. When he came, after I explained myself a bit, they said they would open a room for me. They told me I should stay, and that opening the place for me wasn’t a problem. I was a bit worried they were scared for me to sleep outside, and I didn’t want to impose.

In general, I try to make people understand that it’s really no problem for me to sleep in the tent—even if it’s freezing. It’s been 15 months, two winters now, that I’ve been doing this. I have the proper equipment to do so safely. Above all, I try to ensure people don’t feel obligated to help me. I also try not to offend anyone, but I prefer to be invited out of interest in what I do, curiosity, generosity, or a sense of welcome, rather than out of fear—even if that fear is kindly meant for me.

So, if someone offers me to stay inside just because they’re afraid or feel bad about me being outside, I usually try to politely decline. But they insisted—"sincerely, from the bottom of their hearts," they told me.

And so... Hot shower, battery charging, and everything. Nice!
      It was really freezing outside—minus 5 to minus 8 degrees—and in the late evening, it hailed.

Today, I followed the lake again. It’s actually the biggest lake in Poland, and I walked along it over these two days. More accurately, two half-days, because I arrived in the city in the middle/end of the morning. A very cute town, crossed by a river. Upon arriving in the old part of town, I went directly to a superb 15th-century church.

Some churches are really special. As soon as you’re inside, you feel truly comfortable. Meditating in those is effortless, and you go deep. This was that kind of church. I don’t know how long I stayed in there—it was timeless, or out of time.

On my way out, I passed a tourist office and decided to check if there was a pilgrim center where I could stay for the night. It would give me the opportunity to explore the town and its five churches. The contact with the tourist office was very friendly—not many tourists around these days—and as a pilgrim, I was welcomed very nicely. We talked over a nice cup of coffee, and after some time, because they couldn’t reach anyone by phone to arrange a room, they suggested I go myself and ring the door of the congregation of fathers right next to the church I had just visited.

The building next to it was indeed a small congregation of fathers who might welcome me. And so I went, and they did welcome me! I spent the afternoon at a restaurant, ticking off a few items from my "city shopping list," visiting three of the town’s churches, and walking through the charming medieval environment.

In the morning, the fathers invited me to eat with them. The prior, who spoke fluent French, having spent more than fifteen years at a former French mission in Cameroon, acted as a translator. The first part of the conversation was with the ladies in charge of taking care of the place and the cook. After a nice breakfast, the prior invited me for coffee in his office, and we spent some more time talking.

Before leaving the town, I returned to the tourist office to say goodbye. They had been so kind and helpful that I felt it was necessary. During our conversation the day before, they had convinced me to adjust my plans and visit Lipye, a small hamlet of a small town locally famous for its basilica dedicated to Mary. This small Marian site required a detour of about thirty kilometers, which is insignificant after the 4000 kilometers I’ve already covered!

I could have done it in one day, but I passed through a forest with small lakes—ponds more than lakes by their size—but the place was too idyllic for me not to stop. In fact, I spent two nights there. After enjoying a beautiful walk, I arrived in the small town and, luckily, found the church open because someone was cleaning it. Most churches are usually closed, but I am often lucky like this.

After taking my time to sit quietly, I met the cleaner, who went to fetch a lady who spoke English. She asked me if I knew where to sleep. In short, I didn’t. She said she would find something for me, and that I could come back later, either before or after the evening mass. She assured me that the priest would be informed and let me know.

That was perfect, as it gave me the afternoon to walk to the hamlet of Lipye, visit the original Marian site, and return in a comfortable, contemplative mode of promenade.

I am really happy to have left the Camino (St. James Way) to come to this place. It is truly a beautiful spot for pilgrimage.

This also confirmed to me the way I choose my destinations—or more precisely, how my destinations choose me. I’m not actively searching for Marian sites. Instead, I remain attentive to mentions or suggestions from the people I meet. Sometimes it’s obvious in the way a Marian location is brought to my attention—through spontaneity, eagerness, or enthusiasm. All of that makes it clear to me that I should go.

That’s how I arrived here, and I have no regrets.

After the mass, the priest told me it wasn’t possible to stay at their place, but that I could go to a hostel nearby. I usually don’t like going to hotels or hostels too much—for budget reasons, but also because the feeling is different than being invited as a pilgrim into someone’s home. Paying for a room is, for me, an easy solution that I use from time to time, of course—especially in big cities, when I’m really tired after a long day, or when it’s too late and too dark to walk a few more kilometers to find a place. But in general, I avoid it as much as possible.

This time, however, I found myself in a situation where I was somehow constrained. It was far too late to leave the city and find a place to pitch the tent, and even if it had been possible, the room was already being booked for me. This was the first time something like this had happened to me. Not sleeping in a paid room, but the way it happened.

Nowadays, I pay attention to these small details that break from the routine of my pilgrimage—little bumps in the classic, gentle unfolding of my repetitive days. I wondered what might be waiting for me at the end of this detour from the smoothness I had become accustomed to...

But nothing!

After handing over my passport and paying, I talked a bit with the receptionist and then went to bed. In the morning, on my way to check out, the director of the place was waiting for me. As soon as I appeared, he jumped at the opportunity to ask if I could answer a few questions for the local newspaper.

So I cancelled my morning plans—I had intended to go to the church and then to a big shop to buy food. But as I’ve told you, there’s no such thing as making plans when you’re on a pilgrimage. I accepted his offer to chat over breakfast. A three-hour breakfast.

But I was glad to answer his questions and did my best to do it well and sincerely. He was so enthusiastic and excited, and he seemed to really enjoy hearing about my experiences and adventures.

I suppose my story will now reach many people in the area. Perhaps some of them saw me passing through the town and will soon learn that I am a pilgrim—not a tourist, trekker, or vagabond. I wouldn’t say that was the reason for what happened, but now that it’s happened, I have no issue with how it turned out. Even if it wasn’t the way I would have preferred at first.

The director said he would send me a copy of the article. I’ll let you know.

Later, I received an impersonal alert message warning of great risks of blizzards and violent winds. I found a beautiful, quiet place near the river just outside the city. It’s a small natural bowl, hollow with a flat, very slightly sloped bottom, surrounded by many small fir trees. It’s perfect for pitching the tent—protected from the wind as well as from view. I’m going to spend two days here because, indeed, the wind and blizzard have arrived, but the place is very comfortable.

It happens more often these days that I take a day off. Maybe it’s the winter. Maybe I’m getting worn out, as well as my gear! But that’s fine. I take it easy. I think if I push myself too hard, I won’t be able to go far for long. So, I go slow. I hurry slowly and gently.

During the night, I woke up. I thought a pine cone had fallen on my tent—it makes a big noise inside when that happens. I went back to sleep, finding it strange that a pine cone had fallen when there were no pines around where I was...

In the morning, I realized it wasn’t a pine cone but one of the aluminum frames that support the tent. It had broken under the combined effect of the cold and the tension of the tent fabric, which stretches as it dries. Without any additional force, the small aluminum pipe snapped and, of course, cut the flysheet of the tent by 3 cm as it broke.

Really, sometimes I feel like just looking too hard at those tubes when they’re frozen might be enough to break them...
     I’ll have to find a solution for this. I still have a few spares, but I go through them quickly. I’ll need to find a gear shop soon.

Today, I arrived in a village 6 or 8 km before a town. I planned to stop at the edge of the town, sleep there, and cross it tomorrow. I didn’t want to cross the town today because it’s Sunday, and most shops would be closed. So today would be a short one. That meant I had time, so I could enjoy a coffee at the village grocery store. See how the mind is tricky?

But it was minus 12 degrees Celsius, and somehow hot liquid feels good in those conditions... So I went to the grocery store and ordered a coffee. The woman refused to let me pay for it—it was free for me, she said. Thank you, madame. A coffee given freely always warms better...

I went outside, under the shade of the store, which was made for that. A guy was there drinking, too. And so, we started talking. His boots were of very good quality, I could see—paramilitary or police, or something like that. Turned out he was a fireman, on a day off, working for the fire department in the town.

I thought he was the right person to understand my equipment needs and maybe tell me if there was a shop in town that could fulfill them. And you know what? The guy was friends with the owner of the only shop in town that might have some of the things I needed. He directly proposed to call her to check. Nice coincidence, right?

The lady from the shop proposed to come and pick both of us up where we were, take us to visit the warehouse where all her stock is stored (both for the shop and online sales), as well as the shop itself, to see if I could find what I needed. Then, she would drive us back to our starting point so I could continue my journey while still being able to say I had covered it all on foot.

How cool is that?! On a Sunday afternoon, no less.

She even called her son in England to act as a translator for the whole thing, as neither she nor my fireman friend spoke enough English.

Just stop for a minute and consider the whole situation. Consider my perspective as well. I mean, I’m on foot and can’t easily wander around looking for shops. And yet, I meet these people exactly when I need them. I broke my tent this morning! And here are such kind people who, without even knowing me at all, decide to help me so kindly. Seriously?

And all of this happened because I went to get a hot coffee at the grocery store. This kind of thing has happened with everything I’ve needed since the start of my journey. Anytime I’ve been in need, these crazy, nice encounters have occurred. Or sometimes, I’ve just found what I needed lying on the ground along the path, as if someone had dropped it there for me.

For 16 months now, this has built a strong confidence in me—the belief that I should drop all worries. All is fine, and all will be fine. After dropping the unnecessary items from your backpack, a pilgrim begins to drop the unnecessary burdens they carry within themselves, like worries...

And it feels good!
     It feels so good!

We feel light. We feel confident. We feel free. We feel loved.

When she drove me back to the grocery store where it all started, it was the beginning of the afternoon. I had plenty of time to walk to the small forest I had seen at the town’s entrance, near the river. I could take it easy there, and even stop at the pizzeria I had spotted a hundred meters ahead. Everything was going well. Really well, because the pizzeria was open now—it hadn’t been this morning. There was an electrical outlet next to where I sat, so I started writing what you are reading now. And I told myself that the world is well made...

I had to cross the town again today, on foot this time, and it was nice. Not all towns are comfortable to walk through, but this one was. The center had old buildings, and the churches I visited were massive and beautiful. At the end of the town, I came across a big supermarket. I stopped to refill my bag with food and then continued. Just a few hundred meters later, I saw a restaurant. It was open, even though it was only 11:30. They told me, in perfect English, that I could wait inside for 20 minutes.

The thought of a hot lunch won me over. I sat there, charging my phone while writing what you are reading. A nice vegetarian meal followed, during which a friend of the owner—fluent in English—came over to talk. He offered to take me by car, after my coffee, to a few places I would probably miss otherwise.

I left my backpack at the restaurant, and we went by car to three places: the ruins of a very old monastery that was a pilgrimage site to Mary before it was destroyed, the ruins of a Teutonic castle, and a viewpoint over the valley where I would likely follow my path. After that, he brought me back to the restaurant. I took my bag and continued on my way.

Isn’t it amazing that someone would take three hours of their time to show me these places?
I walked for a few kilometers before darkness fell, then set up my tent and prepared for the night.

I started walking this morning on the path that runs along this small river. It was very cute. And quiet. After a few kilometers, the path took me behind the garden of someone who was in it. He crossed the garden, taking off his gloves to come and greet me. We chatted a bit. He asked if I needed anything, and I said I wasn’t really in need of anything but then corrected myself directly, as I actually needed to refill my water bottles. I had emptied all five liters while staying in the same place for two days. So he invited me inside and offered me coffee. While we talked, my phone was charging. Great!

Then he proposed calling a friend of his, who lived about 7 km further along my way, to see if she could welcome me for the night. She would be happy to receive a pilgrim. Of course, I was interested—you know, all the things: a new meeting, a hot shower, full battery charging...

And so, like that, it happened. I got invited to come to her place, and she said she would cook a soup while I walked there. Voilà.

The walk was nice, following an old railway. I tried to visit some trees I’d been told were beautiful, a little off the path inside the forest. It wasn’t far, and I had time. But I couldn’t find the place, so I returned to the railway and continued on. I arrived at her place quite early. Finally, a small walking day! But that was fine, as everything was going so smoothly.

I received such a warm welcome when I arrived—like we had known each other for a long time. She really put me at ease right away. She had a beautiful, large smile and a kind, vibrant energy. We went directly for the soup, and God knows it was a good soup. I was lucky to meet her—she’s vegetarian like me, so she had made a soup I could enjoy. But you know, with that special touch vegetarians have: a little spice, some turmeric, a bit of ginger... Whaaaa...

A soup that was perfect! And not just because soup is perfect in cold weather, not just because it was vegetarian, not just because it was a change in my culinary routine—the soup was really, really good. Thank you, Maria! (That’s her name.)

Then she went to work, in the next room. Nowadays, people work from home, you know... I took advantage of that to have a nice, hot shower, and afterward, I wrote these few words on my phone. A little later, she went shopping for a few things, including what was needed to make an apple tart! When she got back, we cooked together and ate while talking. A very nice day that ended late.

I left in the morning after a nice breakfast. It was quite late, I remember, because it was 11:11.
That made me think of a French humorist, Desproges, in one of his sketches, playing a maniac who loved how this time of day looked perfectly organized on his electronic watch. For me, it was a kind of sign—to recall my concentration...

Nice snow carpet. About a dozen centimeters. Small, tiny flakes. Less wind, but it’s really cold. Walking in this is a real pleasure. The way is beautiful today—forest and lakes. I enjoy my walk but also my rests.

As I’m resting on the trunk of a tree, cut down by beavers on the side of a lake, enjoying the scenic view, the fresh air, and the quietness, I am joined by a group of three young adults who also enjoy walking. We only met for a few minutes, but we had a very nice interaction. After our small chat, they went on their way. I stayed there for more than an hour, watching the water, watching myself, my thoughts, my breathing. It was really nice. And then I went on.

The forest was large, and I stayed inside it until the end of the afternoon. I was getting tired and thought I’d probably find a place to pitch my tent somewhere around—just a bit off the main path. Somehow, those paths seemed well-used by many walkers, dog walkers, and cyclists.

I was actually considering a little group of trees that looked like a good place to hide myself for the night. But I thought I’d wait until the path cleared of people before heading into the woods. You know, so no one would see me going in—it can look strange to people. So I sat on a big rock to wait for the path to clear.

A man jogging passed by, and before I could go, I saw two ladies coming. So I waited for them to pass as well. When they reached me, they started talking. They were mother and daughter—the first spoke French much better than I speak Polish, and the second was fluent in English. They invited me to their home, which was in the center of the next town, about 7 or 8 kilometers further.

I wasn’t sure at first because I was kind of afraid they were inviting me out of fear for me sleeping outside. In that case, I would prefer to reassure them that it’s totally okay and safe for me to sleep outside, even in the cold. But they insisted. They were happy to invite me, having been pilgrims themselves on the Jacobs Way a few times. Well, they went on their way, wanting to finish their walk before heading back home in their car. As for me, I followed the path to their house, having checked their address on the map.

Wonderful evening. The family of four—though one son isn’t here—tends to have an Italian-style communicative hubbub. Noisy talk, very enthusiastic, with lots of teasing and joking. A really nice evening full of laughter but also full of questions, answered very thoroughly by the dad, who is a history teacher and, like all history teachers, passionate. I don’t know about you, but for me, all the history teachers I’ve met are always the most passionate ones...

I’m writing this in the bed of the room they told me I could occupy for several nights if I want to rest. I don’t know yet if I will, but it’s tempting, as the family is really cool and their invitation feels genuine. It’s 10:30 p.m., and since I usually sleep around 6 p.m. when I’m in my tent—and considering I had a nice long walk today—it won’t take long for me to fall asleep. And I’ll sleep well.

Finally, I decided to stay today. I’d like to go see the church I passed yesterday—a magnificent building of the Teutonic Order. And then, a little rest can’t hurt, especially after a good hot shower!

It’s Sunday morning. My hosts are obviously more of the evening than the morning type. These days, I’m more of a morning person—as you know, if I sleep at 6 p.m., it’s natural... Although I went to bed late last night. But it’s good—I’m writing. So I wait until I hear movement. I’m in a room next to the kitchen and dining room, so I’ll know as soon as someone comes.

It’ll be Małgorzata (Margareth) who moves first. After a small breakfast, she’ll take me to the church, and after that, we’ll visit the center of town a bit. We’ll go back home for lunch, which will be as lively as yesterday’s dinner—full of talking, laughter, and kind teasing of each other. Then, Darius will propose giving me a historical tour of the town. That would be great indeed. And his wife will tell me about another plan: going to a very special place, a pilgrimage site dedicated to Mary, to see the Tsigane Virgin Mary, about 50 km from here. Super great!

And so the entire afternoon was about running around with Dareck (almost literally running). He was so enthusiastic and eager to show and explain everything that the tour turned into a race, with me trotting behind him each time, straining my ears to catch the translations of his explanations via Google Translate.

But this was obviously Dareck’s first time using the translator, and it’s hard to be enthusiastic, thorough, and rushed all while managing the app. Add to that the noise of the streets and the fact that we were constantly darting right and left, and the visit quickly became an exhausting exercise. Super interesting, but I came home on my knees, as we say in France, having listened to—and eventually only heard—far more information than my brain could process in such a short time.

I, who have become accustomed through my pilgrimage to calm, serenity, and silence, all approached gently and slowly, experienced the visit as an overwhelming task. One should restrain themselves to smaller visits; more can be counterproductive.

After the afternoon snack, the three of us—since the children (grown-up but you understand) weren’t joining—went to visit the place of the Tsigane Virgin. That was so cool because I had really been thinking of going there when I first heard about it in the morning. So all was well.

When we got back home, Margaret’s French teacher was there, as well as a family friend’s son, who is a journalist. We spent two hours talking, and then we had dinner. The conversation continued late into the night. Having a history teacher available to answer questions was very valuable to me because I must say Polish history is very complex and complicated—and until recently, it was completely unknown to me. And since Dareck was so eager to inform me, using a lot of charts and explanations, I took the opportunity to ask all my questions.

I must admit, though, that of all our discussions, the one we had on Monday morning was the most beneficial to me.

I got up at the same time as Margaret to say goodbye to her before she left for work. My night was short (as I had talked with Dareck until late), but I wanted to see her off. She was the first person I met from this family, and it was she who invited me. I also wanted to thank her for the warm welcome. And so, in the morning, after she left, Dareck came back—he was on holiday—and we talked again. This time it was quieter, just the two of us. As he was getting much more comfortable with the translator, the conversation went much better.

Finally, I left around 11 a.m.! To be honest, I walked only a little that day, found a nice spot for my tent, and stayed there the next day to recover! And also to write. But I admit, I slept most of the day.

You see now how beautiful my experience of the pilgrimage is. This is just the past 15 days. And it has been like this from the very beginning. I mean, you might think that Polish people are very kind, generous, and lovely—and they are—but it was the same in France, Belgium, Luxembourg, Germany, Lithuania, and Latvia. And I’m only talking about external experiences here.

You will excuse me if I keep my intimate experiences to myself for now, so I can fully benefit from their depth without diluting their essence. Maybe I will share them later, when I’ve assimilated them.

But in the meantime, just the external experiences—adventures, encounters—that alone is already magnificent, surprising, surreal, almost marvelous and extraordinary. Really.

Do you see it?
      I hope so.
    If not, here’s one last example before you go: This morning, as I was leaving the magnificent medieval town of Toruń, I stopped for a small rest on an urban works signaling element at the end of the bridge over the river. A team of construction workers was there, working on the bridge. After a few moments, we started talking. Eventually, a cyclist stopped, and that was fortunate because he spoke English and could translate for everyone. Then one of the construction workers, Maciek, asked me if I needed anything. But I didn’t, as I was just coming out of the city, my backpack full of food and water. Since Toruń is a big city, I had also stocked up on new equipment (a sleeping mat and other camping gear). So, all was fine. I told him this three times because he kept asking. And then he offered me money for my way. I refused. I refused because I have enough for now, and it’s very hard for me to accept money.

Many times, people I meet help me with a night inside, a hot shower, a meal, or some food from their garden or elsewhere. That I accept easily and gladly. But accepting money feels different to me—it’s harder to take.

Yet he insisted, and his gesture was kind, sincere, and simple. He really wanted to participate and help me. I refused three times. But then, I saw him folding a 100 Zlotys note, and as I tried to stop him, he pushed my hand away, placed the money in the pocket of my vest, and locked it there by zipping it shut. I thought it wouldn’t be right to refuse his generosity. I was touched by his kindness, by his gesture toward me and my pilgrimage. I was just sitting there. And this happened.

I finally went on my way, with the cyclist riding alongside me for a while to show me the right direction. After a nice walk, I found a beautiful spot next to the river. I took pictures and recorded a small video there to show you.

In the evening, I reached into my pocket and took out the money. It wasn’t a single 100 Zlotys note. It was 18 notes of one hundred.

It nearly made me cry...

I thought, “This is crazy. Maybe I should go back and return it—it must be a mistake.”

But no, it wasn’t.

We exchanged messages by phone, and it was a kind gift for my way.

This evening, I spent a long time sitting at the foot of a big tree under which my tent was pitched, watching the river flow by, thinking that somehow, I am cared for so well and so warmly that I can’t be wrong in my way. Hopefully.

Voilà. That’s it for today.

It takes a lot of time and a lot of battery to write this on the phone.

I hope you enjoyed it.
