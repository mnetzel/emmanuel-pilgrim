Kindness along the Way

23/4/2024

0 Comments

 
     ​That morning, I woke up as we all like, after having had a great night's sleep. By that, I mean that the night had passed without me noticing anything. It's interesting, I find, that what defines a good night is that we have seen nothing of it, that we were totally absent from it. So unconscious that we have no idea what happened. And the less we know, the better the night was! I find this very similar to the paradox of the spiritual, which defines that we can access it to the extent that we are not there to do or wish it.

     In short, I slept really well. And I knew it because when I woke up, I felt great. Everything seemed to be heading towards a good day. I stretched, watching the sun rise. It was good. I don't know about you, but here we went from the intense cold of winter to the heat of summer without any transition. Within three days, the snow had melted, and it was 25 degrees Celsius. I folded my tent and repacked my backpack like every morning I walk, while enjoying the song of the birds already back. And I set off.

     In the first village that I passed, a small, very small church was there. I was heading there to enter, but as often, it was closed. As I sat on the few steps of its entrance, through the window of the house next door, someone was waving at me. A sign that I translated by deduction from the situation as: don't move, we'll bring you the keys. And in fact, a gentleman was already approaching me. After the usual introductions, Petrus introduced me to this church, almost a chapel in size, then left me the keys and told me to come and have breakfast when I had finished and closed the door.

     An hour later, I was welcomed by the whole family present: the grandfather, the grandmother, the son who is also the village priest, officiating in the other much larger church which he immediately offered me to also visit after eating. There were also the two small children. My host called a friend who told me he was speaking French. Five minutes later, we were all around a bottle of Palinka, a glass in hand, chatting in French/English/Romanian, while the meal was cooked. Marius, who came down from his pastures especially for this, actually has very good French, a little rusty from lack of practice but good enough to have a good discussion. I then visited the farm, stopped by to see cows, goats, dogs, cats... and as the morning began to end, Petrus and his father had to be gone for half an hour, so I stayed waiting, taking advantage of the opportunity to recharge the batteries and use the Wi-Fi.

     I was planning my route a bit to go to the next big city where I planned to receive a package from France. As my bank card was soon to expire, I had to receive a new one, and I took advantage of this necessity to receive other materials. It's a bit complicated to organize all this when you're traveling. But I had already crossed the town in question a few months ago before heading into the north-northeast of Romania, and so I contacted a guesthouse that I knew there to ask them to receive the package for me so that I only had to pass by to collect it... Everything is in the timing...

     The idea being to spend as little time as possible in the city because the city is expensive for the pilgrim's budget, and also because after so much time alone in the mountains or forests, the city, with its agitation, its smells, its noises, becomes for me a place of passage more than of stay... But having to pass there, I intended to optimize the opportunity by taking advantage of the shops to buy clothing items which deteriorate so quickly, and to find a dry cleaner where I could have my down sleeping bag washed. Natural goose down requires special dry washing and many hours of drying to achieve satisfactory results. Impossible to do it in the washing machine at home...

    And then Petrus came back, and we went to the main church. He had to stop at the village grocery store to get the keys, which was a good thing because I needed to restock the food box in my bag. While he went about his business with the shopkeeper, I did my shopping. When it was time to pay, the shopkeeper refused my money and told me that she was offering me the whole thing: a piece of bread, a few tomatoes, bananas, a tin box of flageolets, one of vegetable pâté, a piece of cheese, and a bar of chocolate.

     The church opposite is tall and narrow. We went up to its bell tower to see the bells, of course, but also the view. Then Petrus left me the keys and told me to join him at home when I was done. I usually take my time in churches, as in places that make you want to sit quietly and close your eyes (without sleeping).

     I finally went back to get my bag, say goodbye, and thank everyone who had so kindly welcomed me. I was, of course, invited to stay the night or longer if I wanted. Marius, who had also returned, invited me to come and see his flock. But it was still early in the afternoon, and I felt good about continuing on my way. So I took the stick (Spine) and the bag (Expiation) and got back on my way.

   The small village was spread out in length. Towards its end, particular smells came to my nostrils, and I suspected their origins. Someone was distilling nearby, I was sure. I looked around as I walked for clues—smoke, for example, or a stream of fresh water. But I didn’t have to look for long because, just on the side of my path, I passed in front of a small hut where some people were busy while others chatted around a table with many glasses, looking at the hearth full of flames under an alembic.

     As I passed, Valentin, the boiler, was going out to get wood from the customer’s trailer, which was filled with two large barrels and all kinds of pieces of wood: old boards, pieces of furniture, and a few logs. I was very happy to be invited inside, because the atmosphere was good, but also because I was really curious about the process.

     The small room was divided into two areas. On one side, the alembic was embedded in masonry with the hearth below. A notched wheel, a steering wheel, and a connecting rod turned the scraper paddles inside the copper tank to prevent the heated contents from burning. Nearby was a large water tank where the arm of the alembic was immersed to cool the alcoholic vapors.

     And then there was the friendly corner, just at the entrance: a table, a sofa, some chairs, lots of drinks, dry sausage, not-so-dry sausages, bacon, cold cuts, bread, a few bottles, and a wire hanging from the ceiling with a light bulb at the end above it all.

     I stood there asking all the questions I had. I had a lot of them, and Valentin patiently answered everything whenever his occupation as a distiller allowed. He also managed to balance this with chatting with the many people who stopped by. It was a real hub of village life. When the door was open and the street glowed red from the fire, people clearly knew it was a good time to stop in for a drink, a bite to eat, and a chat.

    The cars stopped and started again. There were bursts of laughter and loud voices. Valentin, though the host, never lost his concentration. Even while participating in the discussions, welcoming everyone, and offering glasses of little plum, little cherry, little mirabelle plum, or little house cognac—you see the scene—he never stopped monitoring his still. If everything looked like a good time, he was still very much at work. Adding a piece of wood from time to time, he told me the temperature must be gentle and constant. The slowness allows the aromas to develop, it seems. Judging by what I tasted, for sure, the guy was bringing out the aromas.

     I stayed to watch the first distillation come out. In this case, it was from 250 liters of grapes from the customer’s garden.

     When finished, in addition to seeing the process of emptying the tank and filling the distillate for the second distillation (to rise to 54 degrees of alcohol), I wanted to see the alembic emptied, to see the copper, and to observe the paddles that scrape its bottom while rotating. In short, I was curious. It was then that Valentin's wife, who lives across the street, offered me a shower. A hot shower! The pilgrim never refuses a shower, because a hot shower is not only refreshing but also unpredictable—you never know when the next one will be. And then, coming out all clean, she offered to wash my clothes as well, using the neighbor's washing machine. Valentin suggested that I stay the night so I could see and taste the finished product while allowing my laundry to dry.

     So, I spent the evening at their place—a beautiful Tzigane family, full of colors. Tzigane, yes, a word I was warned against because it is considered discriminatory. Tzigane means gypsy in the Gypsy language, but as I've learned multiple times, it’s a term used with pride within their own community. "We are Gypsies; we call ourselves Gypsies. The term Rroms, with two Rs, is a government policy term that we never use among ourselves," Valentin explained to me. I must admit, the relational dynamics between "gypsies" and sedentary people, between Roms and Rroms, escape me. The more I discover, the less I understand.

     On several occasions, I heard the term Gadjo Dilo used for me, pronounced with kindness and laughter. I understood the reference to the French film about a young Frenchman (Romain Duris, I think) traveling through Romania in search of Gypsy musicians. I vaguely remember the film, but one scene where the character cleans his host’s house while they are away stayed in my memory and made me laugh, as it did Valentin when I recounted it.

     Finally, the last drop of the distillation finished falling. We filled cans with the finished product. The customer was happy and left, even though he could have slept in his car for a few hours before driving—but, well.

    I spent a wonderful evening at Valentin's table. His house was as welcoming as his distillery, with many people coming and going. Neighbors, family—up to 18 of us gathered at one point before I finally retired to bed. In the morning, the silence contrasted sharply with the previous night. I hurried to drink the coffee that had just been offered because I wanted to go to church. It was Sunday morning, and Petrus was officiating. I had sent him a message the night before, explaining that I hadn’t managed to leave the village and, despite telling him I planned to continue, I had ended up staying at someone else’s house just 400 meters away. I never know how things will turn out, but so far, they always go well.

     I stayed in the church for four hours, meditating gently while the service was conducted. Long ago, I abandoned the idea of trying to follow what was happening—the language isn’t mine, and the liturgical traditions are different from those in which I grew up. Instead, I used the fervent atmosphere to deepen my meditation.

     At the church exit, after greeting and chatting with Petrus, someone offered me a large loaf of bread—special Lenten bread for the pre-Easter fasting period. Then a gentleman approached, saying he had noticed me during the morning, absorbed in stillness. He invited me to his house for lunch, and it was hard to refuse. I agreed, but only after attending a blessing ceremony for a newly furnished room in a house opposite the church, where I had been invited.

     I had attended similar ceremonies in other traditions but never among Orthodox Christians, so I was curious. The ceremony was brief, and I stayed longer because the daughter of the homeowner spoke French. She was happy to practice a little and asked about my journey. We chatted around the buffet set up for the guests. She was a college professor who also worked in an international call center, hence her proficiency in the language.

     Afterward, I went to the shepherd’s house for lunch. It was a simple, rudimentary home. He invited me to stay as long as I wanted—now or when I returned. He was a kind, rustic man who prepared me some farm eggs. We talked about religion, and my pilgrimage resonated deeply with him. He lived with extreme simplicity, and his perspective touched me. He was insistent that I stay, go see his goats (more than a hundred, many of them kids of the year), but I took my leave, eager to walk a bit.

    I returned to Valentin's to collect my backpack and resume my journey. But when I arrived, it was a party—loud music, smoking barbecue—the youngest's birthday. Valentin told me I couldn’t leave yet and urged me to stay a little longer. It was only after many toasts and conversations that I managed to leave. By then, the afternoon was already gone, and I laughed as I walked out, realizing it had taken me a day and a half to cover 800 meters! This somewhat answers the frequent question about how many kilometers I travel each day. And still, I could have stayed two weeks if I had wanted to, given how warmly I was invited.

     But I am a pilgrim. It’s the path I travel that defines me. I didn’t cover much ground that day. A few hundred meters before the next village, I found a pleasant spot to pitch my tent and quickly fell asleep. The next morning, not feeling well and with a bad headache, I stayed there all day. I left again the following day.

     Coming down from my bivouac site, I came across Paul, who was returning from dropping his children off at school. Paul is one of Valentin's neighbors, and we had spent a lot of time talking since he was one of the few at the time who spoke English. He was busy removing the old plaster from his house, right next to Valentin's. During his breaks, he would join us in front of the still.

     Surprised to see me again, I explained to him that I hadn’t walked the day before. After some discussion, we went our separate ways. I crossed the village, then the next one, where I saw Valentin leaning against his car, parked on the side of the road. "You were sick! Come to my house! Rest, recover, and then go again!" Valentin, genuinely concerned, took some time to let me leave. I had to explain that it was just a momentary discomfort and that I felt much better after a full day of rest. I truly appreciated his kindness, which went beyond a simple welcome. I don’t know if I can explain it—it feels like there’s something magical in these encounters.

    In another village, as I walked on the sidewalk, I found myself next to a little lady carrying a small bag of groceries. We walked at a similar pace, giving each of us time to discreetly observe the other. She must have been around 65 years old and still seemed vigorous. She wore a veil on her head. Then she spoke to me, seemingly curious about what I was doing there. I think she said something about my bag because she used the word "greu" (pronounced gréo), which means heavy—a word I know well. It’s the impression you get when you see my backpack. I often joke that when people ask how much it weighs, I respond that it weighs "too much, and then some."

    Walking slowly, I took out my phone to play a short pre-recorded introductory message that explains the essentials in a few moments. She stopped, seemingly shocked. "How long have you been walking?" she asked, using the translator I handed her. "Three and a half years, soon," I replied. The information struck her forcefully, and I saw a wave of emotion cross her face. "But how far will you go?" she asked. I smiled, not quite knowing how to answer, and pointed to my chest with my index finger. "I’m going there," I said. She looked at me and burst into tears.

     Thin. I hadn’t expected that. She put her face in her hands and cried. I hesitated, then gently placed my hand on her shoulder, consoling her with soft strokes. We stood there for a few moments. There was something beautiful in the situation—something magical. Her emotion didn’t seem negative; I even sensed a kind of peace. Finally, she smiled, wished me well, and gave me the customary blessing: "May God accompany you." I continued straight, while she turned right. A few hundred meters later, I turned around to look back at her. At the same moment, she turned as well, and from a distance, we waved. It was one of those surreal encounters I experience relatively often.

     In the next village, as I arrived at an intersection, a taxi was parked on the shoulder, blocking my pedestrian path. The driver spoke to me, asking where I was going. After a few moments, he invited me to his house for coffee and a break. I got into his taxi, and he assured me he would drop me back at the exact spot so I could say my journey was entirely on foot.

     Hari, as he introduced himself, has been a taxi driver since returning to Romania after spending a few years in England working in security to save money. He preferred to return home after saving enough to start his own business—a life path I encounter often here. We talked for two hours over coffee prepared by his wife. He explained that a woman living a few houses down had told him about a stranger in the village. That’s why he came to meet me. It was the lady I had met earlier. He also invited me to stay the night, but I declined. With a few hours of daylight left, I wanted to keep walking. True to his word, Hari dropped me off at the precise spot where we had met, and I continued on my way.

     I crossed the village and walked along the road. It was a major road I needed to follow for a little while longer before turning onto less trafficked side roads. Walking there was somewhat unpleasant, but I made good progress. The flat monotony of the path lent itself to inner contemplation. My body was occupied with its routine, leaving my mind free to wander.

   Time passes quickly like this, and so does the distance. It's when the body gets tired that I naturally look around for a suitable place to sit down and take a breather. Sitting on a stone at the edge of a small village, a gentleman, alerted by his dog’s incessant barking, came out from his small farm to meet me. After introductions, he asked if I needed anything and offered me milk from his farm. I couldn’t refuse. Fresh, organic milk. He filled a 2.5-liter bottle with milk, rich in flavor and freshly drawn from the tank in his small milking parlor.

     This is how surreal the journey becomes—the welcome I receive from the people I meet. It has been like this for more than three years now, and I can continue like this. Every day I walk, and every day I meet people, have these kinds of encounters. I will continue like this.

    The next day, at the entrance to a village, I was stopped by a man surrounded by a crowd of children. He was working on the fence of his small plot of land and, as usual, asked what I was doing there. I gave my standard response: pilgrim/French/not understand. Suddenly, I heard a woman’s voice say in French, "Ah, you're French, but what are you doing here? Have you eaten? Come inside the house, sit down." She gave me a plate of soup with a thick, two- or three-centimeter slice of bread that she baked herself in a wood-fired stove. Then the man cooked some potatoes, and we all ate together. This is how I met the family of Arabella, Christian, and their six children. I spent the night at their place.

    Two days later, while resting on a bench at the crossroads of a road and a street in a small village, a car stopped next to me. The driver rolled down his window and asked what I was doing there. He spoke both English and French, while his wife next to him spoke fluent German. Within a minute, Eugène, the driver, and his wife Héléna invited me to their home for lunch and a shower. Eugène was the priest of his village, and Héléna was a religious icon painter who had previously been a professional opera singer.

    The connection was instant. Eugène invited me to stay in his vacation home, a disused sheepfold in the hills 4 km from his house. He had set it up as a small retirement retreat. It was perfect for me because I could organize the delivery of my package from France to the nearby city and wait until it arrived, aligning my visit with Eugène’s schedule.

     I stayed there for three nights. The small wood stove in the room kept me warm. The space was simple: a bed, a table, a chair, a small shelf, and, at the foot of the bed, a piece of furniture with a television. Eugène came to see me every day, sometimes with Héléna, bringing food, sharing a glass of homemade wine, and chatting.

    I left with a superb memory of this couple, so evidently in love. Eugène, with his Santa Claus appearance, radiated kindness and honesty. Héléna, older but vibrant and brilliant, exuded the sensitivity and refinement of a true artist. As I left, I thought I could have stayed much longer. Eugène had even invited me to return and live in the sheepfold if I wanted.

     At the edge of the village where we had met, in the church where Eugène was officiating that morning and Héléna was singing (I couldn’t miss that), I stopped at the last house. Sitting on a bench against a fence, I pulled out my topographical map to plan my path. I wanted to avoid the main road and noticed a small river flowing in my direction. A dike beside it looked walkable, though its meanders would lengthen my route. Still, it was a better option than dealing with road traffic.

   As I sat there, a woman of about 70 emerged from her house. The dogs had, of course, announced my presence. She asked what I was doing there, so I played my pre-recorded introduction. She began to cry. "Oh no," I thought, "there’s nothing sad here." She stood before me, tears streaming down her small face, her gray hair tucked under a loosely tied scarf.

    I didn’t know what to do, but a wave of affection for this little lady moved me to act. I gently hugged her and smiled. Then, speaking softly in French, I said a few words—not because she would understand them, but because in that moment, it wasn’t the meaning of the words that mattered, but their tone and timing.

     So, the neighbor across the street comes out to ask what's going on. A very recognizable woman in her attire—the tight scarf on her head and the skirt, but above all, the colors. I spend a moment there explaining, discussing, and consoling before embarking on the dike which, according to the map, takes me to the city.

     The arrival at Targu Mureș takes place under pouring rain that hasn’t stopped for two days. I go directly to the guesthouse waiting for me. I plan to spend two days there; the package has already arrived, but the letter containing the bank card is late. That gives me time to go wash my sleeping bag.

    For this, I head to a dry cleaner in a large shopping mall. Cleaning a natural goose-down sleeping bag requires industrial dry cleaning. Many small dry cleaners are not equipped for this. After dropping it off, I visit the local tourist office to ask for some information, including a dentist's contact. Since I’m in town for a few days, I figure a quick dental check-up wouldn’t hurt. But, as you know, appointments aren’t easy to get quickly. It’s not the Tourist Office’s job to arrange this, but the staff member is helpful and offers to assist. He makes the calls—it’s easier in Romanian—and after a few minutes, the third dentist he contacts gives me an appointment for the next morning at 10 a.m. Miraculous, right?

   I go as planned, and to describe the atmosphere that continues to surround me, the dentist refuses to let me pay. I leave with numerous free samples as well.

    The next day, I retrieve my sleeping bag. I take the bus and arrive at the dry cleaner’s counter. The lady hands me my bag in a large transparent plastic cover—you know the kind. I’m immediately about to open it, but she stops me. She doesn’t speak English but seems to suggest I don’t need to open it now. Strange... I tell her I have a compression bag for the sleeping bag so it can fit neatly in my backpack with all the straps tightened. Much more practical for the bus. Yet something in her attitude raises my suspicion, so I decide to examine the sleeping bag right away to ensure everything is in order.

     Good thing I do! The bag is as dirty as when I brought it in. What’s more, it now has a bad smell—worse than before! So this, I realize, is the reason for her behavior. She didn’t wash the bag—or perhaps she tried, then gave up after seeing how much work it would take. Whatever the case, my sleeping bag is now in worse condition than when I dropped it off.

     She insists the smell is normal for goose feathers. But I know my sleeping bag well, having spent over 2,000 nights in it and had it professionally cleaned several times before. I show her the dark marks that prompted me to bring it in for cleaning in the first place. They are still there. She explains they didn’t come out in the wash, even though she “washed it twice to be sure!” I know I’m being scammed. She took the money (cleaning a sleeping bag is generally expensive) and is pretending everything is fine.

     Seeing there’s nothing more I can do or say, I resign myself and leave. I won’t be able to take it to another dry cleaner before my departure since I’ll have to leave the city soon. My budget doesn’t allow me to extend my stay in the guesthouse.

     Two days later, I left the city and pitched my tent near the river. I got ready to go to bed and climbed into my sleeping bag. I had dried it as best as possible above a radiator in the room I occupied. The smell was still there but slightly diminished. When I brought my feet inside, I felt something unfamiliar. A seam... There was a seam that my feet were rubbing against. Normally, there was none—or rather, the seams were designed to be discreet, both to sight and touch. But here, a new seam was present, on top or in place of the original one. After examining it, I noticed that a roughly made seam, clearly done with a sewing machine, ran along the length of the sleeping bag, cutting through all the compartments containing the precious goose down.

     I now understood that the scam wasn’t just the non-cleaning of the bag but the substitution and replacement of the original materials. You have to understand—a high-quality goose-down sleeping bag like this is very, very expensive. To sleep well, even at temperatures as low as -28°C this past winter, or -43°C in the Himalayan mountains, you need a bag you can trust. It can literally save your life.

     I had bought mine used from someone who had returned from an expedition to Everest. These days, many people, not necessarily mountaineers, make that climb as part of commercial expeditions. For example, company executives may do it as part of team-building exercises or self-challenging endeavors. Most of them will never undertake such an experience again, and after descending, they resell or give away their equipment. I was fortunate to acquire a sleeping bag like that and used it extensively for over 17 years. The bag had served me well and lost some of its quality over time. I could no longer rely on it for temperatures below -30°C, but for temperatures as low as -25°C or -28°C, with proper clothing, it worked fine.

    That night, in addition to the affront and the bad smell, I was cold. It wasn’t even below zero, but I had to wear warm clothes just to sleep. The feeling of having lost a reliable companion of many adventures—the sense that the bag had been desecrated—was painful at first. After some time, I consoled myself by imagining that some child might sleep under a super goose-down quilt next winter. It was just one less attachment for me to let go of.

     I share this story because, until now, I’ve only shared the good moments, the meetings, and the behaviors that could belong to the realm of fairy tales. It wouldn’t be honest to represent my story without also sharing the less positive experiences. This is the second time since my departure that I’ve had a bad experience. (The first, which I kept to myself, involved a grocer in a small village refusing to give me the correct change for a bill, claiming it was half its actual value.)

    This little misadventure isn’t pleasant, but honestly, compared to the three years I’ve tried to describe (which I can only partially convey despite my lengthy writing efforts), you’ll agree with me that this anecdote is insignificant, right? Or even compared to just the fortnight I told you about before... Or even just today, as I sit in my tent near the river, walking along its bank toward the Lipova Monastery.

     Resuming my writing, which dates back a little now, I reflect on the bread that was offered to me at the church yesterday, along with a bottle of liturgical red wine. Another loaf, also offered yesterday by a French-speaking baker in a village I passed through, came with a tour of his bakery, where I recharged my batteries over coffee and homemade tomato juice. A huge piece of goat cheese and hard-boiled eggs came from a shepherd’s wife I met the day before yesterday in the meadow near her village. Her husband had called her to say I was approaching. She gave me coffee, a hot bean soup, sautéed potatoes, and breaded soy patties while the eggs hardened.

     Oh, and the rest of the provisions I bought were thanks to the money a kind gentleman had given me. And yes, as I left the village yesterday, a man on a bike handed me a bag containing a jar of zacusca (so delicious!) and a pot of homemade plum jam.

      And you know what? I don't know about you, but here it feels like spring—or even summer. So yes, I will have to find a replacement in preparation for next winter, but for now, I don’t really need more than the little I have left. And I have time ahead of me. So everything is fine.

     Voilà for the news.

     I hope everything is going well for you.

     I wish you all the best.

     See you soon.

     Pretty P.S.:

     Two days after this misfortune, Gabriel—whom you know because I described my meeting with him and his shed in the mountains where I had stayed to rest—informed me that the Bucharest mountaineering club had collected money to buy a new sleeping bag so that I can continue to walk. Moving, right?
