The Windmill and Hill of Crosses

28/9/2021

0 Comments

 
    I walk and come across many places, people, magnificent trees—majestic ones even—and buildings... Walking gives you time to see, to approach slowly. It gives time for curiosity to form, for interest to be born.

    For example, this windmill. From afar, it seemed strange, then obvious. I couldn’t resist and went to knock on the door of the house next to it to ask if I could go see it, photograph it for you, and maybe step inside. Whaaaa! I felt like I was stepping into a mechanical wooden watch. Five levels of pulleys, toothed wheels, giant gears, and belts. And of course, stone grindstones.

    Although it was still operational about ten years ago, the mill no longer works. It was built in 1904 and is now too expensive to repair or even restore.
    What a pity. What a marvel of engineering as well as carpentry. I’m attaching photos that, as often, only partially do it justice...

     I walk even slower than before. I no longer have any concerns about distance—only direction, because I’m always heading somewhere—but I don’t care when I get there. I walk... Look... Rest... Walk again...

    The weather is getting much cooler. In just a few days, we’ve gone from 20 to 28 degrees Celsius during the day to between 9 and 14. I imagine winter is coming soon...

   The day before yesterday, I arrived in the small village of Krincinas. It was the birthday celebration of a local priest/poet, after whom the school is named. It was the director of this school—also the director of three others (budget cuts to the Lithuanian national education department!)—who welcomed me so kindly. He showed me around his school, asked the maintenance technician to turn on the hot water circuit so I could take a shower in the gym locker rooms, and then accompanied me on a short walk through the village park to show me a sulphurous water spring there.

     We parted ways after spending a few hours together, including time with the students, who I could sense really liked their school. It’s nice to see...

     After spending the night in a grove at the edge of a small unpaved road (very little noise from the occasional passing cars), I resumed my journey. Along the way, I passed by a place with about fifteen large crosses. I, of course, stopped to rest for a bit, sit on a bench, close my eyes, and meditate. I didn’t take any pictures, so you won’t see the place. Maybe I should have, because fifteen crucifixes aren’t planted for no reason.

     What I’m going to share now is probably well-known to most Europeans, but I don’t believe it’s familiar to many of my Indian, Nepalese, and other friends...

     This place was a commemoration site for an event as significant as something from the lives of Mahatma Gandhi or Nelson Mandela.

    On August 23, 1989, at 7 p.m., two million people (fewer, according to the "prefecture," of course, and much more, according to participants—obviously!) joined hands to form a 700-kilometer-long human chain. It was non-violent, fraternal, and stretched across the three Baltic countries—the three sisters (Lithuania, Latvia, Estonia)—connecting their capitals (Vilnius, Riga, Tallinn).

    The date was chosen to mark the anniversary of the secret Molotov–Ribbentrop Pact, where the Soviets and Nazis had agreed on the partition of Europe, leading to the annexation of the three countries by the Soviet Union. This human chain was an act of resistance, a revolt, or at least activism, against the occupation.

    These kinds of actions move me deeply. Truly. Tyranny undermined by benevolent brotherhood, without violence. The undeniable power of non-violent collective refusal...

    You can find plenty of information on the internet about what’s called “The Baltic Way,” which is now classified by UNESCO in the Register of the Memory of the World.

     I should have taken a photo...

    And then I arrived in the small town of Pasvalys for the town's annual party. Am I not really lucky! Small city. A very warm little party. I arrived just in time to listen to a woman singing, and I’m attaching the audio file to this message. I found it very pretty. Sad but pretty. Obviously, I don’t understand a word of the song, but the whole performance seems to me like a lament or an observation of the difficulties of life. Of course, this is just my personal interpretation, based on the emotion and the timbre of a voice in a language unknown to me. I sat and watched her sing, accompanied by her friend on guitar.

     And then I walked around the fair. Friendly. Truly. Simple and friendly...

    A painter approached me to ask me what, why, when, and finally where I was sleeping. He told me to come and see him if the priest couldn’t accommodate me. When I came back from the very beautiful church, empty-handed, he was a little drunk—not clumsy drunk, but drunk enough to shout over the general hubbub, which you can imagine. He called out to someone, who shouted to another, who shouted to yet another person, until finally, the last man came and invited me to his hotel. Graciously. Any room is comfortable at this price! And then—a hot shower, phone charging...

     I went to meditate a bit, and then to have dinner. When it came time to pay, the person behind me in the queue walked ahead and offered to pay for my meal! He heard that I was a pilgrim and wanted to participate in my adventure.

     I am in the dreamlike sweetness of my journey. Wait—it’s not over!

    The next morning, I went to church again. The priest, who now knew a little better who this backpack-wearing wanderer with a stick was, came toward me, shook my hand energetically, and gave me a big smile. He thanked me and left. In my hand was 100 euros. I was puzzled—he didn’t even give me time to respond.

    I left the church and went to eat before continuing my way. At the restaurant in the square, I ordered and waited, looking at the topographic map on my phone, which I had plugged into an outlet nearby. The restaurant owner came to talk with me, holding a bottle of cognac and two small glasses. He was an interesting man—passionate, a hot air ballooning champion, and a former resistance fighter. I enjoyed our conversation—and the cognac!

    When I picked up my bag to leave, the waitress told me I didn’t have to pay. The bill was covered by the house.

    Do you understand why I doubt my reality? Why I am so moved by my experience with others? With strangers?

    Finally, just before saying goodbye, I wanted to tell you that yesterday I passed through the Hill of Crosses. It is also a place of resistance—religious resistance, in a way. This hill is covered with more than 250,000 crucifixes. Crucifixes planted, heaped, hanging from others, clustered together... Destroyed by the Soviets, rebuilt, destroyed again, and rebuilt once more.

    The place is intense. Seriously. I was worried it might feel spooky or creepy, but instead, I had one of my deepest meditations there. If you ever pass by on your journey, don’t hesitate to stop. Even if only out of curiosity.

    Voilà. It’s time for me to go.

    I am 50 km from Šiluva, my next and fourth destination...

    Lots of good things for you.

    Goodbye...

Ask Bilbo!

23/8/2021

0 Comments

 
     ​I know, it hasn’t been that long since I gave you my latest news. But I have to share what seems so important to me in my journey. I know I am redundant, and yet each time again, I am touched and moved by this kindness that welcomes me.

     I must begin by telling you that the last days since I left Aglona have been wonderful. I have been alone and in silence since resuming my walk. The intermittent rain refreshes pleasantly this end of summer. However, it is not too important to hinder my progress. When the rain is really too strong, I stop, put up the tent and take refuge there, but it’s not that often. A few days ago though, I got a downpour that was Hollywood chewing gum level event, if you remember the nineties advertisement. It was so huge, so tropical shower level that it becomes fun in itself. You no longer have to worry about getting wet, it’s so impossible not to that it’s no longer a problem.

    So it’s true that the atmosphere is really humid, but the good in that is that it attracts mushrooms which end up throwing themselves at my feet begging me to pick them up (yes like this). That’s awesome! I eat raw Boletus Edulis, because they are so fresh that there is no need to cook them. Which is good because I have nothing to cook anymore from a long time now. Yesterday I found the flesh of a big champagne cork shape one really sweet. It surprised me. Crunchy and sweet. Happiness...

    And then I also collect Chanterelles. I keep them in a bag and try to dry them a bit as much and as often as possible. I always end up being invited by someone to cook them...

     And then I arrived in this village where a grocery store was at the entrance. It was good because I needed to fill my backpack with food again. I did my shopping there, paid for everything, and the lady in charge of the grocery store asked me where I was coming from and what I was doing. She then offered to charge my phone if I needed, and we chatted.

     It’s crazy how what I do—walking like this, a long pilgrimage, a long-lasting spiritual discipline—can inspire or make sense to the people I meet. It is even disturbing. My approach, however very particular, very surprising, very unusual, very incongruous even, turns out to be full of meaning also for those I meet... Full of meaning and nourishing too. Strange, isn’t it? There is even a kind of envy that I feel, a craving. As if I were realizing in this journey what many would like to, what many are starving for... I guess that’s why having the rule to take the time to share with whoever is interested is important.

     After a while, she kindly offered me a coffee, which I gratefully accepted, because coffee is good! Especially when it’s offered like this. Of course, the coffee was served with many biscuits, chocolate, and white cheese bars sweetened and coated with chocolate or caramel (it’s pasty, somewhere between hard cream and fresh cheese, and sweet. I like it...).

   We chatted using Google Translate until a client, an English teacher at the village college, arrived. So we chatted in English. Nice exchange.

     Of the few new customers who passed by during my break at the grocery store, a dozen I would say, I had a friendly exchange with ten. More than half spoke with me or with the grocer, who told them about what we had already discussed. Four of them offered me their phone numbers in case I needed anything or help on my way.

     Before going to sleep near the village lake, the grocer gave me a bag of tomatoes and cucumbers (the Baltic countries seem to have an impressive addiction to cucumbers... Small cucumbers, like big pickles... Over the past month, there hasn’t been a single day I’ve spent without cucumbers...).

     I spent four hours in her shop, chatting with her while she worked or with her customers. It was really relaxed, and I managed to recharge my phone and power banks. Great! I gave her the bag of Boletus edulis mushrooms and chanterelles that I had picked that morning. It felt completely natural to give them to her. Then we said goodbye.

     The evening was good! A swim in the lake, washing myself and my clothes. A beautiful night without rain (which is nice because the sound of rain on the tent canvas can be really loud!). And this morning, I resumed my journey.

     In the afternoon, I arrived at a small country road—unpaved, with no tarmac—where I noticed someone walking about 100 meters ahead of me. It was a lady. Elderly. Old, but still walking briskly (87 years old! Respect!). It took me nearly a kilometer to catch up and walk alongside her. I could immediately tell she was a character—a nice one.

    When she heard someone approaching behind her (I had deliberately rubbed my foot on the ground to make a little noise, so as not to startle her), she turned around with a warm smile, not a trace of fear or surprise. That alone says a lot.

     We introduced ourselves and decided to walk together to her house, half a kilometer away. We had not gone 50 meters before I inherited one of the bags of tomatoes from her garden, which she carried in each hand.

    Arriving in front of her house, I was invited in for a coffee. I gladly accepted! A good cup of coffee never hurts! (French proverb—or if not, it should become one soon...).

    Watch the little movie I made—sorry, it’s in French; I didn’t think about it at the time. But aside from showing the beautiful place where she and her husband live, you can see her smile, her enthusiasm, her kindness. Notice the touching gesture of the small tablecloth she placed on the table for me, to hold the cup of coffee, along with a few slices of cheese on a plate, homemade donuts, and the white cheese treats I mentioned before.

     Then I met her husband, as kind as she was.

    I was invited to stay the night.
    I was invited to use the sauna and shower.
   I was invited to visit the property—a superb estate that belonged to her grandparents. It included a house, a barn, a garage, a henhouse, a sauna, and toilets behind the barn. There was a magnificent vegetable garden with enormous raspberries and blackcurrants. By the way, the homemade blackcurrant jam I ate with evening porridge was delicious.

    Even the grass was soft and comfortable to walk on barefoot—something I was invited to do almost immediately.

    She told me that tomorrow morning, before I leave, we will eat the mushrooms she collected, with potatoes. How does she know it’s my favorite dish at the moment—fresh mushrooms and potatoes, seriously! Ask Bilbo!!!
    And then, go look at the pictures—come on, watch that splendid breakfast! ??

    Finally, her husband showed me his workshop and his neatly arranged wood; she showed me her sober and simple interior. He showed me the sauna; she showed me the henhouse...

    I’m falling asleep as I write these words... The evening meal, the sauna, the comfortably firm sofa bed, the faint smell of wood fire coming from the burner...

    Once again, these people touch me and move me with the warmth of their welcome, the smiles in their kind eyes, and the care they show me, even though I’m a total stranger, completely unknown to them.

Aglona

17/8/2021

0 Comments

 
      Hello to everyone,

      It’s time to move on.
     The festival here in Aglona has ended.

   It was huge, and even though far fewer people came this year, the entire event was still impressive. Security was tight, with police, the army, secret services, TV, radio, journalists, and everything else you'd expect from a festival important enough to draw the country’s president and many VIPs.

     One of the most special parts was the choir singing every day for a week beforehand. Even if you’re not a fan of religious music, hearing those voices live, right in front of you, is deeply moving.

    The last two days were crowded and busy, especially Sunday from 8 a.m. to 2 p.m., but just two hours after it ended, the entire place was empty. All the cars and buses were gone, military tents dismantled, civil security tents packed away, cameras, sound systems, and lights all removed, food trucks vanished. Everything became quiet again.

    I spent the next day organizing myself—saying goodbye to the priests who so kindly welcomed me, buying food for my journey, washing my clothes, and enjoying a hot shower in the monastery to be fresh and ready to leave in the morning.

     Aglona, in Latvia, is a beautiful place. There’s a Basilica dedicated to Maria, set in the middle of a lovely park with a lake and a spring. The water from the spring is so good—really fresh, not cold, but refreshing and soft, with a great taste. I drink far more than I need every time I go to fill my bottle. It’s that nice. Of course, I’m not the only one. One of the priests, smiling, told me there are more people coming to fill their bottles at the spring than there are visiting the Maria icon! But since the spring is sacred and said to be miraculous, it’s all good.

     The journey here was, as always, wonderful. I met kind people (including a truly sweet dog who welcomed me in such a heartwarming way. She barked, yapped, and moaned the way dogs do when they’re happy to see someone. It felt like I was her master returning after a long absence. She moved me deeply with her softness and gentleness, and we walked together for two hours before she returned home. Such a beautiful encounter).

    I was also invited to stay for two days in a small town just after crossing the border. It was amazing to have warm, homemade meals prepared by my host, who said she wanted me to feel at home—and I did. She was such a lovely person, a former doctor in epidemiology for 16 years before working at customs. You can imagine how fascinating our conversations were!

     This has been a truly wonderful time.

     But you might get bored if I describe again how wonderful it is to walk through unknown places and feel so welcomed. So I think I’ll skip that part...

    Instead, I’ll tell you about my plans for what’s next: I plan to wait until this evening to decide where I’ll go next.

    Let me explain, because I get this question a lot. I always focus on my destination, and only my destination, until I finish my visit to that place I set out to reach. I stay dedicated to it, fully immersed in the experience. Then (and here’s a playful word twist—“end/and”), once the visit is over, I decide what’s next.

    This approach gives me the freedom to wait until the last moment. Maybe someone will suggest a beautiful place nearby that I hadn’t considered. In that case, I can make it my next destination. If no one suggests anything, I have a few ideas in mind and choose one based on logic and my current location.

    This creates a very satisfying sense of liberty. It feels like everything is open and possible, that I don’t even have to think about it until the last moment, and then it can be anything. It feels good. Simple. And once the choice is made, it becomes my goal. I don’t care how long it will take, or what the exact path will be. I just go. I put one foot in front of the other and repeat, repeat, repeat. Whatever I’m meant to see, I’ll see. Whoever I’m meant to meet, I’ll meet. Whatever challenges arise, I’ll face them. And here’s something I can assure you: I’m confident that everything will turn out fine.

    Confidence is a delicious warmth, and my experiences over these past 11 months have given me that—a warm and fulfilling confidence that everything is and will be good.

    Isn’t it beautiful to live this way, especially in these times? I wish I could share the feeling with you more fully, but words seem so weak compared to what I feel.

    I wish you happiness and well-being.
    Happiness is a duty—our first duty!

    Sincerely,
    Emmanuel

    Post scriptum:
    You know what? Last week, I found around 700 grams of chanterelles (Cantharellus cibarius or girolles in French). They were so tasty! We ate them together—four people who had invited me to share their meal by the lakeside. It was perfect because I had no way to cook them, and they had everything needed. They seemed to love mushrooms as much as I do, and they knew that the first chanterelles of the year are especially delicious.

     But that’s not even the best news. Okay, let’s agree that’s great news. But the better news is this: just before I arrived in Aglona, I found a Boletus edulis. A small one, shaped like a champagne cork. I left it there because it was all alone, hoping it will grow into a big family. But do you see my point? It’s mid-August, and there are already Boletus edulis popping up—not even in the woods, but right in the middle of the path! If that’s not a sign of good things in life, I don’t know what is.

    Post scriptum 2:
   I’m now headed toward Šiluvos atlaidai in Lithuania, west-southwest from where I am. On the way—about 50 km from my destination—I plan to visit the Hill of Crosses, which has caught my interest.

   I left Aglona after lunch today, walking until a heavy rain came down, forcing me to stop. I pitched my tent and tried to “dry” whatever I had on me. I’ll start again tomorrow!

Vilnius

6/7/2021

0 Comments

 
     ​I know, I should have told you about Vilnius, my second destination. It's already been a week since I left the place...
    I would have shared my arrival, and all the people I met on my way since my last update. So many nice people. Always. Only.

     For example, I would have told you about this lovely elderly couple, Maceliai and Vingriu. I say "elderly," but honestly, they felt so young in spirit. He, for instance, had the energy and enthusiasm of a supercharged teenager, practically hopping as he spoke. Both of them had bright, keen eyes. It was such a nice meeting.

    From the street, I asked them for water. He came straight to the gate, opened it, and invited me in—all smiles. And what a smile! You can see it in the photos—genuine happiness! They offered me tea, and soon fresh cheese appeared, pressed that very morning, along with honey (forest flowers), bread, and pickles.

    We talked, though it wasn't easy since they didn’t speak a word of English, but were fluent in German and Russian. They were from that generation that doesn’t give a phone translator enough time to work—reading translations aloud as they appear, causing the phone to misunderstand and start translating again, erasing the previous text. After a few tries, we mostly gave up on the translation app, but we still managed to have a great conversation.

     He showed me an ultralight plane he built himself—including the plans! Then he gave me a tour of his workshops: first the mechanical one, with a milling machine, lathe, and welding tools; then the woodshop, equipped with a router, planer, jointer, and bandsaw. Next came his greenhouse and his homemade sauna, heated with a wood stove. Everything at his home had his personal touch, including a visit to his beehives.

     The man must be hyperactive, a genius, and incredibly skilled with his hands. Everything was simple yet impressive. He even offered me a shower in a small building in his garden, where the water tank was made from a repurposed Russian bomb shell that holds 3,000 liters, heated naturally by the sun. With the week’s temperatures at 35°C, you can imagine how welcome that shower was!

     They were deeply connected to nature—everything organic and healing. He showed me a small wooden hut designed for naps. The "bed" was actually two beehives, with the roof made from two more. You’re surrounded by bees, enveloped in the soothing scents of wax, honey, pollen, propolis, and pinewood, while the buzzing sound of thousands of bees hums like a lullaby. You can even feel the subtle vibrations, as if the whole hut is purring. He even built a periscope so you can observe the activity inside the beehives while you drift off, counting bees instead of sheep! It's supposed to be healing.

     I don’t know if it works, but I can say this: a nap there must be amazing, and I’d love to be as healthy as he is at 77 years old!

     They also showed me a pagan ceremony they participate in—shamanic practices around a fire with songs and tambourines.

      It was truly a beautiful encounter.

     Or there’s Rasa—how I ended up in her garden is a story of its own. I had lost my track in the forest and, being too lazy to retrace my steps, I decided to just keep going, thinking, "There are no mistakes, after all—the path I take becomes my path." A bit of high philosophy that ultimately led to me crossing a river on a fallen tree trunk and battling my way up a steep riverbank through tall grass, nettles, and brambles. This adventure ended with me stumbling into the back of her property.

     After asking for water, she offered me coffee, then lunch. Later, to make myself useful, I mowed her garden lawn with her mower, clearing it up nicely before we sat back down on her veranda to chat. I ended up staying the night, and since it poured rain the next day, she took me for a drive in her car to visit at least six beautiful spots I would have passed without noticing otherwise.

     On the way back, we stopped at a wine shop where I managed to find some Côte du Rhône AOP, which paired perfectly with the “real cheese” you might remember from my previous update. That evening, we enjoyed the wine on her veranda while her neighbor joined us for a chat. It was a delightful French-Lithuanian evening.

     Rasa and I stayed in touch, and we met again in Vilnius a week or so later. It turned out the countryside house where I met her was her holiday home. She normally lives in Vilnius, works in a bank, and is very connected—she even has one of those smartwatches that keeps her updated on everything happening on her phone. It’s impressive how efficient she is.

     I’d also tell you about another couple who offered me dinner and a place to stay just a few days later, which turned out to be perfect timing because the weather forecast predicted a massive storm. And I mean massive—at one point, my phone, even in flight mode, emitted a warning buzz, displaying an alert directly from the government. That’s when you realize flight mode is just a software setting, not a physical switch!

    Even though the alert was in Lithuanian, it was clear enough: bad weather was on the way. Camping in the forest during a storm isn’t exactly the safest idea—imagine what protection a tent offers against falling branches! So, staying in their beautiful garden was not only safer but also lovely. We spent the evening watching the lightning and talking, enjoying the storm from a safe and cozy vantage point.

     So I finally arrived in Vilnius, and I could have told you about my first night camping near the Gate of Dawn, where a morning rainstorm turned into a river that flooded my tent, soaking everything—my belongings and me included. I could have told you how I spent four hours the next day drying everything out, especially my battery packs, which were literally dripping. I was lucky enough to find an air dryer and used it on low heat to avoid frying them. (The internet says it’s a terrible idea to let your battery packs get wet—it kills them, apparently, and should never happen. So, I guess I’m really lucky because all of mine survived! I would have been especially disappointed to lose the last one, a gift from someone on my way, with such excellent capacity and quality. This one might even be waterproof!)

    I could have told you about Our Lady of the Gate, the Basilica, or the beautiful churches scattered throughout the town—or just about the town itself.

    I could have also told you how I was invited to a convent near the Gate of Dawn, where I met some wonderful sisters and stayed for four days. They gave me the time and space I needed to take care of everything I had to do.

     There are so many things I could have shared, and I guess I’ve mentioned a little of it now. But what I really want to tell you about is something I witnessed—something that left a strong impression on me.

    One Saturday evening, I was coming back from a dinner I had prepared with Rasa at her place in Vilnius. It was a way to thank her for her warm hospitality and to enjoy a "French dinner" that we cooked together (though I really screwed it up). I also had the chance to meet her friends, which was another lovely experience.

     So, it was quite late, around 11:30 p.m., and I was sitting on a tram in the suburbs of the capital, replaying in my head how the spinach tourte had gone so wrong, when I saw what I’m about to describe.

    The tram had stopped, and more people were getting in than getting out. I was standing in the middle, with only a few seats still available—two of them right in front of me. A young guy, who had just boarded through the front door, spotted the seats and immediately picked up his pace, eager to claim them before anyone else. So far, it was a completely ordinary scene, the kind you see all the time. He was probably around 19 years old, chatting noisily but cheerfully with his friend, as young people often do on a Saturday night.

    At first, I didn’t pay much attention and turned my gaze toward the rear of the tram, where the back door was letting in a few more passengers. And there, the same situation was unfolding. Two Afro-Lithuanian guys, dressed in the full rapper-style outfit—caps, T-shirts, gold chains, the whole look—had also spotted the same seats...

    At that moment, it was obvious—I was about to witness a social tension event. You know, one of those moments you see coming. Both parties had that seat in mind, both sped up to claim it for themselves. They accelerated, and suddenly everything seemed to play out in slow motion. My head went from right to left, and back again, faster as they got closer, and then—bing! Of course, they reached the spot at the same time. Standing face to face, just a foot apart, one with his hand on the back of the seat, the other gripping the vertical bar in front of it.

    This is the moment where you'd expect frustration to explode. You know the scenario: the closer someone gets to their goal, the higher the expectation; the higher the expectation, the worse the frustration when they’re deprived. Basic psychology! And obviously, one of them wasn’t going to sit. Someone was going to be frustrated!

   It felt like such a pivotal moment to watch—something that could reveal so much about the social dynamics of the place... about human behavior. And, let’s be honest, maybe I’d learn a few colorful swear words in the process.

    I projected my expectations, drawing parallels. Paris suburbs, Saturday night, young people on public transport... I braced myself for at least some incivility, if not outright violence. Verbal aggression seemed like the bare minimum I could expect.

    But no.

    They stood there, facing each other, realized the situation, and—together, at the same time—offered the seat to the other!
    "You first!"
    "No, you!"
    "Please, it's okay, take it!"
    "Are you sure?"
    "Yes, yes, go ahead!"

    And then, one of them sat down with his friend, while the other held out his fist for a friendly bump. You know the gesture: Hey, give me five! Yeah, bro!

    They smiled. They were relaxed. They were human.

   I was impressed. How can I describe it? I felt comfortable, confident, and at ease on public transport in the suburbs of the capital, late on a Saturday night, surrounded by a big group of young people, some of whom were lightly tipsy.

     Now, you might not think this is a big deal. I mean, sure, you could argue that this should just be normal. But in my experience—at least mine—for far less than this, I’ve seen middle fingers raised and unsavory comments about people’s mothers thrown around.

    It’s hard for me to tell if I’m just hypersensitive to this now. Spending ten months mostly alone, following a discipline where most of my time is spent in meditation, contemplation, prayer, or the recitation of mantras as part of my yoga practice—all of it aimed at what we often call spirituality—might have changed how I see the world.

    I’m aware of this shift, but I can’t measure its extent. I know I’m not objective, but I don’t know how much I’ve changed. So many things I witness now seem surreal at first, but because they happen every day, they’ve become my new normal. Meanwhile, what’s conventionally considered "normal" often feels unbearably sad in comparison.

    I’m not sure what’s more significant here: the scene itself or my perception of it. What’s certain is that my experience of pilgrimage—my Sadhana—has altered me.

    One thing I know for sure: it makes me happy. It makes me feel good. It gives me a sense of reconciliation with humanity, a stark contrast to the pervasive fear mainstream media loves to sell. The message of the world is always “Be careful. Outside is dangerous.”

    But my experience?
    People are nice.

    So be confident. Be trusting. Be grateful, be kind. And love… laugh… smile. Life is beautiful, in a beautiful world with beautiful people. Even if you sleep outside every day, cannot wash yourself every day, cannot wash your clothes every week, don’t have hot coffee in the morning, or cannot dry yourself when it’s raining… it’s still nice to live. (So imagine when you have a house, a car, a family around you, a hot shower, a coffee machine...).

    Ok. I swear I didn’t drink. Or smoke. Or anything else…

    Let me tell you another one!

    Two days ago, as I was on my way to Aglona in Latvia (my next and third destination), I arrived in a small town and came across a cute church bordered by a small park with a little river running around it. A really beautiful place. I decided to stop for a while and splash myself in the river. So I went down the alley to get closer, and I saw a bank full of people sitting there.

    No problem, I stopped about 20 meters away, dropped my backpack, and sat on it to take my shoes off. Before I could even do that, one of the three guys called out to me, waving a bottle of vodka and saying something that could have been “hey heu euuuu ah beee labbbeu heuuu”... though I can’t be entirely sure of the translation.

   This is another good example of self-projection—where I, like many others, find myself assuming things. I thought, Ah, just a bunch of homeless, drunken guys squatting in the park. But I got over that quickly. I reminded myself that I, too, might look like someone who sleeps outside, doesn’t wash every day, and so on. You see what I mean…

    So I said hello and accepted their offer to join them, figuring I could explain a bit about myself. They turned out to be really jovial, sympathetic, and friendly. I hadn’t even started introducing myself—my name or the fact that I was a pilgrim—when one of them handed me a shot of vodka.

    Okay… I took it and began my explanation. Before I could finish—pretty quickly, I might add—I had to politely refuse the fourth shot.

    It could have ended there: just some drunk people sharing drinks with a stranger who probably looked like one of them. But then, a woman passed by on the path, and they gave her the same warm welcome, offering her a drink as well.

    I had serious doubts about this. I thought, This is a bad idea. There’s no way she’ll engage with them—she’ll just walk by with an air of disdain. But to my surprise, she stopped. She talked with them a bit and even found out that they were celebrating one of their birthdays. And then—they hugged.

    No way. They must have known each other. Surely, I thought, they were longtime acquaintances.

    But no. This same thing happened three more times while I stood there with them. Two more people joined and had a shot of vodka with them. Another woman even sat down with them for a while.

    After splashing myself in the river (which they assured me was totally fine to do), one of the men offered to introduce me to the priest of the church. He knew where the priest lived, just a few hundred meters away, and thought maybe I could find accommodation there. He came with me, and that’s how I left this merry group of people—people I’m now ashamed to have initially judged as drunken homeless.

    Okay, sure, they were drunk. And yes, they were drinking outside in a park. But the way they made me feel—like we were neighbors who had known each other for years—was humbling.

    That’s it. Maybe it doesn’t seem like much to you. But for me, here, in my nearly ten months of walking now, I can assure you I’m taking these small, seemingly insignificant things as truly important—little everyday miracles. And even though I’m not particularly fond of vodka, I gladly drank with them from their glass. I was happy to answer whatever questions they asked, happy to share moments with total strangers I probably would have ignored in my “normal” life.

    And when I left, I felt genuinely happy. A big smile on my face... (The vodka might have helped with that because, honestly, I’m really not used to it...).

    Today, I was supposed to get moving since yesterday was already a day off! But it’s raining hard. Really hard. So I decided to stay in my tent again. I have enough water in my bottles, enough food, and enough battery to write a long message to give you an update!

   And you know what? I just received a message from Rasa. She’s planning to take her car and meet me this weekend by a lake I’ll be passing on my way—just like that. Just to spend an afternoon on the shore with a guy she’s only met twice...

    I hope you’re well and happy.
    And I hope my update isn’t too long...
    But I needed to say this, and I did...

Arrival in Lithuania

I’m afraid you might think I’m exaggerating or embellishing things, or maybe that I ate the wrong mushroom, or even that I’m having some kind of psychological breakdown—I don’t know.      The landscape is overwhelmingly beautiful: vast forests, crystal-clear lakes, and charming little rivers. Seriously, wait until you see the pictures—you’ll understand!

     As the weather is clearly turning to summer, the walking can get sweaty. The sun shines brightly from 3:30 a.m. to 10:30 p.m., and it’s around 28°C. So yes, it feels hot while walking. But can you imagine? So far, I’ve been able to wash, swim, or splash myself every single day in places so idyllic you’d think they were taken from a summer vacation brochure or a postcard—no, better than postcards! The water is crystal clear and wonderfully refreshing, cool enough to take all the heat out of your body but not so cold as to leave you shivering.

     The ground here is mainly sandy, whether on the roads or forest trails. Taking a swim, washing, or rinsing clothes (without soap, of course, to protect the environment—but with fine, soft sand that scrubs gently) has been such a pure pleasure that it fills me with joy. At least, it does for me. These stunning spots are everywhere, as lakes range from several kilometers wide to just a few hundred meters, and I come across one every 5 km at most.

     You see?

     Now, to complete the picture, imagine the air filled with the scent of pine resin, mixed with the intoxicating fragrances of the season. Every day, I pass dozens of blooming lilacs, carpets of lily of the valley with a scent so strong it’s almost overwhelming, and countless flowering trees, bushes, and meadows. Every open field between forests is covered in flowers!

     You might think I’m being overly enthusiastic. But I grew up in the countryside; I know what flowered meadows are. And yet, I’m amazed by what I’m seeing here. Just wait until you see the pictures—really, the whole picture! No wonder the place where I entered Lithuania is a national park and a popular tourist destination for Russians, Belarusians, and Ukrainians.

     And the wildlife! The mosquitoes are so in love with French cuisine—utterly enthusiastic about French gastronomy! 

    And the birds! My God, the birds! There are still swallows here, you know. And robins. And cuckoos. And... well, I’ll stop now. You get the idea.

     Now, about the people!
    You might think that no country can have it all—the beauty, the landscapes—and also have kind, welcoming people. That would be too much, wouldn’t it? Unfair! Unrealistic! But you’d be completely wrong. Totally wrong.

     At first, even I didn’t believe it. After a few encounters, I thought, “This is too much—it can’t be real.” I half expected a hidden camera to pop out or something. But let me give you some examples...

     The first village I crossed was a small one, just a few kilometers past the border. I was walking along the road/path out of the forest, taking in the charm of a new country, with a touristy air about me, my nose in the air, admiring the scenery. Most of the houses are made of wood. Many don’t even have fences, and if they do, they’re often beautifully crafted from wood.

  The roads are mostly dirt, which means sand—brown sand, yellow sand, black sand... Comfortable for walking, let’s say, but dusty. Only a few are paved, and honestly, that’s nice because cars drive slower (though dustier, too—I just step aside and position myself with the wind to avoid being coated in it).

At that point, I honestly thought I was about to be told I was on a private path or trespassing. So you can imagine how tenuous our conversations were. But still, they showed me warm hospitality and genuine kindness. The woman even called her son to help translate a bit. And once the language pack was downloaded, everything became much easier, of course.

A wooden house flanked by two buildings: one a cute barn and the other a series of stables, chicken coops, and workshops you’d expect to find on a farm. In front of it all was a tiny, picturesque house, and behind, down their backyard, lay a lake. The view was just breathtaking. God... It was a beautiful arrival.

We tried to communicate as best as we could, at least enough to establish the basics: that I was a Frenchman who had arrived here on foot, and for him to tell me that it was great! We shook hands, and then he left. I stayed there for a moment, wondering what had just happened.

Kornelija—that’s her name—was with her older sister and her mother. She was elegantly dressed in a beautiful summer dress, which she had chosen especially for church that day to please her mother.

Even though I sensed she could be stern or severe, her warm openness and enthusiasm made it nearly impossible to say anything but "yes" to whatever she asked.

I politely declined, explaining that I preferred to carry it myself—it’s part of my journey, my karma. She introduced me to her mother, who was driving, got back in the car, and left, waving enthusiastically as they drove off.

They have a brilliance about them. And I’m not just talking about Kornelija studying pure mathematics, or her youngest brother studying political science, or the fact that they are all fully fluent in English, Norwegian, and Russian.      They talk to each other! It’s like they genuinely care about what the other says! They hold hands and close their eyes to pray before meals. They smile at each other and hug to say hello or goodbye. They ask their mother for blessings, which she gives by tracing a small cross on their foreheads with her thumb. They care for each other—it’s obvious.

     This isn’t my world. I come from somewhere else entirely. But it’s a wonderful world, and they invited me into it so completely, so trustingly, so gently. It felt surreal. It couldn’t be real. And yet it was—for two magical days.

A traditional sauna—jumping into the river behind the garden to cool off when it got too hot and sweaty. We covered our bodies in a half-clay, half-vase paste. He packed his camping gear, as well as Kornelija’s, who had left in the morning to meet another brother in the next town. Ignas assured me we absolutely had to meet him before I left. That brother was walking toward us, planning to join us at our campsite for the night.

     We spent the evening and part of the night sitting around a fire, sharing stories and laughter after a lovely dinner.

     In the morning, we bathed together in a nearby lake, went to see a farmer to get some "real cheese" for the French guy (me), and said our goodbyes.

     Ignas stayed with me the whole day again so we could walk together to the next town and talk along the way.

     Now, if I tell you that the town we’re in is one of the most beautiful and peaceful towns I’ve ever seen, would you believe me? It’s surrounded by forest, with forests even in the town center, along with lakes and a river... It’s beautiful and green. You can swim in the lake if you want—it's allowed, and the municipality keeps it clean for that purpose.

And we stayed the next day as well, except for the morning when I got up early to run some errands: getting money, having coffee, taking a little tour of this beautiful town, and shopping for new shorts or trousers because my constant walking had worn them out really fast.

    Later, we met again in town, had more coffee, visited a special second-hand shop, and then went to the library to make a kind of paper to show people along my way, introducing myself in Lithuanian. Afterward, we met his cousin and went to a restaurant together, then took a long walk through the town park, talking the entire way. Finally, we returned to the flat, where I am now in bed, ready to sleep and writing this message.

     Ignas went out for the evening. I wanted to go to bed early to start my journey early tomorrow. He had some plans to figure out for his next move and the coming weeks. So we said our goodbyes in case I leave tomorrow morning without seeing him again.

     Before I leave, I need to switch off the flat’s utilities: turn off the main water tap, take out the trash, close the windows, lock the door, and put the key in the mailbox. Then I’ll go.

     I’m alone in the flat of people I only met this past Sunday. And you know what? I already miss them...

     Am I just that lucky? Or blessed? Or maybe both? But seriously, think about it: how many people do you know who would give their home for the night to a total stranger who doesn’t even speak their language?

     I feel like I’m living in a dream. A beautiful dream. The only way it could get better would be if the mosquitoes became vegetarian!

     And honestly, I’m starting to believe it could happen. Why not?

Black coffee. A hot meal—not just bread, butter, cheese, tomato, or banana this time. (She’s a great cook, I must admit.)
     And conversations.
     Silence.
     More conversations.

     Another way of living. Another way of seeing. Other ways, in general...

     It’s nice.

     Voilà!
     Wishing you lots of good things,

For some, it hasn’t been this cold in 20 years; for others, since their childhood. Like everywhere in Europe, winters in the past were very different from now.

    I mean, I remember being stuck at home for two extra weeks of Christmas holidays because snow had blocked the roads! I remember playing sword fights with icicles in at least 40 cm of snow for a few weeks every year. But I guess that’s just a detail... Let’s say we’re having a proper, beautiful winter here.

    It’s full of cold (between -10°C and -16°C during the day), full of snow (every day—sometimes very thick, sometimes soft, with heavy snowflakes at times, but more often tiny ones). The snow is so powdery that I can’t even make snowballs! I press it between my hands, and when I release the pressure, it crumbles through my fingers like dry sand.

     And then there’s the wind (it feels really cold and turns those tiny snowflakes into little needles that sting any exposed skin). I guess you could call it a blizzard. I feel so lucky to walk and sleep in this beauty!

    Of course, I’m wearing seven layers of clothing! Of course, I gave up on my five-finger shoes—I still need my toes! So I bought proper shoes and socks. Naturally, most of my food is now frozen: frozen oranges, frozen cheese, rock-hard butter, frozen carrots, and radishes... everything just freezes! Even the wet wipes and my shoes freeze at night.

     So nice... sorry, I mean: so ice!

    But that’s not why I’m so slow in my progress. It just doesn’t help. No, what’s really keeping me here for weeks when it should be days is that the Polish people I meet are dangerously welcoming! I mean, you can’t really get away! It almost always starts with a simple “Come inside for a coffee,” but then it turns into “You’ll stay for lunch!” and somehow ends up as “It’s too late to leave now; you’ll sleep here tonight and go tomorrow!”

     And then it happens again the next day. Seriously! Even when we can’t communicate properly, as many people here speak fluent German or Russian but not much English, and rarely French...

     I’ve set a few rules for myself during this journey, and one of them is that I shouldn’t stay in the same place for more than three nights. It’s meant to keep me moving! But I find myself using this rule almost every three days. Do the math, and you’ll understand just how much I’m not walking! That should give you an idea of how welcoming people here are.

   I finally managed to arrive in a beautiful city, Wrocław (see the photos I’ve attached to this message). It’s stunning because of its architecture and history—now imagine it under 30 cm of snow! It’s been four days since I got here. I know you’re thinking, What about the three-night rule? But the rule has an exception for sickness. I’m not that rigid. Don’t worry, though—it’s nothing serious, just an ear infection. With the current weather conditions, I decided to accept the kind offer of Martin, a guy who invited me to stay in the room he rents, right next to a 93-year-old woman (an absolute master at checkers, by the way). She has no mercy for me—the crushed loser—and plays without a hint of compassion. A true checkers killer.

    I met Martin while he was putting salt on the icy floor of the church where he works. I had stopped to ask if the priests offered a pilgrim shelter, and when they didn’t, Martin invited me to his place. He called a friend, bought a bottle of spiced hot wine, and voilà! Three days later, here I am—lazy and feverish (just a mild one), my right ear ringing and hurting as if I’d been slapped repeatedly.

    It’s getting better now, and I’m already thinking about my departure. I’m also wondering how many days it will take me to escape the kindness and generosity here without offending anyone—except maybe the lady next door, whom I’d really like to beat at checkers before I leave!

    That’s all for now.

    I hope you’re well and happy.
    I wish you the best.
    I’ll keep in touch.

    Emmanuel.

First Week in Poland

28/1/2021

0 Comments

 
     Dear friends, hello.

     It’s been a while since I shared any news. Please note that this is probably the last update you’ll receive from me on WhatsApp. Following the latest (and, let’s face it, imposed) update to the app and my strong disagreement with its privacy policy concerning the use of personal data—especially since WhatsApp was acquired by Facebook—and knowing that there’s an equivalent to WhatsApp that seems more trustworthy (among other things, it’s non-profit), I’ve decided to migrate to Signal.
​
     I am therefore leaving WhatsApp and strongly encourage you to do the same. You can download Signal here: https://signal.org/install.

     So, that’s that.

     Now for the news.

     I’ve been in Poland for a week and I’m still moving forward, but perhaps even more slowly. The weather over the past 15 days has been a bit harsh, and even though I enjoy the cold, walking under snow and wind significantly slows my progress. Folding up a frozen tent in the morning takes much longer, too.

    I must admit that everything feels more tiring. I imagine I’m burning more calories due to the conditions, but I also think that having walked for nearly 4 months non-stop, apart from the occasional day off spent resting in the tent (about one day of rest per week or every 10 days), is finally taking its toll. I’m starting to feel the combined weight of walking and carrying the backpack. So, I’m not forcing myself—I’m taking it easy to ensure I can keep going further.

     Add to that my painful feet (I’m essentially walking barefoot in my five-finger shoes), cold and wet from slush (up to 25 cm deep), and it’s easy to see why I’m only managing about a dozen kilometers per day. I’ll definitely need a vacation when I arrive!

    My arrival in Poland was under good omens. Under normal circumstances, I wouldn’t have been able to cross the border due to movement restrictions from the virus. However, a phone call the day before from my German host to the police at the border in Görlitz reassured me. The officer on duty explained that crossing the border to buy cigarettes or alcohol (as prices differ significantly between Germany and Poland) was out of the question. But for a guy walking all the way from France on a pilgrimage to Częstochowa? That was a totally different story. Of course, I was allowed to cross the border, and so I did.

    My first interaction with the locals—well, “native indigenous people”, if you’ll pardon the redundancy—was equally reassuring. I stopped to ask for water to refill my bottles, and the man invited me into his home. He told me to put my bag down, take off a few layers, and have coffee. He refilled my bottles with hot tea, filled my thermos with hot coffee, gave me a little something to eat while chatting with his son on the phone, and even offered for me to stay the night since the snow had started falling even harder.

     It was very difficult to refuse such a kind offer, but I wanted to make a bit more progress that day. Still, I spent three hours with an incredibly nice couple—super welcoming, caring, and enthusiastic—even though they didn’t speak a word of English or French!

     This will be a challenge to manage in Poland so far: people here don’t seem to speak English at all—not even basic phrases like “thank you” or “bye-bye”. However, I am in the countryside, where English might be less prevalent and is perhaps only taught to the younger generation.

     So, I’m back to improvised sign language, mime, and Google Translate—when there’s network coverage! But goodwill makes everything surmountable.

     There’s also something very heartening: when I mention that I’m on a pilgrimage to Jasna Góra, people immediately know what I’m talking about. It resonates with them, and they understand. That feels good.

     Voilà.
     I’m attaching some photos in “vrak”!

     See you soon on Signal!
