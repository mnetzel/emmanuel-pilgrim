Meeting with a Bear

11/7/2023

0 Comments

 
      It’s been a long time again, and I feel and sense some impatience. It’s nice to know that my news messages are awaited. It’s somehow a form of support for my journey.

     I have now been in Romania for a few weeks, and I really appreciate what I am experiencing and seeing here.

   But since Slovakia, I’ve been dragging my feet. I feel tired, physically exhausted, without this being justified by walking. This is because my walk is slow and interrupted by many breaks to enjoy the landscape, an encounter, a stone, a stump, or a bench perfectly placed for me to rest on. I walk, contemplate, and meditate—sometimes all at the same time. It’s a pleasant exercise, during which the inner chatter eventually dies out, and I find myself alone, silent, and content.

   The breaks under the walnut tree, on the warm trunk of a tree in the local sun, stretch on without me worrying about it, without any movement occurring either inside or outside. A flat encephalogram (which some might say must be easy for me—see the end of the message...) certainly, but with vigilance, with purposeless concentration, if that makes sense to you. As a result, my average mileage drops quickly, but since the goal is not athletic, as you understood, I don’t care.

    However, for some time now, not only has my walking slowed down, but the breaks have grown longer and far more frequent. This made me consider the possibility of a health problem, which prompted me to change my route and pass through a big city to consult a doctor for a check-up. After all, it’s been 4 or 5 years since I’ve had any follow-up care, and almost 3 years that I’ve been walking in living conditions that some would call precarious.

    So, to reassure you right away: the examination and analyses revealed nothing abnormal. The doctor who examined me at the hospital simply told me that I needed to rest...

    So, that’s what I’m doing.

    Before continuing, I would like to point out that the Romanian health system is welcoming. Any individual is received and treated free of charge for 72 hours, regardless of their nationality. This will probably not impress the French, whose health system also has some good aspects, but when you experience this attention, it is touching. Really. The Hippocratic oath no longer feels hypocritical... and it feels good.

     So here it is: for a month, I have been walking for one day, then resting for two or three. And I feel like it’s paying off, as I now feel better rested and often eager to start again—something I had been missing lately.

    But I’m seeing the good side of things: I’m spending a lot of time in the countryside. When I stop, it’s often in the forest, which here finally covers the land that, to the east of where I came from, was sadly bare. I don’t know where you are, but here it’s hot or very hot, and as soon as it gets too much, we get a storm. A real summer storm with torrential downpours (think Indian or Nepalese monsoon), thunder that shakes the ground, and lightning that gets me out of my tent to check if the tree next to me was hit.

     After that, the temperature becomes much more bearable, but it’s incredibly heavy because of the humidity. It might seem unpleasant, but you have to look at the whole picture: when it’s hot and very humid, it feels heavy and uncomfortable, but—and I allow myself to use capital letters here—BUT, all the conditions are suddenly perfect for the appearance of our fungal friends.

    I picked summer ceps in mid-June. Yes! And they were a delight. At the end of June, I was invited to breakfast by a local mushroom enthusiast who prepared a fricassee of chanterelles. You can imagine that if I spend time in the woods, I eat mushrooms regularly. And to me, that’s divine.

   On the other hand, the local forests have one major drawback: bears. And I admit, there are many. Even in a small wood, like a one-hectare thicket, there is likely a bear in it. I didn’t really believe it at first—just like in Slovakia. But here, I see traces every day. Fresh traces, even very fresh ones. I’ve even had morning visits. In wolf and dog form. Their schedule seems to be around 3:30–4:00 a.m.

    I discovered that even when we sleep in the middle of the woods, a part of us continues to "listen." I was amazed to realize this, but I believe that our ears continue to hear, and our brain processes information intuitively. It’s strange to notice, like finally becoming aware of your unconscious.

    The two times a bear approached my tent while I was sleeping, I woke up immediately, as if shaken by my subconscious warning me of danger. There was no gentle awakening phase where you take your time, where reality is still blurred by the paradoxical sleep phase, free cinema... No, I opened my eyes instantly, no transition—I was alert, attentive, concentrated, and immediately ready to act.

    I already knew there was something on the other side of the thin fabric of my tent. My first thought, clear and certain, was: a bear. About ten meters from the tent, I then heard heavy but hesitant footsteps. Without hesitation, without even thinking, I began to sing—softly but loudly—while at the same time lighting up the tent with the headlamp given to me by a firefighter from Târgu Mureș. I knew that my tent would then become a softly glowing lamp, about 3 meters long and 1.2 meters high. A beautiful sight for the bear, I imagined, lying there, motionless.

    Singing right away when you wake up because a bear is outside might seem like a crazy idea. But let me explain:

    The only bear attacks in Europe are related to surprise—when a bear is startled by a human who was too discreet (the bear neither hears nor senses them), or to fear—when an aggressive human mistakenly thinks that making a lot of noise or acting threateningly will scare away a 350 kg beast. The bear, feeling threatened, might attack instead of fleeing. Another situation is when an inattentive human finds themselves between a bear and her cubs, triggering a protective maternal instinct—with claws and teeth.

    Other attacks are linked to predatory behavior, triggered when a human runs (or cycles or skis) in front of a bear. This activates the bear’s predatory or attack reflex, much like what happens with an excited dog that is likely to bite if you run from it.

     So, I tell myself that if the bear has approached me despite the unmistakable olfactory signals for its sense of smell—signals I deliberately place around and on my tent (a cloth soaked in diesel between the roof and the double roof of the tent, and my urine poured on the trunks of trees around my campsite)—it’s because the bear is curious (like many animals I’ve noticed) and opportunistic (hoping the human is gone and has left something to eat).

    Of course, the bear probably doesn’t have a clear concept of UFOs, but the tent must seem like a very strange object to it—especially when it lights up.

    When I wake up, I sing so the bear knows I’m there, in the tent. Singing, unlike shouting or howling, is far less aggressive. It also benefits me: singing has a soothing effect, both on the one who hears the song and on the one who sings it. You can’t sing while being completely paralyzed with fear. Singing keeps fear at bay.

     In short, my theory is that, like many animals I’ve known since childhood, bears are not so much receptive to what I say but rather to the tone in which I say it. They are not as affected by my movements as they are by my attitude—my mental state, in a way.

    I believe that singing is a way to show my presence while maintaining a relaxed and peaceful attitude. It reassures the animal while informing it of my presence and my nature.

     Both times this happened, the bear ran away immediately. The second time, it was really close to me and let out a kind of nasal breath, an impressive Mmufffff. Either I wasn’t mistaken about its presence, or I sing really badly!

    Also impressive is the sound of cattle running through the undergrowth. You might not know this, but when we sleep in the woods, sounds are amplified—not only by the acoustics of the place but also by the unconscious analysis our brain performs. It’s probably a remnant from the time when we lived outdoors and were a tasty treat for many predators.

    At night, a simple beetle can seem like a huge rat snooping around. A toad becomes je ne sais quoi weighing at least 20 kilograms... A bird landing on the ground and scratching with its claw for food becomes a wild boar. I assure you, it’s destabilizing to realize how much our brain exaggerates these sounds.

    Over time, with practice, the brain recalibrates, and you can relax completely despite the many noises of the forest. I now sleep in the woods as comfortably as I would in a bed in my room—even though, in reality, I haven’t had a room or a bed for more than 28 years.

    So, I believe bears aren’t really the danger. They represent a risk, because, in the end, nothing prevents encountering a bad-tempered one—there are difficult characters in all mammals; it’s not exclusive to bipeds in an upright position. But I think that with certain precautions and behaviors, the risk is ultimately very small.

     I accept this risk because, ultimately, I have no choice if I want to continue my journey.

    When I spend several days in the same place, choosing the right location is essential. A sheltered spot—from the sun, so it’s possible to stay under the tent, and from view, to avoid worrying or being disturbed. Some places lend themselves perfectly: a small clearing of a few dozen square meters of flat and sparsely sown ground, where dense vegetation around and above filters the direct light... A small stream of clear water to cool off during the heat of the afternoon, and to wash, too.

     This is where I was yesterday and the day before. Superb... Magnificent... Ideal.

     Until the storm.

    The rain began to fall in large drops, accompanied by a deafening clap of thunder that tore through the silence and my senses. It became so intense that I went out to check the stream, only a few meters away. The rain was so heavy that, already soaked, I decided to take out the soap and wash myself in the deluge.

     When I returned to the tent, I knew there was a risk the water would rise and the stream might overflow. But there was space...

     So I lay down and thought about something else. The rain stopped, and after looking outside, I assessed the risk of flooding as acceptable. The groundsheet of my new tent is waterproof and can withstand being in a layer of water up to 5 cm without compromising the interior.
And inside, I have a small self-inflating mattress a few centimeters thick.

     Even if water were to penetrate a little, most of my belongings would still be sheltered.

     The happy optimist who sees the glass half full... I fell asleep like a child.

     As I write this, I am washing all of my belongings and gear. I was awakened by the tent shaking and pushing me aside. Water was penetrating inside, and by the time I began organizing my things and planning my escape, I was in more than eight inches of water.

     Of course, night was falling, and I had to move all my stuff to a place that wasn’t flooded, about 50 meters away. By the time I finished, the torrent covering the whole area was 40 cm deep. Suffice to say, the stream below was no longer visible at all.

     The tent was almost lying in the water, and dismantling it became a major emergency before it broke. The battery in the headlamp chose this moment to signal that it needed energy. I realized that if I handled things poorly, I would lose a lot—some of my equipment had already been carried away. I removed all the pegs and fought to get the tent out of the water without it being swept away by the current.

     I managed it, securing the tent in a safe place and getting back to it later. I spent the next day rinsing my belongings of the fine, sticky mud and the whole afternoon looking for what had been carried away: water bottles, clothes, bags.

     I found almost everything, which feels almost miraculous.

     But I confess, I’m not proud of myself. There were signs I ignored because I thought everything would be fine. I listened to optimism until it turned into foolishness!

     Fortunately, in the village where I arrived, there’s a small guesthouse. I’m staying there to wash my clothes, clean the mud off absolutely everything, and dry everything in the sun.

     Finally, everything is fine.

   Tomorrow, I’m back on my way—a day of walking to a suitable place, then two days of rest. With a little luck, in a few weeks, I’ll be able to resume a normal rhythm.

     In the meantime, I wish you all the best.

     See you soon.

Romania

6/5/2023

0 Comments

 
​     Romania.

     I entered Romania today.
    And just where I was supposed to pass by, I got stopped by the police and stayed a little longer than expected. Just after crossing the border, when I had planned to put my tent on it (the border! Because I still enjoy doing that)—you know, in the middle of the line in a wild area with grass and a few trees—I told myself that I was going to walk a few more kilometers instead.

     A village was right there, and barely halfway through it, a car stopped at my level. A gentleman in stained overalls showed me a border police badge. I admit that at the time, I wondered if it was a hoax, or even a scam, because the guy looked like anything but a policeman on duty... But he called his colleagues, who came to pick me up and took me to the station a few kilometers away.

     So yes, Romania is in Europe, but it’s not in the Schengen area. So, while you can cross the border whenever you want with a French passport and stay as long as you want, the crossing must be done at a border customs post—not in the wild like I did...

     I have to confess that I hadn’t thought of that. This morning, I had the choice between a large, long, very busy road with fast vehicles, or a small country road ending in a dirt path, deserted and much shorter, but crossing grass fields and small woods.

     Guess which choice I made?

     I spent my afternoon waiting for my case to be processed. Lots of paperwork, with many copies of each. And after placing maybe twenty signatures, I was taken back to the Hungarian border, where I had to pay a fine.

    It’s intriguing to me because it wasn’t Romania that punished me for illegally entering their territory, but Hungary for leaving theirs...

     Anyway, I paid my dues. The customs officers were very nice. They did their job. I would have liked the law and its application to remain at their discretion because, yes, I made a mistake, but it was an honest mistake. I really didn’t know and wouldn’t have done it if I had known.

     After a few photos together and an exchange of telephone numbers, the customs officer who had called me in the morning was kind enough to accompany me back to the place where he had caught me so that I could resume my journey while continuing to be able to say, without lying, that I really did my entire journey on foot. Very nice of him.

     Why am I telling you this?

    Well, now I feel ashamed in addition to having been negligent. Because I think of all those people who often give me a coin or more to support my journey, many of whom are financially modest people, even very modest. People to whom I cannot refuse the money they give me—I assure you that I really try because it is very hard for me to receive or accept money. But in the face of their insistence, to avoid disappointing them, or just because sometimes I feel their genuine motivation to participate, I agree to receive the donation.

     But today, those donations went to pay a fine.

    It’s silly, maybe... Probably. But I feel ashamed that these pennies were spent this way. I feel as though I’ve betrayed the gift of these people, and this impression stays with me until the evening in my tent, where I am deeply moved.

     It’s 80 euros that I wasted...

     That’s how it is. But I think back to the 10-year-old boy who caught up with me on his bike as I was leaving his village after he heard in the grocery store who I was and what I was doing. Once he caught up with me, he got off his bike and asked if it was really true that I had walked all this way. While I was confirming it to him, he put his bike on the ground, took out the only coin his wallet contained, and very seriously—almost solemnly—offered it to me.

     It was important to him, and I feel ashamed to think that his gift foolishly went toward a fine...

    As I write these words, the Romanian officer (Marian), who now has my phone number, calls me to find out if I was finally able to cross the border. He tells me I can sleep at his place if I want.

     But as I’m already in my tent, he invites me to have a coffee with him in the morning near the post where I spent my afternoon.

     He also invites me to spend the Easter weekend at his house since it’s on my way, and we seem to have a lot to discuss.

     The next day, I resume my journey, which should bring me to the town where I’ll be hosted by Marian. Upon arriving in town, a car stops beside me, the driver gets out and approaches me—I recognize another customs officer. Shaking my hand, he tells me he’s happy to see me. He asks if I need anything, if he can help me, and even if I’d like to sleep at his place. I tell him I’ve already been invited by one of his colleagues, but we exchange phone numbers just in case, and he resumes his journey as I head to a restaurant for a late lunch.

     During my meal, while I’ve saved Mugurel’s number in my contacts to exchange messages via WhatsApp or Signal, he texts me to ask if I’d like to wash my clothes at his place. Washing my clothes is certainly a good idea—I’ll see how to organize myself for that.

     In the end, I will spend three nights at Marian’s, and during these three days, we will spend two at Mugurel’s house with his family, enjoying a good meal, engaging in nice discussions, and savoring a very pleasant little red wine.

     Well, that’s my arrival in Romania. And even though I felt a little bad (and very stupid) for crossing the border without checking out beforehand, I tell myself that I probably wouldn’t have met these people otherwise. So, it was a blessing in disguise, as we say...

     On my way back, I plan to stop at a small chapel on the outskirts of town. To get there, after leaving the road, you have to cross a garden/meadow adjoining a small house with a few chicken coops and sheepfold buildings nearby.

     A senior man with a wheelbarrow is busy there. We greet each other. He no longer has many teeth but keeps a beautiful smile. He leaves his wheelbarrow to accompany me to see the chapel, tells me where to put my bag, and then leaves me there. I stay for a few long moments, eyes closed in front of this small reconstructed cave, and then I climb the small hill, at the top of which stands the little chapel.

     When I come down the opposite side, I stop to greet a horse grazing there. And when I finally get back to my bag, the gentleman and his granddaughter are bringing me something to eat and drink: eggs tinted with red onion, as is the tradition for Easter here, cake, and fruit juice. He shows me around his house, his two lambs, his two goats, their kids, his beautiful hens, and his three piglets. I also greet his dogs, though I can’t get close to his two cats.

    He and his wife are interested in my little pilgrimage story—impressed, maybe—but really happy to welcome me.

     He cried twice as we spoke. The first time was when I told him how much the people along my path have welcomed me, listening with emotion to the kindness, attentions, and care that I described having been offered to me. His hand rested on his chest as I spoke, tapping it softly. His eyes grew wet, and a tear rolled down.

     When I got ready to leave, we shook hands for a long time, and I thanked him for his kindness. He had offered me food, a drink, the chance to charge my phone, and welcomed me with such warmth. He replied, full of emotion to the point of tears, the monotonous voice of the translator relaying his words to me: "This is the God I serve."

     And as I was about to let go of his hand to leave, he added: "Will you come back to see me? I'll welcome you again. You'll come back, huh?"

     This is how I entered Romania.

     Now that I’ve been walking here for a few weeks, I can say I am welcomed just as warmly as anywhere else.

     Today, as I was describing this hospitality on the phone to a friend in France—telling him how, the day before, I had been offered a phone battery recharge and a beer while waiting by the village bartender in a village I was crossing, and how, while I was sipping a local lager, a gentleman seated at the next table went out for a moment and returned with a full bag that he offered me: fresh grapes (about 1.5 kg), a large brioche bread (an Easter tradition for the Orthodox Church), and a good kilo of sausages, which I unfortunately could not accept because they were full of meat... So, then he offered me another beer.

     And then, when it was time to leave, as I shook hands to say goodbye to everyone, I felt a folded note slipped into my palm. When I refused to accept it, the bartender made big gestures to ensure I wouldn’t refuse it...

     There you go.

     I was telling my French friend all of this to describe the welcome I always receive. I was on the phone while walking when I arrived in a small village. I decided to stop, put my bag down, and sit under the porch of the church to finish chatting comfortably in the shade, on a bench. In the middle of my conversation with France, a lady entered the space, which sheltered some tombs around the church. After pulling out a few weeds here and there, she came toward me and, as though I spoke the language, said what I presume to be the following:

     "What are you doing here? Where do you come from?"
     Heu Francia, pilgrim, I said, pointing to my chest...

   She spoke kindly, and I didn’t understand much, but the principal message was full of benevolence. She ended up taking me by the arm, as ladies sometimes do with a man, and led me toward the low wall by the side of the road to point out a house. She gestured to me that I had to go there to eat. I understood the invitation. My friend was still on the phone and quietly shared in the spontaneous local kindness, listening to all this discreetly.

     I’ll skip the details because otherwise, it would take pages and pages, but that’s how I was offered a magnificent breakfast/lunch: fried eggs from the farm, bread with plum marmalade, and fresh morning milk (which, for those who don’t know, is exactly like the milk bought in cartons, but with a smell and taste... which is really good). All homemade.

     The nephew, who lived across the road, came to translate since he spoke English (which helped a lot, especially as I had no network for phone translation). All this very nice food had to be washed down with a superb little homemade plum spirit, double distilled to reach 53 degrees—just to help bring it all down. I left with a bottle of fresh milk, shortbread cake with chocolate between the layers, a jar of plum marmalade, and two small bottles of plum spirit and blackberry cream.

     There are a few things I’d like to point out here that I think are important. This lady came to see me and spontaneously invited me to her place to eat and drink. She took me by the arm to show me her house, and we went there together. She had never met me before and had only seen me briefly before approaching me.

     This, for me, tends to demonstrate that her act was dictated by her own qualities and not by anything about me. In other words, she was good and kind to me because she was good and kind, not because it was me. I was a total stranger to her (she had no way of knowing how wonderful I am...).

    I find that reassuring. There is something truly savory here, and I have the privilege of witnessing this expression of kindness. It’s precious, isn’t it?

     This lady was moved when I left, and so I was touched...
    I resumed my journey very briefly, as I was invited to the house opposite (the nephew’s, you know) for a little coffee, a little plum spirit that I refused, a galette of sheep’s cheese, and an Annette (a pastry folded like an envelope around the filling and pan-fried on both sides). It was really delicious, even when you’re completely full as I was. I’m attaching a photo so you can get an idea of the taste.

      All this to say that, even if it’s a shame you can’t experience these kindnesses live, I do my best to share them with you as authentically as possible...

    Ah yes, I forgot: in the next village, a gentleman in his yard saw me pass by, greeted me, and waved. I stopped because he offered me water, and since I was about to start looking for a bivouac spot, filling my bottles seemed like a good idea. I refused the little plum spirit... but I gladly accepted the homemade sheep’s cheese. A true delight, which I am savoring as I write these words.

     I’m under a beautiful tree that will give me shade tomorrow morning, so that waking up and packing my bag and tent won’t leave me sweating. I’m just a few meters from a small river where I bathed and rinsed my clothes. The lawn is a soft, vibrant green, dotted with flowers sprouting everywhere.

     This place was shown to me by two children who followed me for more than a kilometer and a half. Even though I would have passed by it on my own, they wanted to walk with me.

     That’s it, that’s all for this time. I’ll stop here. I hope that I’ve been able to convey, if not the flavors or the smells, then at least and above all, this wonderful feeling of being welcomed with kindness and generosity by strangers full of humanity...

     Voilà.
     Au revoir.

     And water buffalo too, but they were too far away for me to take a photo...

Happiness

30/3/2023

0 Comments

 
     ​I thought I was going to send some solid news today, and that passing the 7000 km mark would be my pretext. But I have to be honest—this is fake news. I don’t really know where I am in terms of kilometers. I have this estimation due to my intimate relationship with topographic applications that I use every day to navigate, avoid busy roads, impassable rivers, and just not lose my way. Let’s say I suspect the distance, but I haven’t calculated it. It must be around 7000 km, give or take 10%. Although, in general, it’s probably less than more, because I believe that in my situation, we tend to evaluate rather higher than lower...

     But hey... Let’s leave the kilometers aside.

   I travel places, I meet people. And since my departure, everyone has been magnificent—welcoming, generous. This makes my path magnificent as well, and I walk it serenely, without its vagaries being a burden in any way.

    For some time now, I’ve been crossing villages that consist of two parts: one “normal” village, and then further out, on the outskirts or in a small remote corner, a second part that’s much more dilapidated—almost like slums. It’s very visible, and you can recognize a population of “Roms,” as they are called. The former Gypsies, sedentarized by the Soviet governments, living in difficult conditions without really being able to escape them, even though it seems local and governmental aid is mobilized to help.

     I come across these places. Poverty. Promiscuity, with everything that always accompanies it... precariousness.

    And yet, I must say that I don’t think I’ve come across one of these places without someone inviting me at least once to eat, drink coffee, or sleep.

     It’s a lesson in humility for me every time. I am forced to appreciate the welcome and the gift, contrasted against their living conditions. How touching.

     Ah yes, speaking of touching...

   For some time now, I’ve been scratching a lot. It’s not touching—it’s irritating, but let me explain. I was scratching a lot (like, really a lot and uncontrollably), and it didn’t take me long to realize I had scabies. Local generosity? A dirty towel in a restaurant bathroom? A questionable bed in a questionable motel? It’s hard to know how or by whom. Especially since it’s the first time in my life that I’ve caught it (I’ve never had lice or crabs either...). But that’s the thing—when it’s the first time, and the body doesn’t know the problem, in this case, Mr. Sarcoptes scabiei from the mite family, it can take time for symptoms to appear. Sometimes 15 days or more before the itching starts. So, it becomes difficult to determine the origin of the contamination.

     But here, I tried to contact all the people with whom I spent time to warn them about the possibility of contagiousness. It’s really contagious, those small beasts...
      But no panic, I often wash my hands with soap or hydroalcoholic gel, so a handshake doesn’t involve much risk. No, the risk was when I got invited to sleep inside.

      The problem is that with the practice of pilgrimage as I do, it’s difficult to get rid of the beast without a very effective treatment: ivermectin. And this product is prohibited in Hungary, where I am, and that’s problematic because the remaining solutions are really cumbersome. While the ivermectin treatment consists of taking pills with a large glass of water twice at eight-day intervals, the alternative treatments require brushing your entire body with a lotion, letting it stand for 8 or 12 hours depending on the mixture, applying a second layer, waiting again, and then washing with hot water and soap. At the same time, washing clothes—all clothes—at 60 degrees. And the groundsheet. And the sleeping bag. A lot of work...

     You can see how it becomes really complicated for the camper that I am.

    While I was explaining this on the phone to the people who had invited me to spend three nights with them some time after my contamination a few weeks ago—they live in the last big city in Slovakia before the border with Hungary—Michaela and her neighbor Bruno (Branislav) decided, without me being able to dissuade them, not only to find ivermectin on the black market (it’s also forbidden in Slovakia, as a result of European policies concerning coronavirus treatments, if I understood correctly—some drugs have been blacklisted...), but also to drive four hours to bring it to me in the neighboring country. And with homemade cake—two, in fact, because both of them had made one for me.

     You see...

     It leaves me speechless. Silent. Calm, in every way.

     When I resumed my journey, after a coffee offered for the three of us by the pilgrims' inn where I was staying, I was immersed in a deep silence. Deep and full. Not empty. I was calm, serene, grateful, confident. I walked calmly. I sat down whenever I felt the need, wherever the place was suitable, and I took time to contemplate this silence and sink into it.

     There is nothing to say... nothing to be said.

    Of course, there are times when loud shouts try to reach my ears or my eyes. This is always the case. Whether it’s the din of war that I circumvent geographically, or those more distant, like the rumblings from French streets at the moment, or more simply, a bistro conversation I come across while having a coffee (or a slivovica!)...

    But once I’ve finished paying attention to it, the trace of the writing, the sight, or the sound fades almost instantly. There are no leftovers, no residual clutter. It becomes like distant knowledge that doesn’t truly reach me—at least not until I decide, with conscious effort, to pay attention to it again.

     This distancing is pleasant. The noise of the world remains distant, even as I cross it. When it is present, this distance, born of healthy silence, feels like crossing or visiting myself with each step. I say "healthy silence" because it comes from the calm of the one experiencing it, not from the absence of noise around them.

    So, I regularly spend weeks alone in the woods, then pass through towns or villages, meeting people and forming connections. When I leave again, there’s this same distancing—I rediscover the silence where I left it and sink into it, burying myself pleasantly in its embrace. I am privileged; I am aware of it.

    The practice of pilgrimage, or perhaps simply long-term walking, gently forces me into this abandonment. It constrains with a certain tenderness. The need to adapt to the path, in order to walk it for a long time, is both mandatory and essential. To achieve this, it’s necessary to move toward what is pleasant, nourishing, and reassuring, while abandoning what is not.

     I had to leave behind objects too heavy to carry in my bag to avoid the pain of carrying them. Finally, it’s clear: to feel good, you must stop hurting yourself. It seems obvious, and yet I notice so many counterexamples.

     This principle has slowly extended from the backpack on my back to the inner one, in my head and chest.

     It’s no longer the non-essential objects I leave behind now, but the petty fears and anxieties of everyday life.
     Where am I going?
     Will I have enough to eat?
     To drink?
     To sleep?
     What if this path leads nowhere?

     And then, the worries about the future—because I will have to work, earn money, and return someday...

     We can’t go long without leaving these false hassles aside. This is the moment when we begin to learn freedom and happiness. And the more one realizes this, the more one likes it, the less difficult it is to realize it and participate in it. In fact, the more you walk on its path, the easier it becomes to walk there...

    I learn freedom, I learn happiness. I’ve discovered that you can’t find them, you can’t receive them, or give them—the only way to live them is to learn to be. And it doesn’t need much; in a way, it just “stops preventing them” from being, naturally...

     The most frequent question I receive concerns the number of kilometers I do per day. But this is a question that no longer makes sense to me. The preoccupation with the number of kilometers traveled no longer exists at all. You now understand what I wrote earlier concerning the 7000 km fake news. A glance at the map tells me that I’m almost there, but in the end, I don’t know. I haven’t made the effort to calculate, despite being asked very often.

     I measure my days, if I must, by the quality of the time that has passed. I walk when I want or need to. I stop as soon as it’s pretty enough to look at, and the notion of average mileage is completely absent. I don’t feel bad or guilty for having walked a little—that’s something that took me a long time to give up: feeling bad for not having walked a lot today. The concept of obligation, if it still exists, has been transferred to the pleasure of being.

     And in my long walk experience, finding a pretty spot is extremely pleasant. Finding a suitable place to pitch the tent is pleasant. A source of fresh water to fill my water bottles and refresh myself. A lake or river where I can bathe, wash myself... A hot shower. A coffee. A glass of slivovica (a little plum!). A smile.

     I take pleasure in such trivial details, and I’m amazed at the power they possess, the strength they wield. To say that finding mushrooms on my way, lighting a fire to cook them, and eating while sitting on the ground in front of my tent nourished me is an understatement. It certainly nourished my body, but also a whole part of me which, until I let it enjoy and live, was curled up in a withering and sterile non-existence.

     The silence I listen to is a fullness, not an emptiness...

   Then the inner discourse slowly fades away... The incessant brouhaha, which we no longer realize deafens our inner ears to the point of not only deafening ourselves but others too—not only inside but also outside—eventually dries up and goes silent. There is then nothing to hinder the full enjoyment of the contentment of a deep breath of fresh air, the freshness of a sip of spring water, the sight of a wild animal, the song of a bird, the scent of a fir tree, the delicacy of a flower petal, or the taste of a hazelnut...

     And here I am, with an almost ecstatic feeling, even though no event has occurred to provoke it. I find myself laughing without having thought of anything funny. A huge smile splits my face as I stand totally alone in the woods. And yet, I can’t stop this smile that I don’t offer to anyone.

     I am well. I am here. And it’s good...

     I wish you all the best. Be well and happy.

Meetings - Poland and Czech Republic

​     Now we are sure—winter is definitely here. No need to comment on that, there are far more cold days than fresh ones. That’s how I recognize it!

     I would like to describe my journey day by day, you know, as a journal for you to see and realize how crazy nice it is. Because it is—it’s just crazy nice. Every day. From the beginning until now. I mean, come on, 15 months! Non-stop!!

    So I’m going to write, or at least try to write, a little summary of my day every day, to give you an idea of how things are going for the pilgrim, day by day.

   Last night was nice. I slept in my tent in the forest. Usual and nice. My way is really easy nowadays, as I follow a very well-indicated path of Jacob (Compostela way/Santiago or St. James way). Most of it is on small woodland or rural paths. Only occasionally do I walk on roads, and through towns only when there’s something worth seeing. The route has been designed to showcase beautiful natural places and cross important historical, archaeological, and cultural landmarks. So it’s nice.

    I don’t have to use my phone map much—the yellow symbol of a St. Jacques shell on a blue square is at every ambiguous crossing, waiting to guide me.

     After packing my tent and backpack, I walked, rested, and walked some more. Then, suddenly, I realized I wasn’t wearing my hat anymore. First time this has happened! Strange, because normally I can feel it. But I understood why: at my last stop, I had been a little sweaty, so I sat on a bus stop bench to cool down and took off my hat. Somehow, I went on without it because I was still warm. Really unusual. But, let’s say it happens.

     I realized this at the entrance of a village and felt like I had wasted my morning because I needed to go back to fetch my hat and then walk the same path a third time. I did, because even though this hat is a real disappointment (it was supposed to be high quality, but no—it’s really not. Yet it was expensive! And the slogan of the brand is “Be protected by a legend”—seriously, you should see this “legend”!), I still use it and need it. A hat is an essential part of a pilgrim’s outfit, after all.

    So, I went back about 1.5 km, found it on the bench where I suspected I’d left it, and then returned to the village entrance. There, it seemed I had two options to get to the church whose steeple I could see. I chose the one on the right. It was a dead end—I walked half a kilometer for nothing!

    I tried to find an alternative way that wouldn’t make me retrace my steps, maybe by crossing a field, you know... but after a while, I realized that wasn’t possible either because fences were all around. I even spent more time looking for an Indiana Jones-style solution than I would have by simply going back. Finally, I had to walk all the way back to the place where I had first realized I was missing my hat and take the straightforward path.

    At that point, I really thought my morning hadn’t been productive at all! It felt like, somehow, I had done my best to waste time on my walk. Without intending to, I had worked hard not to make much progress this morning. It was as if I were delayed, as if I had to wait a little before going further.

    I was probably too early! For what? No idea. But I was confident it was for something, and that I was there for that thing. It might sound a little crazy to you, but after walking for more than 400 days, I no longer follow my plans. In fact, I stopped making plans as much as possible very early on. “As much as possible,” because humans love to make plans—for everything, always! Try not to plan. If you really look and reflect honestly, you’ll see how hard it is not to.

    But as a pilgrim—someone walking in the unknown but with a goal—planning my way, whether in time or space, becomes completely irrelevant, to the point of being a total waste of time and even counterproductive. Instead, to put it poetically or mystically, I let my way show me my way. And sometimes, it takes strange coincidences, meetings, or events (like this morning) to make me aware of what’s supposed to come next—if something is supposed to come next. This might sound strange to you, and I hope I’m not too confusing in my attempt to explain it.

    Let’s just say something was unusual about my morning walk, and all of it seemed to result in nothing but a nice waste of time. My experience over the past 15 months, however, tells me that instead of focusing on the wasted time, I should stay aware of what’s coming next. That’s a relevant attitude to adopt even outside of pilgrimage, I’d say, but it becomes almost undeniably clear when you’re on one. There are no wrong ways. This concept and feeling of “wrongness” are, ultimately, very relative. One should strive to be more flexible. (The way trains you in that!) Finally, when you arrive, if you look back, everything that brought you to where you are is simply the way you used—your way. You might have used a lot of time, but you are here, aren’t you?

    So I finally arrived at the church I wanted to stop by, and after taking off my backpack, plugging in my phone and power bank, I started to meditate a little, as I do anytime I can get inside one. Not for long, though, because not even five minutes had passed when a lady and her husband arrived. When they saw me, they came over very enthusiastically. And that’s how I met this very nice couple that I would definitely have missed if everything had "gone well." So, in the end, I was glad for my "wasted morning time."

     Very soon, we were joined by the parish priest. After the mass, I was invited to have lunch at the parish house next door. It was a good opportunity to answer the many questions I was asked. I’m sure I’ll be in the next local newspaper. As we talked after lunch, night came fast—15:45! And so, I got to stay the night in the same place. After a kind farewell, I set up my sleeping mat on the floor next to an electric plug and started writing the summary of the day. Then the priest came in. He insisted that I feel free to use the kitchen, dig into the fridge, and cook myself dinner. And again, we talked—for at least two hours.

     In the morning, I woke up early. The priest had to get ready for the mass, and I needed to pack all my stuff before he left. After the mass, I bid him farewell. To my surprise, a few people were waiting for me with gifts—chocolates, biscuits... With what the priest gave me, I was settled for the next few days!

     Of course, for all of them, I collected prayer intentions for my next destination. Pilgrim duty, which I take very seriously. And then I went on my way.

     Today, I passed through a town. Nothing particularly special, except that I crossed paths with a lady on the street at the entrance to the town. She saw the St. Jacques shell on my backpack and stopped to chat. It turned out she was one of the two organizers of the Jacob’s Way in this area. And guess who the second organizer was? The lady I had met the previous day, who invited me for lunch with her husband. Small world, isn’t it? The coincidence was nice.

    My backpack is really heavy because I carry as much food as I can and all my water bottles are full. Just for those two needs, I carry around 10–12 kg! Now you understand why my backpack’s name is "Expiation." But that also means I can stop in a nice place and rest for a night—or even two if I want. I have enough supplies for that...

     Today, I slept very well again. I was in my tent in the forest for the second night in the same spot, as it was a very nice place and I needed a day of rest. So, I just stayed for another night. This morning, I felt really good. Fully rested, and as you feel after a very good sleep. I woke up early and was ready to go after about an hour of packing up all my stuff and tent.

    Beautiful forest. It seems this part of Poland is really nice for that—big, beautiful forests everywhere. Luckily, the Jacob’s Way goes through them. It’s so nice to walk there.

     The only downside this morning was that I ran out of water. Water is heavy, and I carry only five liters with me. Since I stayed an extra day in the wild, I went through my reserve and ran dry by the morning. My map showed me a tiny village a few kilometers ahead, so I wasn’t desperate—just thirsty.

     When I arrived at the first house, you know, the ones on the periphery of the village but still a good distance from its center, I stopped to rest on a big rock next to a canal, facing two houses and a superb bridge. A very nice place to stop and enjoy the water passing by—and I did. But thirst shortened my contemplation, and I went on my way. I crossed the bridge and stopped at a Y-bifurcation of the road, just to be sure of my direction and decide which of the two houses I’d ask for water. Exactly at that moment, someone called out to me from the other side of his garden fence, asking if I wanted a drink. The timing was so perfect. I did want one, and, even more, it was precisely what I was concerned about at that exact moment.

     It turned out I was invited by a really cool guy—for coffee first, then lunch, and eventually to stay the night. Super cool for his warm welcome, but not only that. The guy is a passionate enthusiast of the Middle Ages and knights. To the point of belonging to a brotherhood whose members dedicate themselves to preserving medieval culture and organizing jousting games and duels as they were done back then. That means sword fighting, with real equipment and everything! Fully functional armors and weapons, made authentically like in those times. And yes, the swords are real (though not sharpened, to avoid cutting too heavily into either the brotherhood’s numbers or their limbs). It’s not just for show. How cool is that?

     In the afternoon, we went on a quad bike ride through the forest because he wanted to show me a few things about the area. First, the canal—a massive and famous feat of hydraulic engineering designed to transport special pine wood from this region (and only here in the world!) that was perfect for creating aeronautical masts back when wood was still in use. Then, a grim place tied to the war, the post-war period, and Poland’s tortured and complex history—especially in this region.

     It was the site of the totally senseless deaths of former deportees used for forced labor, French prisoners of war executed by communist forces, likely out of doubt and miscommunication, as none of them spoke the right language to explain their presence in the area.

     I really enjoyed his kindness and the time he generously spent showing me around. Also, his wife was a great cook and had prepared homemade pizza with yeast dough she made herself for dinner...

     I slept in his wooden bungalow. The next morning, after coffee, he walked with me for a good kilometer—just like that—until the entrance of the village. We parted there. Nice, no? My day was nice. Lots of forest. Small villages. Still plenty of food, a bit frozen, but it’s okay. All is well.

     I found a beautiful spot between fir trees, with a soft carpet of moss. A perfect place to sleep—protected from the wind, with almost no risk of a tree falling if the weather decided to get a little wild during the night. It’s winter, and there’s a blizzard right now. Fir trees provide good protection against both wind and snow coming from the side. Looks like a good night in perspective...

     In the morning, after the usual ritual of packing the backpack and folding the tent, I resumed my journey through these magnificent woods.

     For the second time, I saw and observed a moose—another female. A superb moment when time seemed to stop, and everything was in suspension, alert.

     In India, we use the word Darshan for such moments, often reserved for mystical encounters. But the concept can also extend to events like this—an encounter with the animal world. Because, obviously, there are very, very few moose in India, that’s for sure...

     In the middle of the morning, I left the forest and arrived in a small village. I stopped at the first house that seemed occupied to ask for water. The lady who answered didn’t speak a word of English, and even less of French. Thanks to my few words of Polish vocabulary and a lot of gestures, I explained the basics: I am a pilgrim, I am French, I have walked a lot to get here. Could I have water to fill my bottles, please? As soon as she understood, she said many things that still elude me, but the essential and general part was clear: she had understood me well, said she regularly saw pilgrims but definitely not in this season, and was impressed that I had come from France on foot. She told me I must be totally crazy. She insisted I shouldn’t take tap water but bottled mineral water, which she said was much better. After that, we said goodbye.

     Two hundred meters further, I came across a wayside crucifix with a bench for onlookers, which was perfect for me. Places to sit are scarce and highly sought after on the way. A stone, a stump, a fallen tree... The bench is technically the best.

     In Poland, benches are often found at the foot of crucifixes and calvaries. They are practical and comfortable but usually located in villages or near roads with cars and noise. In Germany or Luxembourg, public benches are everywhere—even on remote forest paths. That was very surprising for me. But I have to admit, it’s nice to know you can sit comfortably without getting dirty or wet every 500 meters.

     I was enjoying a well-earned sitting time when the neighbor from the house nearby went out for a firewood expedition. With a wheelbarrow, a saw, and a hatchet under his arm, he came over to talk when he saw me. He had much better rudiments of English than I have in Polish. He offered me hot tea, and as we chatted outside—me still sitting on the bench, sipping the hot liquid—the lady who had given me water earlier joined us. She was practically running, in her dressing gown and slippers, through the morning snow, holding a plate in her hand. She must have been preparing food when I came to her door, and when she saw me sitting on the bench, she brought me four pancakes with jam that she had just made. A few words were exchanged between her and the gentleman, and then she trotted off again. It must have been minus five degrees this morning, which cuts roadside conversations in dressing gowns short—that’s proven!

      In any case, the gesture was touching. And the pancakes were super good. Pancakes in these conditions are always super good. But you see, it’s like this every day. It makes me cry sometimes.

      I got back into the forest, found a nice place, and voilà...

     Today I walked a good 15 km before meeting a couple of dynamic retirees who were on their weekly walk for him, daily for her.

     I say walk, not promenade, because their pace was brisk. We crossed paths, and when we were level, stopped to chat. Then they invited me to their place—a newly built house in the next village, 6 km back from where we were. That was 6 km I had just walked. But nonetheless, I went back with them to their place.

     We had a lunch/dinner... In Poland, meal schedules are a bit weird to grasp for a French guy. As I’ve been told, it’s probably because there are no meal breaks during working hours. So when your 8-hour shift is done, the first thing you do is eat. And then, obviously, everything gets messy. The schedules are jostled—lunch happens at 4 p.m., dinner is very late or skipped entirely, and snacks appear in between. We then arrive at situations worthy of hobbits, where we hear about first breakfast, second breakfast, the 11 a.m. snack...

     Anyway, it was such a nice meeting with this couple. They were fluent in English because they had spent more than 15 years in Japan for his work.

     There is always something common among people who have lived abroad—wherever it might be—for a period long enough to drop part of their own ways and open up to the local ones. A kind of open-mindedness emerges, where the habits and certainties specific to each place are put into perspective. I often feel this when meeting expatriates or returnees. I quite like it—knowing that we are right to do or see things as we do at home, while also putting into perspective the fact that elsewhere, "we" do things completely differently. It might even be the opposite way, but it also works. Putting my certainties into perspective has given me a lot of flexibility, that’s for sure. And tolerance too...

     But let's come back to the narrative. After eating and talking, night fell, and they had to leave. They had plans that couldn’t be postponed. So they went, feeling a bit sorry for leaving me alone, but we decided to spend the next morning together in a "cultural exchange"—an apple one. Normandy-style apple cultural exchange, also known as tart. Cultural exchanges are great!

     But see again: someone I didn’t know invited me to their home, offered me food, hot water, electricity, showed me interest, kindness, generosity, and even trust. They left me totally alone in their house for three hours.

      I don’t know about you, but for me, it feels like a blessing. Like being cared for. You see what I mean? Every day. Every day...

     I tried to wait for their return, to spend more time with them, but I probably stopped trying because I can’t recall anything after laying down on my mattress and starting to write my daily review. 

     The next thing I knew—it was morning. We had breakfast together, talking. Then we got into the apple pie. I always start with salted butter caramel, in which I brown some of the apples cut into very small pieces. That becomes my pie crust, or appareil in French, which I then spread on my shortbread dough (sandy dough? Like crumbles...)

     When I got ready to leave, he offered me a foldable and portable solar charger. He said he didn’t really use it and thought it could be very useful for me. For sure it could! The last time I tried such a device was in Tibet, attempting to charge a satellite phone. It wasn’t very convincing. But that was a dozen years ago. Maybe progress has made small photovoltaic cells more efficient.

     I was super happy—both for the gesture and for the object itself.

    If it works, I could attach the cells to the top of my bag, run the cable along to one of the portable batteries that were given to me last year, near Gorzelec and in Warsaw. Even if I have no illusions about the charging capacity, it might be enough to recharge a little bit every day—or at least to make a phone call if necessary. I’ll see. It’s not really the sunny season, but I’ll try as soon as possible.

I also try not to offend anyone, but I prefer to be invited out of interest in what I do, curiosity, generosity, or a sense of welcome, rather than out of fear—even if that fear is kindly meant for me.

After a nice breakfast, the prior invited me for coffee in his office, and we spent some more time talking.

I wasn’t inclined toward big noises, excitement, or anything else that comes with New Year’s celebrations in town. I guess I’ve become more interested in quietness and silence. That’s the effect of spending all day in peace and sleeping in forests!

    It’s strange, though. Even when I’m invited to stay indoors nowadays (which happens often), I find myself missing the cold. My hosts, out of kindness and worry—or maybe trying to compensate for the nights I’ve spent outside at minus 15°C—always propose to turn up the heating in the room I’m sleeping in, to make it cozy and warm. But I can’t. These days, I sleep best in the cold. Just being inside is enough—no heating required!

    Now that you have a sense of the atmosphere of these days, I have to tell you about my journey. Since the last time I wrote, I’ve crossed the border and arrived in Poland. Thanks to the help of a Polish friend—whom I’ve officially declared my topographic planner-in-chief—I’ve joined one of the Jacobs Ways. (For my Indian or Nepalese friends who might not know, Jacobs Ways are part of a network of pilgrimage routes across Europe that lead to Santiago de Compostela in Spain.)

    Following these routes is great for many reasons. First, they are usually very well signposted, making navigation easier. Second, they are designed to avoid big roads as much as possible. And third, they are planned by locals to guide you through beautiful places—old villages, historical sites, or cultural gems you might otherwise miss if you don’t know the area or can’t speak the language. (Speaking of which, did I mention that Polish is a nightmare to pronounce? I think I’ve sprained my tongue!)

    And here’s the best part: the Camino I’m currently following passes through three places I really wanted to visit.

    Święta Lipka, a very famous Baroque-style basilica dedicated to Mary, is located in a rather small village. It has a huge organ, and I tried to record it with my phone. Unfortunately, the phone isn’t the best tool for live recordings, and I’m afraid the result doesn’t do justice to the incredible concert I was lucky enough to stumble upon the day I arrived. Still, you can listen and let me know what you think.

    Then there was Gietrzwałd. I have to say, it’s an incredibly beautiful place. I enjoyed my time there immensely.

     Initially, I had planned to arrive in Gietrzwałd on Christmas Eve, but that morning, as I woke up in the forest just outside the town, I felt so comfortable, so at peace with the place where I had spent the night, that the idea of entering the town during the hustle and excitement of Christmas Eve made me a little anxious. So, I decided to stay in the woods for another day and night and only arrived in Gietrzwałd on the 25th. It was a wonderful decision. Honestly, if you ever take a walk and don’t know where to go, head there—it’s such a special place to be!

     Now, I’m on my way to Licheń, about 350 km ahead. Licheń isn’t on the Jacobs Way, but I just have to leave the path at one point, and it’s only 40 km south.

     One of the best parts of this journey has been crossing through beautiful old villages and towns. On the way, I’ve also encountered castles.

   One castle I passed was open for visits, and it even had a restaurant inside—double jackpot! When I arrived at the restaurant, I met some very kind people visiting Poland with their Canadian friends. It was a lovely encounter, but by now, I guess you know that all the encounters I’ve had have been wonderful—always, everywhere. It’s crazy how true that is.

    They invited me to share their table for lunch and were very curious about my walk. After lunch, we explored the castle together. At first, I was thrilled—walking inside a castle is like living a childhood dream, and I loved it. But then, I was disturbed by what I saw on display. I had expected swords, crossbows, armor, and chain mail—things that stir the imagination of an adventurer. Instead, 90% of the exhibition consisted of torture instruments and machines from the Middle Ages, used by the Inquisition and other authorities of the time.

    It was overwhelming, even sickening, to see such an extensive collection. The sheer variety, complexity, and elaborateness of these devices left me feeling deeply unsettled. The contrast between the cruelty on display and the peaceful, meaningful journey I’ve been on for the past 15 months created a dissonance I found hard to bear.

    Thankfully, shortly after that, I was warmly welcomed by a community of religious sisters. Their kindness and hospitality quickly helped erase the heavy feeling, restoring my sense of peace.

    And then, sometime later, I arrived in the town with the castle where Copernicus lived for many years! That was a much nicer visit!

   Yes, I am a pilgrim, not a tourist. I don’t go out of my way to visit many places, but when something is directly on my path, of course, I let the child in me enjoy it. What could be better than a drawbridge, a dungeon, a crenellated guardrail, a trebuchet, a mangonel, a ballista, or a catapult?

    Voila. This whole journey feels like a dream I am living. Truly, it seems like my feet barely touch the ground these days. Maybe that’s why my backpack feels so heavy! Did I tell you I gave it a name? "Expiation"—I find it very fitting while making a Christian pilgrimage.

    Even when something bad happens, I am so lucky that it often turns into something good. Let me give you another example because I’m not sure it’s easy to imagine how much, how often, and in what ways things just seem to work out.

    One day, I was invited by a religious sister I met as I was leaving the church next to her congregation house. She welcomed me to stay the night, which meant I had a bed, but most importantly—a bathtub. A hot bath. I hadn’t taken a full hot bath in over three years! (I see you raising an eyebrow: yes, three years without a bath, but I take showers... sometimes.) But a bath! You know what I mean—being fully submerged in steaming hot water, getting clean, sure, but more than that, truly relaxing... until, of course, the phone rings or someone knocks on the door, because that’s a universal truth of bathtubs!

    At the time, I was expecting a parcel from my friends in France for my birthday. I asked the sister to call my next destination to see if they could receive the parcel and hold it for me until I arrived. Unfortunately, it wasn’t possible. I wasn’t too worried because, these days, solutions always seem to appear.

    And, sure enough, the next morning, Sister Alina (yes, I think it’s fine to share her name) came to me with a solution. Someone who overheard her story about the failed parcel plan, without knowing me at all, without ever having met me, offered to have the parcel sent to his home. Not only that, but he and his wife promised to bring it to me by car, wherever I might be!

    I explained to him that I didn’t know exactly where I would be when the parcel arrived and that I was afraid I might be far away by then. But he simply said, “No problem, I’ll drive to you.” And that’s exactly what happened! Ten days later, he received the parcel, contacted me for my location, and after finishing his workday, he and his wife drove two hours to meet me. They handed over the package, chatted with me for a while, and then drove back home. Just like that.

    And here’s the kicker: that very night, after I received the parcel—which included replacement parts for my tent—the temperature dropped so much that the aluminum structure of my tent froze and snapped. Like... well, like frozen aluminum. (A very apt analogy, wouldn’t you agree?) But because I had just received the new parts, I was able to repair the tent immediately.

    This morning, another aluminum pole snapped... But it’s just that I walked exactly where I shouldn’t have. There are mornings like this. But hey, you know what? I arrived in a village just after that and got invited to stay for the night, even though it was still morning. So after spending a few hours talking, washing myself, and doing my laundry, I managed to repair my tent, so tomorrow will be fine! Great, no?

    I used the extra time to write you this message. I could have done it while staying at the pilgrim center in Gietrzwald, but I wasn’t in the mood. I started writing it, but then thought I’d finish it in the tent... But guess what? It’s too cold to keep your arms out of the sleeping bag, and if you only keep your hands out, you get frostbite on your fingers in minutes. So I guess I had to meet these people. And this meeting could easily not have happened because, when I arrived in the village, I realized I had forgotten my hat on a bench about 1.5 km back. This has never happened before—first time. So I walked back to fetch it, then walked all the way back to the village where I met these people and got invited.

    Now, another story—and the last.

    I got sick in mid-December. I was invited by such kind people, really nice. A beautiful family. Then I was invited to stay a little longer to rest. And I was happy because it was such a nice place with such nice people. So I proposed to cook an apple tarte, Normandy style. I’m very easygoing when it comes to making tarts... Let’s call it cultural exchange!

     So I was in the middle of preparing this tart, and I tried a piece of apple to get an idea of how much sugar I should use. And guess what? I had the worst, no-taste apple possible. You know those industrial tomatoes, the ones genetically modified by companies that want to save the world from hunger? One very famous one starts with "MON" and ends with "ENTO." I got exactly the same sensation tasting this apple as when eating their tomatoes. No smell, no taste... You only know it’s a tomato (or apple) because it looks like one before you bite into it. After that, it’s just your brain trying to fill in the blanks for those who know what a tomato or apple is supposed to smell and taste like. (I guess those who’ve never had a real one might get shocked when they finally taste one from a traditional seed-grown garden.) But I’m digressing—excuse me.

    I was really disappointed with the apples! It’s basically very hard to make a good apple tarte if the apples aren’t tasty or sugary at all. I guess my disappointment was really obvious because Ania and Krzysztof (yes, I use names, but that’s between us!) asked me what was wrong. Then they tried the apples themselves and were very satisfied...

     So either they had no taste in apples, or I had lost mine...

     Do you see where this is going? No?

     They explained to me, while handing me a bottle of essential oil to test if I could smell anything, that one of the effects of a certain virus going around these days (you’ve probably heard of it) is the total loss of taste and smell.

     Imagine the situation: I was with people who had welcomed me very kindly and generously into their home, and I realized there that I was sick with the virus. Honestly, I felt really, really uncomfortable at the time. There’s so much being said about this virus that eventually, I felt guilty—not because of the disease itself, as my doctor had already warned me that I wasn’t part of the population at risk of developing a severe form, but because I was afraid of the fear this virus arouses in people. And, of course, I was afraid of transmitting it to others.

     You can perhaps appreciate the delicacy of the situation. Guilt is the easiest emotion to arouse in humans. But I didn’t even have time to dwell on that because my hosts immediately asked me to stay with them until I got better. They tried to put me at ease straight away, reassuring me that I could and should stay with them.

     They insisted I shouldn’t be afraid, and that’s when I told them, “I’m not afraid of the disease; I’m afraid of the fear of the disease.” They understood me completely and reassured me by saying, first, that she had already been sick and it hadn’t been a big deal for her. Second, he was in great health, and even when she was sick, he hadn’t caught the virus. Then she mentioned that her parents, who were coming to visit that weekend, were vaccinated.

     So, all was okay. She promised to prepare me herbal teas and potions and assured me I had nothing to worry about. I could stay with them without any problem, doubt, or guilt. And I did. And it worked out. After five days of full rest—two of which I spent mostly sleeping—I got slightly better every day. By the fifth day, I was totally fine, and my taste and smell had returned. That part had been the worst because they cooked such nice food, and I couldn’t enjoy it. I mean, I couldn’t even tell the difference, except by texture, between raw garlic, peanuts, or a piece of chocolate! How horrible is that?

     Once I got better, I proposed a new cultural exchange in the form of a chocolate pear tart and a spinach pie with Roquefort. They’re easy, fast, and good. I’ve made them dozens of times since leaving France, though I’ve succeeded in only a few of those attempts. Thankfully, this was one of the successful ones.

     I stayed with them for about a dozen days, and honestly, I’m so grateful I fell sick while in their care. I had already been considering stocking up on food and water, heading into the woods, putting up my tent in a remote, hidden spot, and staying there for a week. But instead, I ended up with these wonderful people.

    On top of being friendly and welcoming, their reaction was so cool, full of normality and reason, which seems rare these days. I was deeply touched. I felt at home, and they appreciated that I felt that way.

     So basically, I got sick, but it turned out to be a nice experience! Cool, no?

    And it brought me to Gietrzwałd for Christmas, which, in a way, is perfect timing for the pilgrim I am. (You know, Ania and Krzysztof insisted that I spend Christmas with them and their whole family. I know they would have really liked that, and they even offered to come pick me up from where I was. But, well, you know me and birthdays... I declined. Still, I feel so lucky. Truly.)

     Voila. I think it’s time for me to stop writing. It’s getting really late, and I still have about an hour to go to send this along with pictures to everyone. So, yes, that’s it for today. I have no idea how long this message is. Hopefully, not too much. I’ll add lots of pictures to balance it out!

     Anyway...

     I wish you all a beautiful year.
     I wish you well and happy.

Goldap and Andrzej

22/11/2021

0 Comments

 
    The fact that I am receiving more and more messages asking me for news tells me that I may have once again stretched the time limit for updating my progress. And now, as I reread myself, I realize that this message is going to be really long. Maybe to compensate for the delay. Well, if it’s too much, don’t hesitate to complain.

     And something else: regarding the photos, I am experiencing an as-yet unsolved problem. They are missing, or at least a large part of the period I am writing about now. I do not know why or if I will be able to find them in the depths of my phone’s memory, or if they are definitely gone. So I apologize to those of you who enjoy the photos—especially those who don’t care much for the reading and just like to look at the pictures!

     The last time I wrote to you, I stopped at the Hill of Crosses, of which I have fond memories, though they already feel a little distant. There has been a lot of water under the bridge since then—and lots of things floating with it.

    There was Šiluva, the fourth destination on my journey. A place I’ll let you discover on the internet if you wish, as it is described there far better and in more detail than I could manage. I stayed there for four days, hosted by the pilgrims’ center. The team in charge of the center and welcoming pilgrims was very dynamic and enthusiastic. Once again, I can only partially convey the warmth of the welcome I received, the meetings I had there, and the quality of the discussions we shared.

    It exceeded my expectations. And the place itself! Well, let’s just say that I’m glad I heard about it and had the chance to be there.

    When I felt ready and rested, I chose to resume my way towards Gietrzwałd, via Święta Lipka in Poland this time. This route would allow me to go around the small part of Russia that lies between Poland and Lithuania, as I didn’t want to cross Russia (visas and administrative costs are troubles a pilgrim can do without). In doing so, it also took me through Kaunas.

    Kaunas is the second-largest city in Lithuania, but let me specify that the concept of a big city is very subjective. Second-largest city in Lithuania—that must be considered at the scale of the country, of course. I say this for my Indian friends, for example, for whom the second-largest city in their country must be Mumbai, with a population six times greater than the entire Lithuanian population, not counting the Mumbai agglomeration...

   Although I am not fond of walking through large cities, the need to renew some of my equipment, wardrobe, and shoes made the stop necessary. The presence of a military store that could provide more durable materials and equipment, combined with the old town and many beautiful buildings and churches, made the prospect more appealing. Additionally, I was informed about the presence of a pilgrim shelter there, which made the city all the more accessible, and ultimately, the choice as a stopover was made quite naturally.

    And I’m really glad I did...

    First and foremost, because the trip between Šiluva and Kaunas was one of the most picturesque country paths I’ve had the opportunity to walk. In the midst of verdant nature with autumnal colors, I walked along a small stream, then a bigger one, taking care to enjoy these magnificent and almost wild places as much as possible.

    Besides, I would like to tell you about a meeting with this nature that happened there, but I want to do it nicely and fully, and I’m afraid it might be too long. Too much of a digression for here, so I will include it as a postscriptum.

    Then, and I know it becomes redundant, but I am not tired of it—the welcome I received in this city was really, really nice. I was lucky to be hosted in the center of the old town, probably the most beautiful part of the city. And so, as hoped, I took advantage of the pretty town (churches, old streets, squares, and buildings), but also of the practical side (buying the necessary equipment and food, washing my sleeping bag that was smellily screaming for it!).

  And the meetings—again, always, everywhere. I was not only welcomed, guided, and accompanied in my efforts but also taken to visit superb places that would otherwise have remained unknown to me (it’s very annoying not to be able to show you pictures!).

  And there again, in those meetings—I am not tired of it—each time, the kindness, the availability, the generosity, and the accompaniment, if needed or desired.

   Then I resumed my way towards the border. Easy and slow. Really slow because over the past few weeks, my back pains have given me small reminders. Nothing in comparison to before, but still, I’ve learned to be gentler with myself and to listen to my body’s complaints. I forced myself to walk only one day out of two, sometimes one day out of three. It was necessary and beneficial.

    I am stretching better and better, more and more, which is a really good sign! The way—to be walked far and long—teaches you to be kind and fair to yourself. The need is to be happy. Happiness gives wings. But not only that; somehow, I believe it heals...

   I am now in Poland, in the small town of Gołdap, from where I am writing to you. It is 3 km from the Russian border.

   If you keep up with "current affairs," you are likely informed about the situation with the instrumentalization of migrants from the Middle East, flown by charters to Belarus and then guided to Poland and Lithuania with the obvious aim of political destabilization and financial gain. For me, this results in being checked by flying customs controls—up to three times a day.

    Nothing unpleasant—the checks often end in nice discussions about what moves me...

    And it’s not just the border police cars that stop; sometimes people do so as well, just to check on me—with kindness, to help, or out of curiosity. And that’s how I met Andrzej (Andre, but we are in Poland, so you have to add “z” or “w” all over the place. I’m sure it’s just to make certain words unpronounceable for tourists or pilgrims. Only certain words, though, because in this example, Andrzej is written unpronounceable but pronounced Andre. Crazy tongue movements in my mouth...).

    But to make it short, the car stops, the window goes down, and Andrzej gets very enthusiastic when I finish the short briefing of Emmanuel Gump—the roadside version...

   He is seriously ill. A former soldier in forced retirement due to an unknown or poorly understood illness (we discussed in English, and Andrzej’s English is certainly much better than my Polish, but still a little light for technical details). He tells me it’s very good that we meet, and asks if I could bring prayer intentions to Gietrzwałd for him.

    Of course—certainly—it’s my pilgrim duty, and I ask him to write them down in the notebook I reserve for this purpose.

    He then asks about my route and exclaims that I’ll be passing through his city, where I must stop. He doesn’t guarantee that his wife will agree to lodge me, but he says he’ll speak to the priest and assures me that I’ll be welcomed anyway. I have no doubt about that. Truly. No matter where, when, or how, I know it’s going to be alright.

    We exchange phone numbers, and I resume my journey.

  The next day, I receive an SMS from him with a photo of "his" church. He gives me an appointment there the following day at 5 p.m.

    Funny thing—when I arrive in Gołdap the next day as planned, but much earlier (around 12:30 p.m.), I look at my map for the church. Seeing only one, I head for it. But when I see its steeple, I realize it doesn’t match the photo he sent. And I can’t see any other church nearby.

    So I decide to ask passersby. First, a lady who doesn’t speak English (nor French, nor Tamil—well, okay, I didn’t ask about Tamil). Then a delivery boy who eventually speaks Russian. Finally, I consider approaching the only individual remaining in the area, a few hundred meters down the street. I think that if he doesn’t speak English either, that’s fine—I’ll just call Andrzej.

   But before I can reach him, he speaks to me from a distance—in English—to ask if I need anything. Before I can respond, he’s already reached me.

    Guess what: it’s Andrzej!

  He was passing by on his way back home from a friend’s house. He couldn’t believe the coincidence. I don’t want to sound jaded, but I’m used to it now. For him, it’s a big coincidence; for me, it’s Friday. I mean, it’s been 13 months that everything has been going better than expected, and I’m finally really confident.

    At the beginning, faced with this level of coincidence, I might have hesitated (not for long) to consider the possibility of surveillance of the pilgrim’s activities by the Polish secret services. But now I know it’s just like this. It’s become routine (but not boring!). Welcome to the pilgrim world...

    We talk together while walking. There is indeed a room waiting for me. Yup! Hot shower (necessary...) and everything else that goes with a roof in general—you know the drill already...

    He leaves me at the church, where I have to wait for someone to pick me up. The priests are busy; it may take a while. Andrzej is also very busy and has to leave immediately. So I stay in the church, which suits me well.

    It’s small, cute, warm, old, and nice. I put my backpack in the entrance, sit on a bench, close my eyes, and before I realize it, an hour and a half have passed. It feels good to close your eyes sometimes. This was one of those times.

    When I take my backpack back, because Father Mathieu comes to get me and invites me to follow him to my room, I notice a plastic bag placed on it, under my hat, which was on top. Inside: a chocolate bar, bananas, and dry sausages.

    Someone, totally unknown to me—Andrzej, the priests, or someone else—went to the store next door after seeing my bag, or me, or both, bought these little treats, and left them under my hat. I didn’t see anything and have no idea who could have done it.

    But I’ve been here less than two hours, and this has already happened. Isn’t it touching? Seriously. Sweetness. Kind attention.

    I’ll give away the dry sausages—I’m a vegetarian, you know—but I’ll keep the intention well in my heart. This kind of attention feeds a man well. The chocolate, too, for sure! ??

   I live this permanently... I do not know if you realize... for the past 13 months. Sometimes, remembering the "mainstream" discourse of the media, or the almost universally morose discourse, I feel like I’m not on the same planet. Be careful, I was warned when I left: nowadays, you never know!

    I am careful—I pay attention with enthusiasm and interest, because yes, you never know. The good, the beautiful, the kindness could crash joyfully against my armor of projections, prejudices, certainties, and precautions. Without paying attention, without being careful, I might not realize it and miss all of this. That would be a pity—a shame—sad...

    I am currently staying in one of the parish houses in the city. Father Mathieu and Father Albert are welcoming me with a touching quality as well. And again, I meet and discover. I stayed two full days here; there were a lot of repairs to be done on my backpack (I had to buy stronger sewing thread).

    When I’m ready to resume my journey, it will be with a backpack filled with homemade cheeses I was offered, dishes prepared for my journey by Ewa, the housekeeper here, a jar of very good honey, a dozen eggs from Father Albert’s mother’s house, and a bottle of hooch that, if I understood Father Albert correctly, is very good for me.

    He explained—in pretty good French, considering how long it’s been since he last practiced it—that this drink, made from black elderberries, has powerful "reheating" properties, which should help during the winter. At 82 degrees of alcohol, I can believe it. I haven’t tried it yet—I’m saving it for the big frosts in the tent. Along with the bottle of "homemade," he gave me a small hooch glass on a pendant with straps and a leather pouch. I’m well-equipped for the road.

    Our Lady of Gietrzwałd, here I come...

    Voilà. That’s all for today. Well, almost, because I went on a "little" bit in the postscript...

    Anyway, I wish you all well and happy!

    Emmanuel

    PS (long one):

    In the photos, there was one of my tent by a river. I took it on the woodland route, superb in color during this autumn season, when I was along this river between Šiluva and Kaunas... Bucolic... natural... green... Superb...

    I surely wasn’t the only one who thought this place deserved a stop since a wooden bench had been placed there, in the middle of this great nothingness full of nature. A few meters from the bank of the river below, in the middle of a small grassy meadow, with a magnificent view despite the bumps and hollows, like small hills and valleys. There was a small patch of flat grass next to the bench, flat and large enough for me to pitch my tent.

    There was no hesitation. It was obvious that I was going to sleep here. I had to sleep here.

    It may seem strange to you or hard to believe, but for over a year that I’ve been sleeping here and there, many of the places where I decide to stay the night impose themselves on me rather than the other way around. And often, those are the places where I sleep well. It’s astonishing, but when I come across these places, it’s obvious, certain. I sleep there... and I sleep well.

    Once the tent was set up, the bench that had been waiting there all along did its job. I sat and watched the water flow. It’s good to watch the water flow. Really good.

    And it must have been a while since I started watching the water because the sun had gone down behind me by the time she arrived.

    I say “she,” but actually, I have no idea if it was a she or a he. The other thing I didn’t know was what it was.

    But it was super cute, that’s for sure—really super cute.

   It is often in those moments when I find myself at a standstill—whether for rest or contemplation—when my noisy and olfactory intrusion has finished disturbing the place and those who live there, when calm and silence prevail again through my immobility, and with a little luck, a contrary wind, that the most beautiful encounters occur.

    And this one was a magnificent one. Do you remember the wolf that appeared, which I observed a few months ago in Poland? But there have been many others—many, but all unique and memorable... A pair of foxes once followed each other and played together, wild boars and baby boars, roe deer, deer, hinds, birds... It’s always captivating to me.

    This was the first time I saw her. Her and her kind. For now, we’ll agree—very arbitrarily—that it was a she. But a what? I’m not sure... To be honest, I really don’t know.

    I do know her family well, however—the mustelids. The problem is, it’s a (very) large family: from polecats to martens, including badgers, weasels, wolverines, ferrets, minks, and more. Well, these are the ones I’ve heard of or even seen, but there are many more. I haven’t yet looked for a picture online, but I’ll try to find it...

    Because, of course, at that moment, even though I really wanted to take her portrait—because she was as cute as can be, and I assure you, those little beasts are insanely cute—even though I really wanted to, I didn’t move an inch. Not even a hair.

    I wanted to watch her. Take my time. Not move and spoil the moment, as I did with the wolf.

   I observed, prolonging the moment as much as possible before she realized, with horror, that she was within just a few steps—3 meters at most—of the most harmful and dangerous species of mammal on earth (and perhaps even of the universe; we don’t yet have proof to the contrary): a human...

    A little smaller and thinner than a ferret, with pretty light gray fur. Lighter than mouse gray—ash gray, I would say.

    She had just climbed the embankment from the bank of the river to the small clearing where I was going to spend the night. She stood there, motionless, and I couldn’t tell if she was looking at me or frozen, on the lookout for the slightest movement or smell, perhaps blinded by the sun, which was now behind me but certainly in her eyes.

    After a wonderfully long moment of stillness, she got on her hind legs and stood above the grass to get a better view (animals know that to see better, sometimes you need to gain height! Oh captain, my captain!).

    If she was cute before, suddenly, I had no words for what she became now. I was speechless—and couldn’t have spoken anyway. I was barely breathing.

    Then she continued on her way, resuming whatever she had been doing before, without haste—therefore probably without fear—completely unaware of the horror of her encounter. (What could be more horrifying for a wild animal than meeting a human! Meeting two humans?)

    Leaving me in awe of her beauty, I went to bed happy like a kid who had just seen his first wild mustelid and loved it.

    I ate a little, then "closed" the tent (which is a big word since the zippers of both the flysheet and the inner tent are long gone). I use safety pins to close the flysheet effectively (it works very well, phew!) and have given up, months ago, on the idea of closing the inner tent. Goodbye mosquito net and privacy. Everything that can pass under the flysheet has direct access to the half-wild and hopefully sound-asleep Emmanuel. Hello spiders, beetles, earwigs, and so on.

    The slugs and snails, though, are the ones whose "cuddling" I appreciate the least. Who likes sloppy wet kisses, I ask you?

    So I "closed" my tent and fell asleep in an instant as soon as it got dark. Basically at 5 p.m. Well, yes, the days were still long back then. Now it’s more like 3:30 p.m. for sunset and 4:00 p.m. for full night. It’s strange to me, I admit.

    And I slept really well. Very well—until I woke up. And I woke up.

    That, too, is something surprising. When we sleep "outside," there’s a sort of inner wakefulness, as if part of our brain (reptilian?) remains alert to give warning if necessary. Like a sudden attack of slobbery wet kisses, for example—brrr—or a creature wriggling at my feet.

    Now fully awake but taking care to remain still and breathe calmly, listening, I tried to determine what had woken me. And without waiting too long, an animal, about the size of a cat, began to fidget—right at my feet, and even partly on them.

    Do you remember that night when a cat came to visit me? After overcoming its apprehensions, it entered my tent and stayed for the night. Frenzied cuddles several times during the night, followed by purring and sleeping on my sleeping bag—it was a warm encounter, full of sweetness.

    But I was pretty sure it wasn’t a cat this time. Most likely, it was my afternoon friend.

   I say friend—I was fully her friend. From my side, at least. As for her feelings, I didn’t really know yet.

    But I admit, I had hope...

    She was walking between the flysheet and the inner tent. A few times, she put her front legs on the stretched canvas of the inner tent, giving me the feeling she was climbing on my feet...

    How to tell you—I was full of hope that the brotherly meeting of two predators, one of which was super cute and both super hairy, could end in sweet hugs. She wasn’t afraid; just for that, I liked her. No particular inhibition, from what I could tell, because I started to move, and she stayed. Any other animal would have scampered away in fright and howls. But not her. She was like this, you see...

    Soon, I started talking to her softly.

    Okay, let me stop you right there—no, I wasn’t talking to her so much to tell her something, and even less to expect an answer. I’m not that crazy yet. But I used my voice to convey feelings of calm, harmlessness, and assurance.

    Animals have an intuitive, nonverbal perception capacity. We do too, of course—it’s what allows us to perceive during a phone conversation that the person on the other end is smiling. But animals are even better at this. They can feel our attitude.

    And it must have worked, at least a little, because she didn’t flee despite my words. She paused for a moment, then resumed her activity.

    I was really starting to consider contact! Seriously! Being able to stroke the animal would be like Christmas come early!!! We weren’t there yet, but hey—you know boys!

    I took my phone to use the flashlight function and discovered two very disappointing things.

    The first is that the flashlight function is antagonistic to the camera. So I couldn’t show you the magnificent little creature. Well, I say little, but she was about the size of a good-sized cat. Shorter legs. And a small muzzle with lovely whiskers.

    The second—and this crushed my romantic hopes of spontaneous contact between two critters—was that she was busy with the little bag containing my rubbish and food scraps. I always take care to gather these while waiting for the opportunity to dispose of them in the first trash can I come across.

    She had grabbed the bag, carried it to my feet, and began extracting its contents.

    That might mean nothing to you, but for me, it meant she wasn’t there for my beautiful eyes, but for the olfactory hope of a nighttime snack.

    It was a hard blow—for me and my dreams of soft, furry caresses. (Bah, yes, it must be super soft in addition to being super cute! People make coat collars out of it, let me remind you! And let’s admit it, the fur really makes you want to touch it. Forget the fine teeth at one end of the thing and the fart-spray anal glands at the other!)

    It puts things in perspective and me back in my place.

    And yet, I still wanted to believe it. After waiting for her to move to the other side of the tent, I reached out to retrieve the plastic bag—not easy at all, but I thought it was better to do it now than later, when all its contents would be scattered everywhere!

   And then, as she still didn’t leave and my romantic hopes revived, I decided to try indirect contact—through the thin canvas of the tent. I touched her, almost stroked her. Almost, because there was the thin tent canvas between us and, quite honestly, because she didn’t really let it happen.

    She still didn’t run away but didn’t let herself be touched either. As soon as the sensation became more than her own friction against the canvas—my hand pressing gently against her through the fabric—she changed position. She didn’t leave, but she wouldn’t let herself be caressed.

    Finally, she began to focus her activity on my left side, right at the height of my elbow. To be precise, exactly where I store my food in the tent. Food that was protected only by the thin canvas and a Tupperware box, though she couldn’t know about the latter.

    So yes, she was only here for the promises of her nose. I, meanwhile, counted for nothing. The story—our story—was doomed to failure before it could even begin.

    Realizing this, the whole situation suddenly seemed absurd, a bit drawn out, a waste of time. I figured it was better to let the night offer the possibility of good sleep.

    That hope vanished abruptly when she began to emit little shrill cries that, without a shadow of a doubt, reflected frustration, annoyance, and finally, anger.

    It was around this time that the cute little furry creature began to turn into a real little evil pest. Forgive the expression.

   She started to attack the tent—or rather, not attack it outright, but in her frustration and in trying to reach what she was smelling, she began biting or trying to bite the canvas of the inner tent.

    Biting a stretched canvas is not an easy task.

   I was watching her, resting on my elbow, using the arm whose hand held my phone. The phone’s flashlight beam projected between the flysheet and the inner tent, while my right hand pulled on the tent canvas to widen the gap between the two, clearing my view of the otherwise cramped space where she was putting on her show.

    We were 12 inches apart—snout to snout.

    Now visibly frustrated, she managed to sink her teeth into the canvas. When her grip was firm, she gave a sharp little jerk, pulling with her whole body and stepping back in short, jerky movements...

    There, I really stopped agreeing with the situation. As cute as she was (I mean, you should have seen her jerking back—it was adorable!).

    "But would you stop, you little evil," I said, remembering that the tone was more important than the words themselves.

    We had to find an arrangement quickly, or it was going to be a very long night. An idea—almost a plan—came to me and seemed relevant: if she was no longer hungry, she would leave me alone. To sum it up.

    So I opened the magic box, took out my knife, and opted for a piece of Brie. A good piece of Brie stuck at the end of the knife.

    She sniffed, then very quickly grabbed the piece, took a few steps back (it’s super cute when they go backwards!), landed on her hind legs, chest upright, holding the piece of cheese with her front paws. She began tearing off small pieces, chewing them carefully before swallowing.

    You might say I’m repeating myself, but God, this little beast was cute when eating something other than my tent! I could have watched her like that for hours.

    She devoured the first piece very quickly, the second too, and the third more slowly. I never got bored. In addition to the hope of having satiated her so she would leave me alone, watching her eat the Brie—me being from Normandy—I felt a kind of connection, a kind of sympathy. After all, we shared certain tastes. It wasn’t all totally hopeless between us...

    And finally, she left.

    I was a little disappointed, all the same. I am sure of the potential for fraternization. After all, these animals have been tamed for a long time, either as pets or as burrow-hunting animals...

    But she was gone. And I could relax, go back to sleep.

    But she came back.

    And by the time she woke me up for the second time, she had already made a hole 7 cm long by 4 cm wide.

    That’s when I went for contact—but more firmly this time. No caresses.

    With my phone light in one hand, I watched her closely. Each time she was about to bite the canvas, I hit her on the muzzle from the other side.

    At first, just gentle taps, accompanied by reprimands with no real conviction—I mean, I was still under her charm and couldn’t bring myself to hit or scold her in anger.

    But I have to admit, my patience was slowly running out.

    I was really impressed by her determination—and her insolence too! After all, wasn’t she aware that she was in the presence of the worst mammal in the galaxy? Seriously? The representative of the supreme masters of all other forms of life on Earth, because intelligent, industrious, and artistic (and egocentric too...)?

    She took a dozen taps on the muzzle. The last one was no longer a joke—it was a slap. A good one. And the beast fled. Phew!

    Sad that it had to come to this—for a piece of Norman cheese! But now, my tent had two big torn holes and several smaller bite marks...

   I was exhausted and needed sleep, but I couldn’t fall back asleep right away. The night was dragging on. And just as I was finally dozing off again, guess what—of course, she came back a third time.

    Around two in the morning, another wake-up, another hole.

    At that point, I began to doubt my victory over the little monster. The separation had to happen—now. A divorce, before she took the whole tent apart. But how to do that without resorting to brutality? I couldn’t go beyond the slap; it had already made my heart ache.

  I really wondered how to get out of this relational impasse without ending in a brutal heartbreak...

   That’s when I remembered the little pepper spray given to me by a Ukrainian security guard in Germany. He had handed it to me, saying it was for wildlife and possibly humans (apparently, sprays specifically for wildlife are stronger but forbidden for use on humans). He had insisted on telling me that there were a lot of junkies around.

    Well, personally, in the middle of the woods, I had very rarely encountered junkie attacks. (The junkie is, after all, an urban animal.) But we all have our fears, I suppose. Mine? Slobbering wet kisses attacks...

    In short, I looked for the small spray and, despite the probable discomfort of using it in a closed—or almost closed—space, I threatened my assailant with it.

    And at that moment, I also decided that for being so persistent, she had to be a he.

    Yes, I know—big discriminatory clichés, totally against any concept of gender equality.

    But hey, it was the feeling of the moment, at three in the morning, on a restless night.

   The first pchitt was almost nonexistent and therefore totally ineffective. I was reluctant... But when he started working on his fourth hole, he got a proper pchitt on the muzzle. A little one. Half a second. Maybe even less.

    I didn’t even see him leave—it was so fast.

    But it had worked.

    And now I was there, and he was gone. I was relieved but disappointed. Disappointed and a little sad...

   Fortunately, I had the opportunity to take some air for a good hour, to clear my head and properly ventilate the tent!

   I hope the critter hasn’t had any problems because of this. I don’t think so. I am a little disappointed, not really surprised—except by his cheek, his daring, his determination...

    But honestly, I remain under his spell. I assure you, these little furballs are so cute. Really...

    And now, I won’t advise you to go on the internet to check whether mustelids might be a key link in triggering an unprecedented historical event—a panzootic (animal-to-human pandemic). According to Wikipedia in 2020, “mink farms are the animal farms that have been most affected by the SARS-CoV-2 virus responsible for COVID-19. If this virus were to spread among wild mustelids, the latter could become a permanent reservoir of infection for other animal species, as was the case with rabies transmitted to raccoons and skunks, or with bovine tuberculosis transmitted to badgers, or with paratuberculosis, transmissible to many wild ruminants and sometimes to immunosuppressed humans.”

    But hey, without going as far as "Haaa, my God, it’s awful, we’re all going to die," it’s true that mustelids become less cute when you read that.

    I guess.

   On my side, I loved the encounter. Even if I now have three big holes in my tent (though compared to the gaping hole at the entrance where the zipper is completely out of use, the three side holes feel conceptually negligible).

    And he looked in great shape—fully healthy, the little devil!
