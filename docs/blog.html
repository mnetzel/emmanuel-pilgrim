Five years walking across Europe — 15 countries, 10,000 km, 5 winters. And now, a pause...

28/8/2025 0 Comments

       Dear you all,

For the past 4 years and 11 months, I have had the privilege of walking across Europe on a pilgrimage, visiting about 150 Marian places in 15 countries.

I did it in one continuous journey, crossing France, Belgium, Luxembourg, Germany, Poland, Lithuania, Latvia, then Lithuania again, Poland again, the Czech Republic, Slovakia, Hungary, Romania, Serbia, Bosnia and Herzegovina, Montenegro, and Croatia — covering about 10,000 km. Ten thousand kilometers with my backpack, my hat, my stick, all on foot, in the tradition of pilgrimage.

I can’t explain how beautiful and powerful that experience was. How magnificent it was to learn freedom, happiness, to cross mountains, forests, rivers, and beautiful lands. To sleep beside lakes, under the shadow of trees, on a soft mattress of moss, on sandy beaches. To meet so many kind people, receiving the warm gift of their hospitality, the incredible gift of their trust, their beautiful curiosity about me, about my way, about my approach to spirituality, to life — or simply to pilgrimage itself.

I don’t feel as if I spent 5 years on the road. It passed in a flash, the seasons rolling like the weeks of a single month. Yes, 5 months — no more. That’s how it felt. But not for my body, not for my flesh.

So yes, I must tell you: I decided to come back to France. I just arrived yesterday, and I am going to stay for a while before resuming my pilgrimage. I need to recover my health, which slowed me down so much over the past 2 years. And I also need to earn and save enough money for the next part.

Because I am not done yet. Far from it. Pilgrimage is a practice. A spiritual practice. An inner quest. You know how long it can be if you truly aim to go deep, to learn truth, and to meet your Self.

My plan — or more honestly, my wish — is to continue as soon as I am ready. I am less than halfway. There are still many destinations to reach, and my intended path was this:
Croatia, Slovenia, Austria, southern Germany, Switzerland, southern France, Italy, then by ferry to Albania, Kosovo, North Macedonia, Greece, Turkey, Lebanon, Israel, Jordan, Egypt, Libya, Tunisia, Algeria, Morocco, then by ferry to Portugal, through Spain, back to France, Belgium, the Netherlands, western Germany, and finally southern Poland — where I was initially going, where it all began. A long way still ahead.

That is the plan, or maybe just the hope. It would have been fantastic to complete it all in one stretch. But I will wait, and prepare. Hopefully the world will have time to calm down a little, for peace to come, for the way to clear.

If this seems sudden or rushed to you, it is not. My silence these past months may already have been a sign that I was going through hard times. And a few weeks ago, arriving in Dubrovnik, Croatia, I decided I should stop there, because I was going so slowly, with such difficulty, that it no longer made much sense to continue.

But crossing the town, visiting its churches and resting in each one, taking something like 11 hours to cross what I could normally walk in just one, gave me new energy — and I changed my mind. I see now, five weeks later, that I actually made the decision to stop there. It took me all this time to accept it, to integrate it.

So when the moment came to take the decision again, it was easy. Painless. Obvious. I was ready. I was sure, determined. I have no regrets. I gave everything I could. I went fully into this experience, this practice. I am at peace with myself in letting it take its course.

I am happy. I am grateful. I feel lucky. Blessed. And contrary to what I feared, I find myself calm and serene in the middle of this restless activity — such a contrast with the 5 years of silence and solitude I just lived. Yet it does not affect me, does not disturb me. I stand in the middle of this agitation, I recognize it, and I am well. It gives me a slightly surreal feeling, a kind of detachment.

Now I will prepare for the rest, and let you know when the time comes.

We can stay in touch — I would love that.

Thank you so much for everything.

I wish you well.
     I wish you happiness.

Emmanuel

Crossing the Balkans

11/1/2025 2 Comments

A lot of time has passed since the last update. I had many opportunities to write, but I didn't really feel like I had anything particular to say. So, I didn't.

I could have shared that over the past few months of autumn, I traveled across Serbia, passing through Belgrade and the mountains, receiving a warm welcome everywhere I went. I could have mentioned that this year wasn’t very good for mushroom hunting in the area I visited, as the summer was too hot and dry, and the autumn wasn’t rainy enough to sufficiently moisten the soil for the mycelium to grow. But I didn’t...

I could have used the fourth anniversary of my departure on September 26, 2020, as an occasion to send an update...

Or the milestone of 9,000 km that I reached upon entering Bosnia-Herzegovina, although we all know how imprecise this calculation can be.

I could have mentioned the Christmas celebrations, celebrated at least three times depending on the church, of course...

But no, I wasn't really in the mood, I guess... and I felt it was better not to share than to force myself to write you irrelevant or uninteresting news. Though it doesn't mean that the one I am writing now will be relevant and interesting, but at least it comes from a genuine desire to share, strong enough to make me break my comfortable silence.

I am now in Bosnia and Herzegovina, between the capital of Sarajevo and the town of Mostar, where I am headed. I found a nice route on my maps to get there. A path through the mountains, avoiding the major roads in the valley. I even found a water source along the way, so I could easily get water without having to carry too much. It was perfect—a solid plan! So I set off on that route. It was nice because it took me through all the places where the Olympic Games had been held before. It was strange to cross through them.

The area was beautiful, albeit a bit tough, something like a 1500 m positive elevation change from the capital... You have to know that the mountains are directly around Sarajevo. The Olympic village is maybe 25 km from the center... I had just left this lovely capital when the first snowstorm hit... beautiful.... I had to stop and find a place to pitch my tent because the snowfall was intense. I'm not sure, but I think I wasn't even technically out of the Sarajevo municipality when I pitched my tent on a tiny hill, in the woods, under a heavily cloudy sky and during a thick snow episode... I stayed the next day because it was still heavily snowing, and because why not? 😂.

The next morning, the entire area was covered with a good 50 cm of snow, with all the tree branches around bent under the weight... It was beautiful, magical as only snow can be... It was my Christmas present!

Packing the tent became a bit harder than usual as I first had to clear all the snow around, and also remove the ice from the edge of the tent roof... And then roll the frozen fabric into the bubble I had fixed under my backpack. But I managed it; this wasn’t my first rodeo. I continued on my way uphill... As I was walking on the main road, I wasn't in too much snow. There was about 5 to 10 cm depending on when the last snowplow had passed... It was still snowing, but only lightly. Nothing compared to the past two days... So cars and trucks were going slow. I felt comfortable... My plan through the mountains was good. The entire way was through roads... No paths... So I would be clear to go, as the snow plowing seemed very efficient in the area... And that is the keyword: in the area... Efficient indeed...

So I walked up and up and decided to find a place to pitch the tent again as night falls fast around 4 PM. Knowing that when there's so much snow, you have to dig the place you choose for the tent to fit, but also to have enough room to walk around, you need to count about 1 hour to build your camp... 1 hour of intense work because once the backpack is off your back and on the ground, you don't want to get cold, you don't want to start working in the night, and there is a lot of work to do. First, it's about 3 or 4 m³ of snow to shovel out... without a shovel... I use one of my food containers to do so. That was about when the second snowstorm hit me... which I found totally okay because I was about to finish and ready to get inside. And I did...

The next day, it snowed again and I stayed. The spot I had found was next to the main road. It wasn't really pleasant because of the traffic, but I didn’t have much choice. About 20 meters to the side, there was a flat area, free of trees and just big enough for my tent, but not much more. I think my tent was visible from the road, but only slightly, through some branches covered with snow and only from one specific spot on the road. Motorists would have to be looking sharply in that exact direction and not focusing on their way, and this for about 2 seconds before their view was obstructed by bushes, trees, and snow. But still, at least one person saw me, probably when I was setting up the tent. How do I know? Because at one point, while I was comfortably settled in, a policeman knocked on my tent door. He might have called me from the road, but I had my audio earplugs in as I was listening to a book to drown out the traffic noise. The guy was tense, annoyed... probably because he had to walk the 35-meter path in 60 cm of snow, slalom around trees, following my footsteps to my camp. When I opened the tent and looked out at him, he wasn't really smiling. He was a bit loud, like someone issuing authority orders, and asked me to come to his car. I greeted him with a nice smile (I assume it was nice...), but I added: "Sir, I am in my underwear in my sleeping bag, can't you check my passport here?" He refused. "Get dressed and come down to the road"...

So I did. I took it easy, but really, it’s not that simple when you're in a tent in the cold, dressing again in clothes that are still wet from your day, and getting out. I mean, it’s a pain. You lose the calories you had been conserving in the sleeping bag. You have to gear up again... It's not like just jumping into clothes in a dressing room and going. But I took it easy and went down to the road to meet the guy, managing to keep an authentic smile. I put myself in his shoes, thinking, “What the heck is that?!”

He, with an authoritarian and borderline angry tone, demanded, "Your passport!"

After examining it, he asked, "You are French?"

"Yes, sir," I replied.

"What are you doing here?"

"I am resting, I was tired... I needed to sleep... But when you arrived, I was also listening to an audiobook, so I didn't hear you..."

He cut me off, "Why?"

"Why am I tired?" I asked.

"No, why are you here?" he clarified, slightly annoyed.

"Because it's a reasonably flat and discreet enough place to put the tent. I don't disturb anyone here, it's public, so it's good enough for me... I couldn't find a better spot on the way."

He then cooled down a bit, "It is dangerous, sir..."

"Dangerous? No, it's a safe place... Why? What danger?"

"Sir, it is minus 15°C..."

I smiled, "Exactly! It's not even really cold yet..."

There was a moment of silence.

He looked at me, half amused and half in disbelief. "There are bears and wolves in this area..."

It's possible, but I doubt it... Not many, I'm sure. The bear and wolf threat is often exaggerated to scare tourists. The place isn't really interesting for a bear, even less for a pack of wolves. And even so, I've had my share of bear visits in Romania, Slovakia, and Hungary... I'm not really worried anymore.

"I'll do my best not to disturb them, sir..." But then I smiled and added, "I have bear spray with me."

He handed me back my passport.

"How did you find me? Did you see me from the road? Because I'm pretty well hidden, especially now that the tent is covered by snow..."

"Someone phoned the police station..."

"Really? Phoned for what? I'm doing nothing wrong, I'm respectful of the place and collect all my rubbish... Why would someone complain?"

"Are you used to this?"

I smiled, pretending not to acknowledge that it's kind of uncommon, perhaps even unconventional: "To sleep?"

"To sleep in these conditions?"

"You mean comfortably set up in my tent? Yes, I am. A lot..."

Finally acknowledging that I was not totally naive, incompetent, or unaware, he said, "Ok... You can stay... Have a good night, sir."

"Thank you, sir. And please tell your team that if another one calls to say there is a guy here, just answer: 'Yeah, there is a guy there...'" I continued, mimicking a jaded voice, and pretended to make a distressed call in a panicked voice: "'Yes, but he is in a tent!'" Then, miming the phone to my ear, I responded in the same nonchalant jaded voice: "'Yeah, he's in a tent!' 'But sir, it is highly unusual!' 'Yes, it is. Anything else?'"

At that moment, he laughed... I made him laugh, and it made me very happy... A small success, sometimes delicious!

I walked back to my tent, he returned to his patrol... I am still smiling at that exchange today while writing it...

I slept very well. The morning was sunny, blindingly so, because of the pure white snow everywhere. But it was really comfortable... I packed everything and continued on my way. That day, I planned to cross through a ski resort, perhaps have lunch there in a restaurant to charge my batteries while enjoying a hot meal, and then proceed on the upper mountain road to cross the mountain range. That was the plan... and it went well up until the lunch, the battery recharge, and the hot meal. After that, things got tricky because I learned there was about 2 meters of snow up there. Walking was out of the question, of course, but maybe the roads were okay.

So I went looking for information about the snow conditions on the roads in the upper part and I especially wanted to be sure that all the roads on my itinerary were cleared of snow. But it was really difficult to find anyone who knew. Most people I asked were guessing, answering generally, or not at all. I went to the police, the ski resort staff... I tried anyone I could think of to extract solid information. But I ended up with nothing reliable enough for me to attempt the mountain pass. And it was getting late. So I decided to abandon my plans and head back downhill, on the main road. Not really pleasant, but I had no other choice. Night was falling at 4 PM, and I had to find a hotel for the night. Also, because I would use a lot of internet data to make new plans and chart a new itinerary... and it was not a cheap place, I can tell you that... But again, I didn't have many options.

So, I spent the night in a glorious hotel just for an eye and half a leg (they gave me a generous discount). Refreshed, I set out in the morning, easily heading downhill, pondering a new itinerary that would make me backtrack just a bit before turning west to take a road that would showcase the 1984 Olympic Games installations.

It was a lovely day. The sun shone brightly throughout. The path gently sloped downwards. It was a comfortable walk... The Olympic Games installations were a bit disappointing as most of them had simply been left to decay. But I guess it costs a lot to maintain those, and there probably aren't enough people using them... Even the large hotels built to host all the athletes and staff are now crumbling into ruins...

I was truly impressed by the ski jumps. On TV, we're used to seeing those long ski jumps plunging downhill into the void. Watching athletes, half jumping and half flying with their skis slightly opened, bodies straight as ash tree planks, arms at their sides trying to gain aerodynamic lift... it's not that impressive on the screen.

But once you see the installation they're flying from, it's a different story. Just the thought of them jumping, the visualization of them flying—it's genuinely awe-inspiring. It gives you chills... Gone is the thought, "Yeah, I could do that." No. A big no. I wouldn't jump that even if I were paid for it, not even to reclaim the eye and half a leg I'd spent previously.

In the afternoon, around 3 PM, I stumbled upon a small restaurant on the side of the road. There was just a trench in the snow to access it; the snow in the place was about 1.8 meters deep. I paid for a hot meal, charged my batteries for the little I had used during the day, and continued on my way after leaving a few coins and a note from my wallet. The kind owner insisted on giving me two beers to take with me, which he slipped into the pocket of my backpack.

It was getting late, so I walked quickly, my eyes carefully scanning both sides of the road for a potential place to pitch my tent. Roads on mountain slopes aren’t the best for finding camp sites, but I eventually managed one after 3.5 km.

While walking down the main road, I noticed a small road branching off uphill. I followed it and about 100 meters later, in the area between the two roads, I found a flat, tree-free spot big enough to accommodate my tent. It was eight meters from this small road, and the main road was about 40 meters down, mainly below me—so the spot was really quiet and out of view. That was ideal because I wanted to stay two nights there. The second night would be New Year's Eve, and I didn’t want to be in the town 12km down during that time. I don’t enjoy the noisy, hysterical behaviors of New Year's celebrations, especially the crackers and domestic fireworks. The idea of staying comfortably and quietly in my tent was a far better prospect.

I ventured off the small road toward the campsite I had chosen. There was a 1.4-meter-high embankment completely filled with snow, followed by a relatively flat area with about a meter of snow up to the campsite. As I descended the embankment, I slipped and ended up half-sitting in the snow and leaning against the embankment. The snow was fresh and very powdery, and it quickly filled the space where I had just been. The 35 meters to the site were tough to navigate, but I made it. I set my bag down in the snow and sat on it to rest a bit after the strenuous crossing. After choosing the exact spot, I began the laborious task of digging out the snow to clear the ground, moving about 4 cubic meters in total.

This exercise kept me warm as the sun had set for the day, and the snow on my clothes constantly melted, leaving me quite wet. But I successfully cleared the area, ensuring there was enough space to maintain access all around the tent. You know the drill...

Thus, I secured a beautiful and comfortable place to stay for two nights. As expected, I could hear the distant thunderous noise from the town downhill the next night without being disturbed by it.

On my way down after packing up, I stopped at a charming guest house, hoping they had a restaurant—and therefore, hot coffee. It was about 2.5 km from the campsite. It was another very sunny morning, and a couple was outside drinking tea. I joined them at a nearby table and greeted them. Assuming the man was in charge, I asked him for coffee, but it turned out he wasn't the host. They were on holiday with a group of friends from a club, spending a few days celebrating and walking the passable trails. My coffee arrived, and so did the man, Adnan, who sat at the next table. We started chatting about my adventures. After a few questions, he offered me breakfast, and I eagerly accepted. We talked more as I ate.

Then, we moved inside the guest house to use the WiFi and to show him my route on the map. It was so bright outside that it was difficult to see anything on the screen.

Inside, the guest house was cozy and decorated like a museum with old alpinist gear. That's when I realized they were a club of alpinists. And guess what their last destination was? Nepal! Specifically, the Solukhumbu Valley—an area I'm very familiar with, where I've spent a lot of time, made many friends, and created fond memories. So, we had plenty to discuss. Sometimes, the world feels incredibly small...

I spent about an hour and a half at the guest house, chatting and meeting people who were equally interested in my adventure and my spiritual journey.

When it came time to leave, I approached the owner to settle my bill for the coffee and food. As my hand reached for my wallet in my back pocket, Adnan intervened, insisting on covering the cost. His generosity was deeply appreciated, yet little did I know, this gesture would soon be a source of regret. I accepted his kindness graciously.

With my backpack, hat, and gloves, I bid farewell to everyone and resumed my journey. I still had about 8 km to descend into town, and another 2 km to reach a spot I had identified on the map as suitable for setting up camp for the night. My plan was to grab a hot lunch and a few groceries in town before heading straight to the campsite. But things were about to deviate from my plan.

The descent was relatively easy. Going downhill is always easier when the slope is gentle. However, steep descents can be more challenging and hazardous than ascending. Fortunately, the elevation change here was mild, and I reached the town around 1:30 PM. With nightfall not until 4 PM, I felt I had ample time. At the restaurant, the limited vegetarian options led me to choose a vegetable pizza. I sat at a table next to a power outlet, stood up to ask if I could use it to charge my battery, and also inquired if they accepted Visa or only cash. "Cash only," he replied, as was typical in many shops in the country.

Having recently left the capital with enough cash for my mountain crossing—where I expected no ATMs—I reflexively reached for my wallet to confirm I had enough. But my hand met nothing. My wallet wasn't there.

Time seemed to freeze in that instant. Silence enveloped both inside and outside. I was paralyzed by a sudden stillness.

Then, my mind raced wildly, bombarded by a flurry of thoughts—regrets, assumptions, plans, realizations—all at once. Tumultuous.

A bit bordélique, as we would say in France:

Empty back pocket! No wallet. I had no memory of moving the wallet from that pocket to another. How was this possible? Irrelevant! I checked the pocket thoroughly, only to discover a hole at the bottom...

What exactly did I lose? What was inside and how much? No, not now. First, cancel the pizza.

It must have fallen out along my path, likely right after I tore the pocket. But I had no memory of tearing it, nor of any incident that could have caused such damage without my noticing.

I needed to retrace my steps... First, find a place to leave my backpack so I could quickly backtrack...

Cancel the pizza because, as of now, I had no money and no way of getting any for several days...

The embankment, the embankment next to the campsite where I sank into it both ways... My wallet might be there.

Would I have realized if it fell out when I undressed in the tent? Not sure...

When had I last used my wallet? This morning at the guest house, no—they offered me breakfast... But did I reach for my wallet? No, I hadn’t completed the motion.

Cancel the pizza now, Emmanuel! It would only complicate things to order food without the means to pay.

I couldn’t believe it. I had really lost my wallet.

The pocket, the treacherous pocket of my brand-new pants! Traitorous pants!

I needed to go to the police. If I explained everything, they should be able to help me.

But first, stop the pizza order!

Actions, Emmanuel, actions!

I rushed to the counter, called the waiter over, and asked him to cancel the pizza order because I had just realized I lost my wallet. I asked if I could leave my backpack here while I went to the police station. "No problem," he told me, giving me directions to the station just 300 meters away, on the right. And off I went.

I walked quickly, doing two things simultaneously: first, trying to recall all the places I had stopped and sat—a rock, a safety barrier, on my backpack—any spot where I might have cracked my pocket without realizing it. Second, I strategized about the steps I needed to take—blocking my Visa card, phoning France to arrange a money transfer, and reporting the loss of my ID and driver’s license.

I arrived at the police station, which was exactly as you might expect on New Year's Day—minimally staffed with four policemen. They couldn’t help me find a place to sleep that night, nor could I leave my bag there while I searched for my wallet, and they wouldn’t dispatch a patrol to take me uphill to look for it. However, they mentioned that a patrol had been informed there was a missing wallet somewhere in the mountains. That made me laugh—it was so absurd it was almost funny, though I’m pretty sure the officer was serious.

Of course, these answers didn’t come easily. I had to explain everything from the beginning to an officer who seemed to listen attentively until he abruptly summarized, “So you are French!” and then asked for my passport.

Eventually, they found someone who spoke better English, and we managed to communicate more effectively. The best they could do was give me access to the WiFi at the station. That was helpful; I called Adnan, the man I had met at the guest house that morning, and explained my situation. He was busy but sent a friend to pick me up at the station. After retrieving my backpack from the restaurant, we headed back to the guest house.

Leaving my backpack at the guest house, I took my headlamp and torch, and set out on foot to retrace my steps from the morning and the previous day's evening walk from the restaurant—where I had last used my wallet. I ended up covering about 10 km, including the campsite, but found nothing resembling my wallet.

When I returned to the restaurant, slightly out of breath, the owner invited me to rest by the wood stove. It was already night, and the temperature outside was dropping. He then offered me a glass of hot tea and asked if I’d like him to prepare dinner for me, the same as last time. Accepting his kind offer seemed like a good idea—I could definitely use some hot food. So, I enjoyed a warm meal before heading downhill.

At the campsite, 3.5 km down, I quickly realized that I couldn't do much without a shovel. The day had been sunny, and the snow had melted slightly, only to harden as evening approached. This was nothing like the fluffy, powdery snow of the morning. So, I returned to the guest house to retrieve my backpack, with a plan to find a place to sleep and start again in the morning.

Upon my arrival at the guest house, Adnan and his group were in full celebration mode for the New Year—music, dancing, eating... exactly what you'd expect from a group of friends enjoying their time together. The room was filled with the sounds of folklore music and the sight of folklore dancing, a lively scene that warmed the heart. The energetic group dance went on and on, with everyone sweating and smiling.

Adnan found me a room next to the entrance where I could wait and have a drink. When he returned, he offered me to stay the night. It was too dark and cold to venture out again, and I could sleep on the sofa in the room. The guest house was full, but this was the best he could offer. I accepted right away, quickly concluding that it was much better to stay here than to search for a spot to pitch my tent in the middle of the night.

I occasionally joined the party, but mostly I wasn't in the mood and didn't want to intrude. So, I mostly kept to myself, even though many guests, breathless and sweaty from dancing, would sit with me for a while to chat about mountains, pilgrimage, and various topics. The room I was in served as a common multi-purpose area, strewn with everyone's coats and bags, and fridges full of drinks. The kitchen was just opposite. Whenever the owner-cook found a moment for himself, he would lie down on the sofa opposite mine and close his eyes. He didn't speak English, so our exchanges were silent but comforting.

It was a pleasant evening. At one point, Adnan approached me and revealed that they had organized a collection of money for me without my knowledge. He handed me the collected funds to help me in the coming days. I was taken aback and overwhelmed by such a kind gesture. It was not only unexpected but profoundly helpful.

Now that I was a little rested and no longer in immediate distress, I began making plans. I would walk down to the town early the next morning, retracing my steps carefully all the way. If I didn’t find the wallet, I planned to rent a hotel room, leave my stuff there, and then try to find a shovel. The next day, I would return to the campsite to move all the snow necessary. I wasn’t ready to consider the wallet lost until I had done that. If it was still missing after that, I would accept defeat. Meanwhile, my French support team (Franz) informed me that nowadays, you can temporarily block a bank card without canceling it. You can make a temporary opposition for a few days and then call to have the card reactivated—great news! I was safe to continue my search.

I spent a good night sleeping on the sofa. In the morning, I was probably the first awake. I repacked my backpack—I had opened it the previous evening to take a hot shower. Just when I finished, the guest house owner came to me with a platter of coffee and breakfast. That was very welcome. I warmly thanked him and set off.

Unfortunately, my walk back was fruitless. No sign of the lost wallet. But I harbored a big hope of finding it at the campsite. I had half slipped, half fallen on a small slope there, and it seemed possible, even logical, that this was where I might have torn open my back pocket. Since it was in at least one meter of fresh, powdery snow, the wallet could easily have fallen there without my noticing and might be buried under the snow.

Undeterred and as planned, once I reached town, I headed straight for a hotel, the cheapest possible. After searching unsuccessfully for a shovel elsewhere, I returned to the hotel, hoping they might have one. Fortunately, they did have a shovel and allowed me to take it up to the mountains the next day. Filled with renewed hope, I prepared for another attempt to retrieve my lost wallet.

It was too late to venture out, so I decided to take advantage of the comfortable room to perform some maintenance on my tent. I also harbored a faint, tiny hope that perhaps the wallet had fallen out in the tent while I was dressing or undressing and that I might have overlooked it when packing. It was highly improbable, but hope tends to cling to such possibilities. After unpacking and thoroughly searching the tent—and not finding the wallet—I spent almost four hours stitching and reinforcing parts that needed attention.

I went to bed satisfied and woke up early, fresh and ready to face the day. It was a cloudy morning. After breakfast, I picked up the shovel and started my trek back to the campsite, 12 km uphill. I tried hitchhiking, but it wasn’t easy for cars to stop for a big guy carrying a shovel. So, I walked while trying to hitch a ride, turning around whenever I heard a car. About 3 km into my journey, it started to snow. Normally, I would have my backpack with me, which not only keeps my back warm but also carries an umbrella protected in a PVC pipe. Regrettably, I hadn’t thought to bring the umbrella, so I was at the mercy of the elements.

I had walked nearly all the way up, close to the guest house, when a car finally stopped. A fellow Muslim brother. He drove me the remaining 2 km to the campsite and, as he was early for a prayer meeting in a small mosque in the forest, he offered to help me dig.

For an hour, we dug together, taking turns with the shovel. His kindness was invaluable. After he left, I continued alone for three more hours, shoveling snow and breaking it down with small jabs of the shovel to ensure the wallet wasn’t hidden in a big chunk. The snow was formidable; 20 cm had fallen since the start of my search. Now, my woolen hat (I had brought only my woolen hat for the cold, not my walking hat), my jacket, trousers, shoes, and socks were getting really wet. In such conditions, things deteriorate quickly. Snow sticks to the wet fabric, melts, and wets it further, causing more snow to stick and soak through until you're completely wet and chilled.

Despite the worsening weather, the intense physical effort kept me from feeling cold. I felt the chill of the wet clothes, but I wasn't cold—yet.

I had cleared all the area where I thought my wallet could be. I don't know exactly how many cubic meters of snow I shoveled, but I wouldn’t be surprised if it was around eight. Finding nothing, I had to finally accept that I had truly lost my wallet. Without further delay, I grabbed the shovel and headed downhill.

The snow on the road was thick, with visibility limited to about 10 meters. No cars were passing, so I took out my bike flashing lights to signal my presence should any vehicle approach. The first to appear wasn’t a car but a snowplow, followed by a long line of cars taking advantage of the freshly cleared path. They were all heading uphill, while I was going down. They were really nice guys. After the visit, they asked me many questions to which I answered with pleasure. Their interest and questioning were genuine, sincere, and personal. Before leaving, they advised me to ask the watchman of the nearby silo for permission to pitch my tent on the property. They explained that while I could continue to the spot I had planned, a bit further along the dirt road leading to the border, I might be woken up multiple times during the night by patrols. Sleeping under the watchman’s surveillance would likely ensure a full night of rest. When I asked if they could send a radio message to notify all patrols of my presence, they explained that due to shift changes, the safest option was to ask the watchman. They were confident he would agree.

So, I went to see the watchman at the gate and asked if I could stay on the cooperative grounds. "No worries, put yourself wherever you want on this side," he said, gesturing to an area near the path I’d take in the morning.

I pitched my tent there, a simple setup without the rainfly due to the heat but still enclosed to protect against mosquitoes. I took a "bottle shower" after sunset to rinse off sweat and dust. Over time, I’ve learned to manage a full shower with just 1.5 liters of water, adding another liter if I wash my hair. Carrying water on my back has made me very economical with it. Despite the floodlights illuminating the area around the massive silo and the adjacent park of agricultural vehicles, I had a radiant night.

In the morning, the watchman kindly offered me fresh mineral water from an office fountain. After a handshake and a wish for good luck, I resumed my journey.

Customs control stopped me barely 100 meters later. I was 5 km in a straight line from the border post I intended to cross and 8.5 km by the main road. Given the past two days, I thought this would be a quick check—just a confirmation that I was the "French guy" they’d already heard about. Perhaps a brief exchange, a reminder not to cross illegally, and a "have a nice day."

But no, the same procedure unfolded: a full hour-long check, calls to superiors, and eventually, a personal escort. I couldn’t help but wonder how this compared to approaching the Iron Curtain during its time.

This time, the escort vehicle followed me at a distance of 200–300 meters. The front passenger used binoculars, which made me smile internally, as I had planned to cross a small stretch of wasteland the car couldn’t navigate. They would need to find another path, losing sight of me for a few minutes.

Unfortunately, nature called, and the sparse, flat terrain offered little privacy. I had hoped the tall grass of the wasteland would suffice, sparing the binoculars operator any traumatic sights. However, they anticipated my route and were already waiting on the other side. I’ll spare the details of my necessary stop, but let’s say my training in India has diminished any lingering embarrassment.

The second patrol escorted me to within 1 km of the border post, where we parted ways after several friendly exchanges. One officer seemed genuinely interested in the spiritual aspect of my pilgrimage, while both maintained a professional yet relaxed attitude. They seemed to share my sense of the absurdity of the situation.

During my final break before reaching the post, a surveillance drone appeared overhead. Hovering about 50 meters up, it fixed its camera on me, scanning the surroundings. Leaning against my bag, I raised a hand in greeting to help it locate me. After a few minutes, the drone moved, scanned the area again, then left.

Shortly afterward, another patrol arrived. The officer, one of the morning’s chiefs, asked if I was okay. I said I was resting from the sun and the heavy load. "Was that your drone?" I asked. "Of course," she replied, then asked, "You’ll pass the border at the post, right?" I didn’t answer verbally, but my expression must have spoken volumes. She wished me a good trip and left.

Finally, I reached the border and crossed without issue. A Romanian officer even came out of the post to offer me a bottle of cold water and wish me safe travels.

Whenever locals ask if I’m well-received in their region, I always say I’ve encountered kindness, generosity, and warm welcomes everywhere. Crossing into Serbia, I felt confident this would continue.

I wasn’t disappointed. In the first village, I approached a man cycling to ask about getting a local SIM card. He accompanied me to exchange currency and then helped me buy a SIM card, ensuring it suited my needs.

At a café where I stopped to activate the SIM and recharge, the owner offered me a coffee on the house, saying it was her way of welcoming me as a first-time visitor.

Later, at a restaurant, the staff refused payment for my meal, saying it was their treat. The owner even helped me refill my water bottles at a hand pump outside, making the task easier by pumping while I filled.

All of this happened within three hours of entering Serbia. It was a warm welcome to new territory.

As I write these words, I’m by a river where I bathed with delight every two hours during the day. My tent is pitched 3 meters from the water, shaded and hidden by large trees. I’m seated comfortably on a chair left by regulars, likely fishermen or swimmers. My back rests against one chair, my feet on another—a classic "working position."

A fisherman arrives and sets up nearby. We greet each other, exchange pleasantries, then fall into a companionable silence. I continue writing while he fishes. After a couple of hours, less successful for him than for me, a couple visits him with cold drinks. They offer me an iced coffee, insisting despite my polite refusal. We chat briefly as I give up my chair to the lady, gallantry obliges.

When they leave, they hand me a bottle of ice water.

The fisherman lingers a bit longer, hoping for a catch, then eventually packs up. Shaking my hand, he asks if I’ll be there tomorrow. "No, I’ll be back on the road in the morning," I reply. A fish splashes loudly in the water, as if mocking him.

"So goodbye," he says, "and good trip."

"Goodbye, thank you."

He leaves, and I retreat to my tent. Darkness has almost fully fallen. Another great day.

Hello everyone,

Three months without news—I’m overdoing it a bit.

Here’s the update:

Since last time, many good things have happened, and no bad things. That’s a decent balance.

Still, I feel like complaining. Firstly, because it’s a basic human need, and secondly, because I’m French. As the cliché goes, the French love to complain, followed by a good protest.

My chosen subject of complaint: the temperature. For the past two and a half months, it’s been consistently 35⁰C, with spikes to 38⁰C and even 43⁰C on three occasions.

It’s unbearable. Walking in this sun and heat is no longer just challenging; it’s becoming dangerous. I’ve had to adjust my walking schedule to avoid the midday extremes.

I now wake up 30 minutes to an hour before sunrise to pack my things. When daylight breaks, I fold the tent and start walking early, continuing until around 11 a.m. or noon—longer if I haven’t found a suitable resting spot. Romania is an hour ahead of Central Europe, so the timing feels slightly off.

I stop in the shade of forests, caves, or villages—often meeting new people in cafés or restaurants. These breaks allow me to recharge my batteries—both electrical and social. Sometimes, I’m invited to stay, which I often accept, drawn by the promise of a shower and good company.

I set out again around 4:30 or 5 p.m., walking until I find a welcoming place to stop or until the sun sets, usually around 8 p.m.

I don’t always pitch the tent. In this summer weather, sleeping under the stars is comfortable, with the Milky Way serving as my sleeping bag. It also saves time tidying up in the morning.

Sleeping outdoors has taught me to tolerate the local fauna. Insects often crawl over me as I sleep, but I’ve learned not to react. An earwig running across my skin or a beetle tangled in my hair no longer bothers me as much. I used to think they got caught intentionally, but after observing, I realized their shell design is to blame. If I don’t carefully untangle them, they risk losing legs or antennae—or worse, pulling out my hair.

Some beetles pinch, likely to express their discomfort, creating mine. When that happens, I wake up, fully aware of the incident—unlike many other encounters that don’t disturb my sleep. It’s like how we notice a sore knee only when we bump it, even though we probably hit it just as often when it doesn’t hurt. A beetle in your hair is a similar, unforgettable experience.

Larger creatures, from shrews and hedgehogs to grass snakes, foxes, and weasels, tend to keep their distance. Without the tent obscuring their view or smell, they quickly recognize me as human—the ultimate nightmare—and flee without hesitation.

If they are sane, everyone flees. So when that doesn’t happen, it’s a notable and often touching event.

For example, one morning during this pilgrimage, I was lying on my mattress (it’s still necessary to isolate yourself from the dampness of the ground to sleep better), my head resting on my hand, against my bag, and leaning against a tree at the edge of a wood. Like a hunting dog curled up and asleep, I faced a small grassy clearing. I was awakened by a presence.

It didn’t feel alarming. I stayed calm, listening. I believe the brain, even while asleep, can hear and analyze sounds in the immediate environment, alerting us if necessary. I’ve experienced this four times now during nocturnal visits from bears. But this time, there was no urgency, no danger.

I opened my eyes. About fifteen meters away, an enormous doe was grazing nonchalantly. I say enormous because it was not a deer but an adult doe—and an adult doe is large. She grazed noisily on the grass. Well, noisy for someone like me, now awake and trying to be still. In the calm morning silence of nature, after a good night’s sleep, I lay motionless, amazed by the spectacle. Usually, such encounters are fleeting, as frightened animals quickly flee.

I savored the moment, staying as still as possible while observing her. Then, about ten meters away, I noticed a small fawn, likely old enough to have lost its spots. I wanted to lift my head slightly to see it better but didn’t dare move.

Eventually, I had to adjust—or perhaps my breathing changed—because the doe suddenly raised her angular head. Her whole body tensed, motionless, on alert.

That’s when we locked eyes. We stared at each other, directly into each other’s gaze—a moment suspended in time. It felt so real that it was almost unreal. Probably just a fraction of a second, but it felt eternal. Both of us were frozen in attentive silence.

And then, as nothing happened, she seemed to decide that I wasn’t a threat and resumed grazing.

That’s what moved me the most. The moment of eye contact was intense, but her choice to start grazing again, despite knowing the worst of predators—the most destructive of creatures—was nearby, truly touched me. It felt as though she had decided to trust me.

I felt accepted, as if I had become part of her world. You can’t imagine how proud this made me. It’s a moment etched in my memory, one that strengthens me when I recall it. Hindus have a term for such experiences: “Darshan.”

We stayed like that for a few minutes. She tore at the tender grass, taking a step between each bite, while I remained still, listening to my heart pound as if I were receiving a kiss for the first time.

These are the kinds of moments that can happen when you sleep under the stars.

I’m nearing the Serbian-Romanian border. My journey continues toward the sanctuary of Medjugorje in Bosnia-Herzegovina, which means crossing Serbia.

I hope the summer won’t be as hot as the spring, though I’m not optimistic.

I hope everything is going well for you and wish you health and happiness.

See you soon.

Kindness along the Way

That morning, I woke up as we all like, after having had a great night's sleep. By that, I mean that the night had passed without me noticing anything. It's interesting, I find, that what defines a good night is that we have seen nothing of it, that we were totally absent from it. So unconscious that we have no idea what happened. And the less we know, the better the night was! I find this very similar to the paradox of the spiritual, which defines that we can access it to the extent that we are not there to do or wish it.

In short, I slept really well. And I knew it because when I woke up, I felt great. Everything seemed to be heading towards a good day. I stretched, watching the sun rise. It was good. I don't know about you, but here we went from the intense cold of winter to the heat of summer without any transition. Within three days, the snow had melted, and it was 25 degrees Celsius. I folded my tent and repacked my backpack like every morning I walk, while enjoying the song of the birds already back. And I set off.

In the first village that I passed, a small, very small church was there. I was heading there to enter, but as often, it was closed. As I sat on the few steps of its entrance, through the window of the house next door, someone was waving at me. A sign that I translated by deduction from the situation as: don't move, we'll bring you the keys. And in fact, a gentleman was already approaching me. After the usual introductions, Petrus introduced me to this church, almost a chapel in size, then left me the keys and told me to come and have breakfast when I had finished and closed the door.

An hour later, I was welcomed by the whole family present: the grandfather, the grandmother, the son who is also the village priest, officiating in the other much larger church which he immediately offered me to also visit after eating. There were also the two small children. My host called a friend who told me he was speaking French. Five minutes later, we were all around a bottle of Palinka, a glass in hand, chatting in French/English/Romanian, while the meal was cooked. Marius, who came down from his pastures especially for this, actually has very good French, a little rusty from lack of practice but good enough to have a good discussion. I then visited the farm, stopped by to see cows, goats, dogs, cats... and as the morning began to end, Petrus and his father had to be gone for half an hour, so I stayed waiting, taking advantage of the opportunity to recharge the batteries and use the Wi-Fi.

I was planning my route a bit to go to the next big city where I planned to receive a package from France. As my bank card was soon to expire, I had to receive a new one, and I took advantage of this necessity to receive other materials. It's a bit complicated to organize all this when you're traveling. But I had already crossed the town in question a few months ago before heading into the north-northeast of Romania, and so I contacted a guesthouse that I knew there to ask them to receive the package for me so that I only had to pass by to collect it... Everything is in the timing...

The idea being to spend as little time as possible in the city because the city is expensive for the pilgrim's budget, and also because after so much time alone in the mountains or forests, the city, with its agitation, its smells, its noises, becomes for me a place of passage more than of stay... But having to pass there, I intended to optimize the opportunity by taking advantage of the shops to buy clothing items which deteriorate so quickly, and to find a dry cleaner where I could have my down sleeping bag washed. Natural goose down requires special dry washing and many hours of drying to achieve satisfactory results. Impossible to do it in the washing machine at home...

And then Petrus came back, and we went to the main church. He had to stop at the village grocery store to get the keys, which was a good thing because I needed to restock the food box in my bag. While he went about his business with the shopkeeper, I did my shopping. When it was time to pay, the shopkeeper refused my money and told me that she was offering me the whole thing: a piece of bread, a few tomatoes, bananas, a tin box of flageolets, one of vegetable pâté, a piece of cheese, and a bar of chocolate.

The church opposite is tall and narrow. We went up to its bell tower to see the bells, of course, but also the view. Then Petrus left me the keys and told me to join him at home when I was done. I usually take my time in churches, as in places that make you want to sit quietly and close your eyes (without sleeping).

I finally went back to get my bag, say goodbye, and thank everyone who had so kindly welcomed me. I was, of course, invited to stay the night or longer if I wanted. Marius, who had also returned, invited me to come and see his flock. But it was still early in the afternoon, and I felt good about continuing on my way. So I took the stick (Spine) and the bag (Expiation) and got back on my way.

The small village was spread out in length. Towards its end, particular smells came to my nostrils, and I suspected their origins. Someone was distilling nearby, I was sure. I looked around as I walked for clues—smoke, for example, or a stream of fresh water. But I didn’t have to look for long because, just on the side of my path, I passed in front of a small hut where some people were busy while others chatted around a table with many glasses, looking at the hearth full of flames under an alembic.

As I passed, Valentin, the boiler, was going out to get wood from the customer’s trailer, which was filled with two large barrels and all kinds of pieces of wood: old boards, pieces of furniture, and a few logs. I was very happy to be invited inside, because the atmosphere was good, but also because I was really curious about the process.

The small room was divided into two areas. On one side, the alembic was embedded in masonry with the hearth below. A notched wheel, a steering wheel, and a connecting rod turned the scraper paddles inside the copper tank to prevent the heated contents from burning. Nearby was a large water tank where the arm of the alembic was immersed to cool the alcoholic vapors.

And then there was the friendly corner, just at the entrance: a table, a sofa, some chairs, lots of drinks, dry sausage, not-so-dry sausages, bacon, cold cuts, bread, a few bottles, and a wire hanging from the ceiling with a light bulb at the end above it all.

I stood there asking all the questions I had. I had a lot of them, and Valentin patiently answered everything whenever his occupation as a distiller allowed. He also managed to balance this with chatting with the many people who stopped by. It was a real hub of village life. When the door was open and the street glowed red from the fire, people clearly knew it was a good time to stop in for a drink, a bite to eat, and a chat.

The cars stopped and started again. There were bursts of laughter and loud voices. Valentin, though the host, never lost his concentration. Even while participating in the discussions, welcoming everyone, and offering glasses of little plum, little cherry, little mirabelle plum, or little house cognac—you see the scene—he never stopped monitoring his still. If everything looked like a good time, he was still very much at work. Adding a piece of wood from time to time, he told me the temperature must be gentle and constant. The slowness allows the aromas to develop, it seems. Judging by what I tasted, for sure, the guy was bringing out the aromas.

I stayed to watch the first distillation come out. In this case, it was from 250 liters of grapes from the customer’s garden.

When finished, in addition to seeing the process of emptying the tank and filling the distillate for the second distillation (to rise to 54 degrees of alcohol), I wanted to see the alembic emptied, to see the copper, and to observe the paddles that scrape its bottom while rotating. In short, I was curious. It was then that Valentin's wife, who lives across the street, offered me a shower. A hot shower! The pilgrim never refuses a shower, because a hot shower is not only refreshing but also unpredictable—you never know when the next one will be. And then, coming out all clean, she offered to wash my clothes as well, using the neighbor's washing machine. Valentin suggested that I stay the night so I could see and taste the finished product while allowing my laundry to dry.

So, I spent the evening at their place—a beautiful Tzigane family, full of colors. Tzigane, yes, a word I was warned against because it is considered discriminatory. Tzigane means gypsy in the Gypsy language, but as I've learned multiple times, it’s a term used with pride within their own community. "We are Gypsies; we call ourselves Gypsies. The term Rroms, with two Rs, is a government policy term that we never use among ourselves," Valentin explained to me. I must admit, the relational dynamics between "gypsies" and sedentary people, between Roms and Rroms, escape me. The more I discover, the less I understand.

On several occasions, I heard the term Gadjo Dilo used for me, pronounced with kindness and laughter. I understood the reference to the French film about a young Frenchman (Romain Duris, I think) traveling through Romania in search of Gypsy musicians. I vaguely remember the film, but one scene where the character cleans his host’s house while they are away stayed in my memory and made me laugh, as it did Valentin when I recounted it.

Finally, the last drop of the distillation finished falling. We filled cans with the finished product. The customer was happy and left, even though he could have slept in his car for a few hours before driving—but, well.

I spent a wonderful evening at Valentin's table. His house was as welcoming as his distillery, with many people coming and going. Neighbors, family—up to 18 of us gathered at one point before I finally retired to bed. In the morning, the silence contrasted sharply with the previous night. I hurried to drink the coffee that had just been offered because I wanted to go to church. It was Sunday morning, and Petrus was officiating. I had sent him a message the night before, explaining that I hadn’t managed to leave the village and, despite telling him I planned to continue, I had ended up staying at someone else’s house just 400 meters away. I never know how things will turn out, but so far, they always go well.

I stayed in the church for four hours, meditating gently while the service was conducted. Long ago, I abandoned the idea of trying to follow what was happening—the language isn’t mine, and the liturgical traditions are different from those in which I grew up. Instead, I used the fervent atmosphere to deepen my meditation.

At the church exit, after greeting and chatting with Petrus, someone offered me a large loaf of bread—special Lenten bread for the pre-Easter fasting period. Then a gentleman approached, saying he had noticed me during the morning, absorbed in stillness. He invited me to his house for lunch, and it was hard to refuse. I agreed, but only after attending a blessing ceremony for a newly furnished room in a house opposite the church, where I had been invited.

I had attended similar ceremonies in other traditions but never among Orthodox Christians, so I was curious. The ceremony was brief, and I stayed longer because the daughter of the homeowner spoke French. She was happy to practice a little and asked about my journey. We chatted around the buffet set up for the guests. She was a college professor who also worked in an international call center, hence her proficiency in the language.

Afterward, I went to the shepherd’s house for lunch. It was a simple, rudimentary home. He invited me to stay as long as I wanted—now or when I returned. He was a kind, rustic man who prepared me some farm eggs. We talked about religion, and my pilgrimage resonated deeply with him. He lived with extreme simplicity, and his perspective touched me. He was insistent that I stay, go see his goats (more than a hundred, many of them kids of the year), but I took my leave, eager to walk a bit.

I returned to Valentin's to collect my backpack and resume my journey. But when I arrived, it was a party—loud music, smoking barbecue—the youngest's birthday. Valentin told me I couldn’t leave yet and urged me to stay a little longer. It was only after many toasts and conversations that I managed to leave. By then, the afternoon was already gone, and I laughed as I walked out, realizing it had taken me a day and a half to cover 800 meters! This somewhat answers the frequent question about how many kilometers I travel each day. And still, I could have stayed two weeks if I had wanted to, given how warmly I was invited.

But I am a pilgrim. It’s the path I travel that defines me. I didn’t cover much ground that day. A few hundred meters before the next village, I found a pleasant spot to pitch my tent and quickly fell asleep. The next morning, not feeling well and with a bad headache, I stayed there all day. I left again the following day.

Coming down from my bivouac site, I came across Paul, who was returning from dropping his children off at school. Paul is one of Valentin's neighbors, and we had spent a lot of time talking since he was one of the few at the time who spoke English. He was busy removing the old plaster from his house, right next to Valentin's. During his breaks, he would join us in front of the still.

Surprised to see me again, I explained to him that I hadn’t walked the day before. After some discussion, we went our separate ways. I crossed the village, then the next one, where I saw Valentin leaning against his car, parked on the side of the road. "You were sick! Come to my house! Rest, recover, and then go again!" Valentin, genuinely concerned, took some time to let me leave. I had to explain that it was just a momentary discomfort and that I felt much better after a full day of rest. I truly appreciated his kindness, which went beyond a simple welcome. I don’t know if I can explain it—it feels like there’s something magical in these encounters.

In another village, as I walked on the sidewalk, I found myself next to a little lady carrying a small bag of groceries. We walked at a similar pace, giving each of us time to discreetly observe the other. She must have been around 65 years old and still seemed vigorous. She wore a veil on her head. Then she spoke to me, seemingly curious about what I was doing there. I think she said something about my bag because she used the word "greu" (pronounced gréo), which means heavy—a word I know well. It’s the impression you get when you see my backpack. I often joke that when people ask how much it weighs, I respond that it weighs "too much, and then some."

Walking slowly, I took out my phone to play a short pre-recorded introductory message that explains the essentials in a few moments. She stopped, seemingly shocked. "How long have you been walking?" she asked, using the translator I handed her. "Three and a half years, soon," I replied. The information struck her forcefully, and I saw a wave of emotion cross her face. "But how far will you go?" she asked. I smiled, not quite knowing how to answer, and pointed to my chest with my index finger. "I’m going there," I said. She looked at me and burst into tears.

Thin. I hadn’t expected that. She put her face in her hands and cried. I hesitated, then gently placed my hand on her shoulder, consoling her with soft strokes. We stood there for a few moments. There was something beautiful in the situation—something magical. Her emotion didn’t seem negative; I even sensed a kind of peace. Finally, she smiled, wished me well, and gave me the customary blessing: "May God accompany you." I continued straight, while she turned right. A few hundred meters later, I turned around to look back at her. At the same moment, she turned as well, and from a distance, we waved. It was one of those surreal encounters I experience relatively often.

In the next village, as I arrived at an intersection, a taxi was parked on the shoulder, blocking my pedestrian path. The driver spoke to me, asking where I was going. After a few moments, he invited me to his house for coffee and a break. I got into his taxi, and he assured me he would drop me back at the exact spot so I could say my journey was entirely on foot.

Hari, as he introduced himself, has been a taxi driver since returning to Romania after spending a few years in England working in security to save money. He preferred to return home after saving enough to start his own business—a life path I encounter often here. We talked for two hours over coffee prepared by his wife. He explained that a woman living a few houses down had told him about a stranger in the village. That’s why he came to meet me. It was the lady I had met earlier. He also invited me to stay the night, but I declined. With a few hours of daylight left, I wanted to keep walking. True to his word, Hari dropped me off at the precise spot where we had met, and I continued on my way.

I crossed the village and walked along the road. It was a major road I needed to follow for a little while longer before turning onto less trafficked side roads. Walking there was somewhat unpleasant, but I made good progress. The flat monotony of the path lent itself to inner contemplation. My body was occupied with its routine, leaving my mind free to wander.

Time passes quickly like this, and so does the distance. It's when the body gets tired that I naturally look around for a suitable place to sit down and take a breather. Sitting on a stone at the edge of a small village, a gentleman, alerted by his dog’s incessant barking, came out from his small farm to meet me. After introductions, he asked if I needed anything and offered me milk from his farm. I couldn’t refuse. Fresh, organic milk. He filled a 2.5-liter bottle with milk, rich in flavor and freshly drawn from the tank in his small milking parlor.

This is how surreal the journey becomes—the welcome I receive from the people I meet. It has been like this for more than three years now, and I can continue like this. Every day I walk, and every day I meet people, have these kinds of encounters. I will continue like this.

The next day, at the entrance to a village, I was stopped by a man surrounded by a crowd of children. He was working on the fence of his small plot of land and, as usual, asked what I was doing there. I gave my standard response: pilgrim/French/not understand. Suddenly, I heard a woman’s voice say in French, "Ah, you're French, but what are you doing here? Have you eaten? Come inside the house, sit down." She gave me a plate of soup with a thick, two- or three-centimeter slice of bread that she baked herself in a wood-fired stove. Then the man cooked some potatoes, and we all ate together. This is how I met the family of Arabella, Christian, and their six children. I spent the night at their place.

Two days later, while resting on a bench at the crossroads of a road and a street in a small village, a car stopped next to me. The driver rolled down his window and asked what I was doing there. He spoke both English and French, while his wife next to him spoke fluent German. Within a minute, Eugène, the driver, and his wife Héléna invited me to their home for lunch and a shower. Eugène was the priest of his village, and Héléna was a religious icon painter who had previously been a professional opera singer.

The connection was instant. Eugène invited me to stay in his vacation home, a disused sheepfold in the hills 4 km from his house. He had set it up as a small retirement retreat. It was perfect for me because I could organize the delivery of my package from France to the nearby city and wait until it arrived, aligning my visit with Eugène’s schedule.

I stayed there for three nights. The small wood stove in the room kept me warm. The space was simple: a bed, a table, a chair, a small shelf, and, at the foot of the bed, a piece of furniture with a television. Eugène came to see me every day, sometimes with Héléna, bringing food, sharing a glass of homemade wine, and chatting.

I left with a superb memory of this couple, so evidently in love. Eugène, with his Santa Claus appearance, radiated kindness and honesty. Héléna, older but vibrant and brilliant, exuded the sensitivity and refinement of a true artist. As I left, I thought I could have stayed much longer. Eugène had even invited me to return and live in the sheepfold if I wanted.

At the edge of the village where we had met, in the church where Eugène was officiating that morning and Héléna was singing (I couldn’t miss that), I stopped at the last house. Sitting on a bench against a fence, I pulled out my topographical map to plan my path. I wanted to avoid the main road and noticed a small river flowing in my direction. A dike beside it looked walkable, though its meanders would lengthen my route. Still, it was a better option than dealing with road traffic.

As I sat there, a woman of about 70 emerged from her house. The dogs had, of course, announced my presence. She asked what I was doing there, so I played my pre-recorded introduction. She began to cry. "Oh no," I thought, "there’s nothing sad here." She stood before me, tears streaming down her small face, her gray hair tucked under a loosely tied scarf.

I didn’t know what to do, but a wave of affection for this little lady moved me to act. I gently hugged her and smiled. Then, speaking softly in French, I said a few words—not because she would understand them, but because in that moment, it wasn’t the meaning of the words that mattered, but their tone and timing.

So, the neighbor across the street comes out to ask what's going on. A very recognizable woman in her attire—the tight scarf on her head and the skirt, but above all, the colors. I spend a moment there explaining, discussing, and consoling before embarking on the dike which, according to the map, takes me to the city.

The arrival at Targu Mureș takes place under pouring rain that hasn’t stopped for two days. I go directly to the guesthouse waiting for me. I plan to spend two days there; the package has already arrived, but the letter containing the bank card is late. That gives me time to go wash my sleeping bag.

For this, I head to a dry cleaner in a large shopping mall. Cleaning a natural goose-down sleeping bag requires industrial dry cleaning. Many small dry cleaners are not equipped for this. After dropping it off, I visit the local tourist office to ask for some information, including a dentist's contact. Since I’m in town for a few days, I figure a quick dental check-up wouldn’t hurt. But, as you know, appointments aren’t easy to get quickly. It’s not the Tourist Office’s job to arrange this, but the staff member is helpful and offers to assist. He makes the calls—it’s easier in Romanian—and after a few minutes, the third dentist he contacts gives me an appointment for the next morning at 10 a.m. Miraculous, right?

I go as planned, and to describe the atmosphere that continues to surround me, the dentist refuses to let me pay. I leave with numerous free samples as well.

The next day, I retrieve my sleeping bag. I take the bus and arrive at the dry cleaner’s counter. The lady hands me my bag in a large transparent plastic cover—you know the kind. I’m immediately about to open it, but she stops me. She doesn’t speak English but seems to suggest I don’t need to open it now. Strange... I tell her I have a compression bag for the sleeping bag so it can fit neatly in my backpack with all the straps tightened. Much more practical for the bus. Yet something in her attitude raises my suspicion, so I decide to examine the sleeping bag right away to ensure everything is in order.

Good thing I do! The bag is as dirty as when I brought it in. What’s more, it now has a bad smell—worse than before! So this, I realize, is the reason for her behavior. She didn’t wash the bag—or perhaps she tried, then gave up after seeing how much work it would take. Whatever the case, my sleeping bag is now in worse condition than when I dropped it off.

She insists the smell is normal for goose feathers. But I know my sleeping bag well, having spent over 2,000 nights in it and had it professionally cleaned several times before. I show her the dark marks that prompted me to bring it in for cleaning in the first place. They are still there. She explains they didn’t come out in the wash, even though she “washed it twice to be sure!” I know I’m being scammed. She took the money (cleaning a sleeping bag is generally expensive) and is pretending everything is fine.

Seeing there’s nothing more I can do or say, I resign myself and leave. I won’t be able to take it to another dry cleaner before my departure since I’ll have to leave the city soon. My budget doesn’t allow me to extend my stay in the guesthouse.

Two days later, I left the city and pitched my tent near the river. I got ready to go to bed and climbed into my sleeping bag. I had dried it as best as possible above a radiator in the room I occupied. The smell was still there but slightly diminished. When I brought my feet inside, I felt something unfamiliar. A seam... There was a seam that my feet were rubbing against. Normally, there was none—or rather, the seams were designed to be discreet, both to sight and touch. But here, a new seam was present, on top or in place of the original one. After examining it, I noticed that a roughly made seam, clearly done with a sewing machine, ran along the length of the sleeping bag, cutting through all the compartments containing the precious goose down.

I now understood that the scam wasn’t just the non-cleaning of the bag but the substitution and replacement of the original materials. You have to understand—a high-quality goose-down sleeping bag like this is very, very expensive. To sleep well, even at temperatures as low as -28°C this past winter, or -43°C in the Himalayan mountains, you need a bag you can trust. It can literally save your life.

I had bought mine used from someone who had returned from an expedition to Everest. These days, many people, not necessarily mountaineers, make that climb as part of commercial expeditions. For example, company executives may do it as part of team-building exercises or self-challenging endeavors. Most of them will never undertake such an experience again, and after descending, they resell or give away their equipment. I was fortunate to acquire a sleeping bag like that and used it extensively for over 17 years. The bag had served me well and lost some of its quality over time. I could no longer rely on it for temperatures below -30°C, but for temperatures as low as -25°C or -28°C, with proper clothing, it worked fine.

That night, in addition to the affront and the bad smell, I was cold. It wasn’t even below zero, but I had to wear warm clothes just to sleep. The feeling of having lost a reliable companion of many adventures—the sense that the bag had been desecrated—was painful at first. After some time, I consoled myself by imagining that some child might sleep under a super goose-down quilt next winter. It was just one less attachment for me to let go of.

I share this story because, until now, I’ve only shared the good moments, the meetings, and the behaviors that could belong to the realm of fairy tales. It wouldn’t be honest to represent my story without also sharing the less positive experiences. This is the second time since my departure that I’ve had a bad experience. (The first, which I kept to myself, involved a grocer in a small village refusing to give me the correct change for a bill, claiming it was half its actual value.)

This little misadventure isn’t pleasant, but honestly, compared to the three years I’ve tried to describe (which I can only partially convey despite my lengthy writing efforts), you’ll agree with me that this anecdote is insignificant, right? Or even compared to just the fortnight I told you about before... Or even just today, as I sit in my tent near the river, walking along its bank toward the Lipova Monastery.

Resuming my writing, which dates back a little now, I reflect on the bread that was offered to me at the church yesterday, along with a bottle of liturgical red wine. Another loaf, also offered yesterday by a French-speaking baker in a village I passed through, came with a tour of his bakery, where I recharged my batteries over coffee and homemade tomato juice. A huge piece of goat cheese and hard-boiled eggs came from a shepherd’s wife I met the day before yesterday in the meadow near her village. Her husband had called her to say I was approaching. She gave me coffee, a hot bean soup, sautéed potatoes, and breaded soy patties while the eggs hardened.

Oh, and the rest of the provisions I bought were thanks to the money a kind gentleman had given me. And yes, as I left the village yesterday, a man on a bike handed me a bag containing a jar of zacusca (so delicious!) and a pot of homemade plum jam.

And you know what? I don't know about you, but here it feels like spring—or even summer. So yes, I will have to find a replacement in preparation for next winter, but for now, I don’t really need more than the little I have left. And I have time ahead of me. So everything is fine.

Voilà for the news.

I wish you all the best.

Pilgrim's Winter Trial

31/1/2024 0 Comments

Attention, long (very) message:

The plan is simple: I draw an almost straight line on the map to cross a mountain range. Alternatively, I could walk along a major road (national type) with unpleasant and dangerous traffic (many trucks). The choice is quickly made, especially since the straight line is significantly shorter.

There are approximately 25 km of small country roads, 45 km of mountain trails (mostly forest paths), followed by paths leading to a pass at about 2,000 meters altitude, then more paths and forest trails until I arrive on the other side—about 85 km in total.

The road option isn’t really an alternative. Sure, walking on a clear road with flat, tarmac ground, easy slopes (if there are any), and frequent villages for food supplies, electric charging points for my phone and power banks sounds convenient. But these advantages don’t outweigh the noise of vehicles passing by at 90 km/h, sometimes brushing too close, or the smell of exhaust fumes—especially from heavily loaded trucks crawling at walking pace uphill. Worst of all is the burnt tire smell, which gives me the awful feeling I’m inhaling micro-particles of the tire materials. Did you know tire pollution is ten times greater than exhaust emissions?

In short, I prefer the mountains. And in this case, the difference in distance is more than double.

So, I head toward the small mountain range from the charming little town of Vatra Dornei. From what I can see on the map, the ascent is gentle, with a steeper climb only at the pass. There also appear to be water sources along my planned route, which is great because it means I won’t need to carry much water.

Instead, I’ll just carry the provisions I bought yesterday from the small grocery store in the last village before the massif. I hope the springs aren’t frozen. It’s starting to get cold, and temperatures remain well below zero during the day, even when it’s sunny. For a spring to avoid freezing, it either needs a significant flow rate or natural or man-made protection. Still, the water that flows from a spring usually maintains a constant temperature since it comes from underground.

In summer, it emerges very cool—between 8 and 12 degrees, depending on the depth of the water table that feeds it. In winter, it feels warm, even though it’s the same temperature. At worst, I’ll melt snow if I don’t have access to liquid water.

I spent the night in a guesthouse at the end of the village of Gura Haitii. My batteries are fully charged, my clothes and shoes are dry, my route is marked on the topographical map on my phone, I have the landmarks identified, the pre-determined stages planned, and my backpack is packed and well-organized. The cold-weather gear is easily accessible, and my tent is dry and neatly folded. Everything is ready.

I finish sipping my morning coffee in the guesthouse dining room. The owner comes to ask me about my plans. Last night, he told me that two bodies were found last spring—tourists caught in an avalanche on the path I’m about to take. It’s his way of warning me about the risks.

But I am no tourist—I am a pilgrim.

There are always risks. The mountain is an unforgiving place. It’s important to be aware and to protect yourself, but there are no guarantees. Nothing is ever 100% safe. For me, that’s what makes the experience so valuable. The mountain doesn’t pretend, and that forces a certain humility. Approaching it with arrogance or overconfidence is dangerous, especially in winter. The mountain dominates—in every sense of the word. (See what I did there?)

So, I studied the map carefully to identify the most dangerous sections. I believe the risks on my planned route are limited to a few specific portions. Another path would be much riskier, but it leads southeast, and I’m heading southwest.

So, I leave.

The first day is really pleasant. I walk along a motorable path used by logging vehicles. In fact, at least one vehicle passed recently, leaving tire tracks in the snow. The tracks are covered with 20 cm of powdery snow, but it’s far better than the 40–50 cm of untouched snow on the ground between and around them. Progress is easier—or at least less difficult. But it doesn’t last.

After a few kilometers, I see that the tracks end. The snow was too deep for the vehicle, whose underbody left a large imprint where it turned around. Now, I’m walking on a path with no tracks, through powder snow that’s a good 50–60 cm thick.

Of course, it’s harder. It’s slower. It’s tedious. I tire quickly. After only about 2 km, I decide to pitch my tent at the first suitable spot.

The advantage of this place is that it’s quiet and deserted. I can move to the side of the path without issue—once I find a flat, open surface large enough for my tent.

After a few dozen meters, under some fir trees on the slope to the right, a spot reveals itself. It feels natural, like the place chose me, not the other way around.

I like it when that happens.

There is a stream below, maybe 5 meters away. I place my bag against a pine log lying nearby. I inspect the spot to determine the best way to arrange my tent. If there’s a slight slope, it’s better to position your head at the highest point for a more comfortable sleep. If there’s a bump, I flatten it as much as possible. Any uncomfortable objects—pine cones, thorny brambles, branches—must be moved away.

Clearing the snow comes first. Under the fir trees, it’s about 45 cm deep—a real task. I need to clear an area large enough not only for the tent but also for space to move around while planting the stakes (sardines in French). It takes time and a lot of energy. I must hurry to avoid getting too cold. The sun is setting, with maybe 40 minutes of light left (sunset is around 4:40 p.m., and it gets dark by 5:20 p.m. Romanian time). That’s enough time, but the temperature is dropping quickly. With my backpack now on the ground, it no longer shields my back from the cold. I need to work fast—both to warm up from the effort and to get “inside” as soon as possible.

Finally, the ground appears. I deploy the tent, starting with the groundsheet. Then the tent itself, inserting the aluminum poles—not telescopic but nestable—with an elastic cord inside to simplify assembly. I slide the poles into the fabric housings designed for them. The tent stands, though still frail. Finally, I secure the flysheet over it.

I anchor everything with aluminum pegs (sardines). Since it will freeze hard overnight, I plant the stakes carefully, shaking them in all directions once they’re in place to leave them free in the holes I’ve created. They must be angled opposite the direction of tension to avoid pulling out in gusts of wind. However, it’s crucial that they remain free; if buried completely, frost will lock them into the ground by morning.

I’ve learned this the hard way. On a few occasions, either due to forgetting or poor weather predictions, I’ve made that mistake. Once, I had to go to a farm at the end of the road to borrow a crowbar. Even with the crowbar, it took over an hour of grueling effort to free the eleven pegs holding my tent. The ground was rock-solid, frozen to a depth of 10 cm. The crowbar, icy cold from minus 20 degrees Celsius temperatures, made my hands ache even with gloves. I had to take frequent breaks to warm my hands in my pockets when the pain became unbearable.

I don’t let that happen anymore.

Finally, the tent is pitched, the straps tightened, and the daily ritual ends as I sink inside, completely exhausted.

Now the inner ritual begins. Everything has its place, precise and dictated by the experience of the last three years. Each object comes out of the backpack one by one, following the optimized order of storage. I lay out and open the sleeping bag, then finish undressing. The dry clothes stored just below the sleeping bag go into the backpack. Even if the clothes I’m wearing aren’t wet, they are damp from sweat, and they will dry quickly in the dry cold since they are still warm. Staying completely dry is crucial because moisture drains body heat. To warm up properly, it’s much better to be entirely dry.

The temperature is around minus 12°C, maybe even minus 15°C. Before doing anything else, I must warm up. It’s only when I’m warm at the bottom of my sleeping bag that I can start preparing something to eat. The sleeping bag, no matter how good, doesn’t generate heat—it only slows down the dissipation of body heat. I am the radiator. So, I radiate as best I can...

I’m utterly exhausted and start to doze off.

Suddenly, I jolt awake. I must have fallen asleep—a significant mistake, as I didn’t finish my setup ritual. It’s completely dark now, after 8 p.m., and the temperature has dropped further, likely to -15°C or even -18°C. The tent ceiling sparkles like a mountain sky on a clear night. The humidity trapped inside has condensed into frost on the icy walls and ceiling. It’s beautiful when I turn on my headlamp.

I finish setting up. Unfortunately, the shoes are already completely frozen—hard as wood. I know I won’t be able to put them on tomorrow morning, even if I try to force them on while hopping on one foot. There’s even a risk my foot could get stuck in them—frozen shoes are like cement blocks.

The pre-moistened wipes are also frozen solid, just a single block of ice. No cleaning up tonight. I put the wipes into my sleeping bag to thaw for tomorrow morning. When you can’t wash with hot water and soap daily, it’s crucial to at least use wipes, especially after using the toilet. Luckily, I’m a morning person—that works in my favor.

The portable batteries and the telephone also go into the backpack. Toothpaste as well, if I want to brush my teeth with it. The water bottle, too. It’s funny because the bottle of sparkling water I have with me wasn’t actually frozen—very surprising, while the water in my other bottle was completely solid. Does sparkling water freeze at a different temperature than still water? I was very pleased with the discovery. But as I opened the bottle to take a drink or two, the escaping gas hissed, and within seconds, all the water inside froze solid before my eyes. I assure you, it took only a few seconds.

You have to remember, I tell myself, that the decompression of gas produces cold—that’s the very principle of refrigeration. That sudden drop in temperature was the last straw. The experience was amusing, but now the bottle had to sleep with me if I wanted to drink from it later.

The shoes also had to come into the sleeping bag, stored in a plastic bag to avoid dirtying or wetting the interior. But now that they are entirely frozen, I tell myself it’s a little late. I’ve decided to stay here tomorrow to dry them in the sun. If my estimates are correct, the sun should reach my tent late in the morning and stay until mid-afternoon. This should be enough, if not to dry them completely, at least to melt and soften them. I’ll then place them inside the sleeping bag to ensure I can put them on the following morning.

For now, I’m going to spend the night without these unpleasant sleeping companions, which, I assure you, give the impression of taking up all the space in the sleeping bag.

Everything I’ve placed in the sleeping bag is like an ice cube that I’ll have to warm up and melt. You can understand why it’s essential to get very warm before attempting this.

At these temperatures, I sleep with two pairs of socks, thermal tights, a long-sleeved thermal t-shirt, a second cotton t-shirt, a long-sleeved fleece jacket, and a thick hat.

I also place a fleece around my feet on top of the sleeping bag, along with a thin sleeveless down jacket. My sleeping bag has lost a lot of its insulation over the years. I’ve used it intensively for over fifteen years. Additionally, my feet often touch the bottom of the tent. This area is made of mesh canvas to allow interior ventilation, making it very cold and prone to condensation. Ice forms there and clings to the end of my sleeping bag whenever it touches.

Because my sleeping bag is "heated" by my body, the ice melts and gradually soaks the fabric, then the down. Perhaps you didn’t know, but wet down loses all its insulating properties. While it’s possible to sleep with cold feet, it’s much better to sleep with warm ones! By covering the sleeping bag with a fleece and down jacket, it’s the outer layers that get wet instead of the bag itself. I sleep much better this way.

However, in the morning, I have to put on damp or wet clothes—often frozen and stiff. If it’s really cold, by the time I finish the morning tidying-up ritual, they’ve dried somewhat, and the tent’s ceiling drips with the humidity released during the night.

Finally, I eat something: bread that has become very hard and cold, cheese that I break more than I cut, and two frozen tomatoes.

Quickly, my arms outside the sleeping bag feel the sharp sting of the cold, so I pull them back under. I lie still. I fall asleep immediately. It must be 9 p.m. It’s very late for me—normally, I sleep around 7 p.m. But I’ve already completed one cycle. I’ll manage to catch up tomorrow morning.

Everything is fine.

I am awakened in the morning by the dull, slow sound of a heavy vehicle. A snowplow bulldozer is followed by a forestry machine for handling and transporting wooden logs. I did well to move out of the way. I hadn’t expected this, but it’s a pleasant surprise. Tomorrow, I’ll walk on a path with only a day’s worth of snow! I wonder how many kilometers these machines will clear.

Two hours later, the vehicles return in the opposite direction. The first one honks. The second stops. I poke my head out of the tent, a big smile on my face, and wave hello. The response from the two passengers is very enthusiastic. One of them asks, with the window down, what I’m doing there. It’s a classic question, and I respond with the usual: French/Pilgrim/Not understanding/English? One of them speaks English. He asks me if I’m going up or down.

I tell him I’m going up, aiming to cross the pass tomorrow or the day after, depending on whether I stop at the mountain refuge cabin near the pass. In Romania’s mountains, there are many mountain shelters: small wooden cabins with just a plank bunk bed, a window, and a bear-proof armored door, always left open to welcome travelers.

He looks at me, and I can tell he’s assessing my credibility. The weather has worsened; heavy snow is falling. The silence in our conversation lingers a little longer than socially comfortable. He must be wondering whether I’m completely crazy, reckless, or superhuman. But then again, he’s meeting me just after I’ve spent a night here in minus 20°C. I’m clearly doing fine—smiling and everything. I must seem like I know what I’m doing. He suddenly wishes me good luck, gives me a thumbs-up, and drives off.

The box of vegetable pâté is frozen solid, its contents iced. I slice it as thinly as possible to place on the sliced bread. Spreading it is impossible, even though the bread is hard enough to handle strong pressure. (This is cold bread.) The orange is frosty too.

I quickly tuck my arms back under the sleeping bag and shiver for a moment. Getting out of the bag to say hello was enough to lose the accumulated warmth, but the sun will soon help.

I wait until I’m warm again to place my shoes where they can best absorb the life-saving rays that will soon hit the entrance and side of the tent. This is an important detail to consider when setting up the tent: in winter, you seek the sun; in summer, you avoid it.

The advantage of the tent, especially in a dark color, is that as soon as the sun hits it, the interior temperature rises immediately. Right now, this is a welcome benefit.

So and very quickly it gets above zero inside. The entire canvas is dripping with condensation. I then open the entrance so that everything evaporates outside. That's less humidity inside. As it is always cold (you know the thing between real temperature and felt temperature...), drying is quick. In a few minutes, the condensation disappears and the tent fabric is dry.

My shoes defrost quickly, and as soon as they are soft enough, I take off my socks so as not to wet them, immerse my warm feet in the very cold and still a little hard shoes, and leave the tent for the mandatory inspection tour.

1: Remove the snow from the tent. Although I do it regularly from the inside by tapping and shaking the canvas, there is about ten cm on the top.
      2: Clear the snow around the tent so that the space between the flysheet and the ground is clear and air can circulate there. In addition to breathing, which is important in life, this also prevents too much snow from melting on the bottom of the flysheet and refreezing during the night, which makes it much more difficult to fold the tent in the morning because ice packs remain attached to the canvas.
    3: Loosen the sardines so that they come loose and move in their housings. Some are stuck by ice. They have to be forced. If it doesn't move, light kicks so as not to break the frozen metal, but strong enough to break the ice bond with the ground. Everything is in the right balance... Too strong and you cry about the breakage of a sardine. Too weak and you cry in the morning not being able to extract it.
     4: Finally, tighten the tie-downs, which soften a few times during the night.

I finally return after a toilet detour. I then clean myself with the wipes that remained in the duvet, which is still a little warm.

After this busy morning, a nap is called for, and I indulge in the gentle warmth that now reigns in the tent. About three hours later, I wake up feeling perky. The temperature has dropped, the sun no longer shines. It must be minus ten.

I bring the shoes inside. They are almost dry; this can be seen in the color of the leather. I close the entrance. I surround the shoes with the long canvas bag that holds the tent, the groundsheet, and the inflatable mattress in the morning, to protect them a little from the frost. We put straw at the foot of the fruit or rose bushes so that it does not freeze.

Because there are two forms of frozen shoes. The first is that they were soaked. It then freezes at -10C. The second is when the temperature drops to -20 and beyond. There the shoes freeze even if they were dry. Because in this state that we commonly call "dry," the leather retains a certain percentage of humidity, very low certainly, but sufficient to become very hard at -20C. By surrounding the shoes with thick canvas, as well as blocking the entrance to the shoe with a large sock rolled into a ball, I reduce the risk of freezing.

The temperature continues to drop. The sun will set soon. There is something that I sense more and more. It's a little complicated to explain because it comes from feeling. I can tolerate the cold when it's there. -10C, -25C, that's no problem. I even experienced nights in -35C and once -43C in the Himalayas. On the other hand, the drop in temperature is painful. It's strange.

To make an explanatory comparison, I would say that it is like speed.
    We tolerate being at a high speed very easily; however, acceleration is difficult for the body. Well, I feel a bit the same thing with the temperature. Acceleration (deceleration in this case) of temperature is significantly more painful than remaining at a constant temperature at the lowest. I am not sure whether I am clear in what I am expressing, nor whether this is a reality other than totally subjective. But the impression is strong...

As I think about this and write it on my phone, I feel the bite of the cold. Fingers go numb. It stings... and suddenly the phone goes off. Too cold for him. I tuck it under the sleeping bag, and there, wrapped up in the sleeping bag, I notice that my breath has condensed on the sleeping bag right in front of my nose. There is now a frozen patch there, all hard and shiny. This is a sign that it must be in the -15C to -20C range.

I fall asleep like a baby and stay with Morpheus until morning. It was cold, but I was warm. Everything is fine. The fleece around the bottom of my sleeping bag is hard. The bottom of my pants, which folded forms part of my pillow, too. It was really cold.

I also realize this when I try to get out of the tent once all the morning rituals have been completed. The tent zipper is frozen and really hard to open. That of the double roof impossible. I have to heat it with a gas lighter, which itself has to be heated in my pocket for a few minutes for it to work. By passing the flame gently along the closure, gently, the ice on the other side melts and after about ten minutes I am able to open it. What happens is that the heat from inside the tent, coming from my body, escapes to the outside of course, but like everything else, it uses the path of least resistance to do so. The zipper is one of them. And therefore becomes a hot spot compared to the rest of the flysheet. So when the snow falls, the snow that arrives there tends to melt first, then to be covered with fresh snow which also begins to melt and so on until an equilibrium is achieved. What is melted ends up freezing and thus forms a more or less thick film of ice which completely blocks the zipper. I really wish the tent manufacturer had thought of making a small outer flap covering the zipper to prevent this. Maybe I'll send them the suggestion...

When I finally manage to go out, I realize that it had snowed during the night. Approximately 20 cm. And it's still snowing. It's snowing well; I can't see 50 m away...

I take out my backpack, the inflatable mattress, and begin the ritual of folding the tent.

First, I clear the snow accumulated on and around it. I then extract the sardines which are obviously frozen and stuck in their holes. Fortunately, I planned this the day before because I would never have been able to extract them without it. A few calculated bumps and kicks later and they are removed and put away in their pouch.

The tent is technically free now, but it's stuck to the ground. During my stay here, the snow and frozen ground I was standing on melted and then refroze, especially during the half hour I was already outside. So now I have to “peel” the tent from the groundsheet and the groundsheet from the ground. Slowly of course because as everything is frozen everything is fragile. When I can finally move the tent and groundsheet freely, it's time to dismantle the poles that give the tent its shape.

First of all, the eyecups in which the end of the hoops are placed are a pile of snow, ice and plant debris that must be broken before the whole thing can be disassembled. I have to take a break to warm up my hands. Too cold. They are all numb and my fingers melt badly. I place them in my pockets, near the femoral artery which provides a lot of heat. I walk back and forth to the side of the tent, on the path which was cleared yesterday but which is already well covered with powder so as not to cool down too much.

After a few minutes I find the digital functionality and only then can I really start dismantling the tent. The arches released from the eyecups do not move. They are stuck to the fabric through which they pass. These were humidified by condensation and as it constitutes a really cold point (aluminum and its derivatives are excellent thermal conductors...) they froze and the condensation stuck. You have to manipulate them cm by cm to break the ice and free them.

Hand warming break...

So I extract them from their housing and place them on the ground. I roll the flysheet into a ball and crush it all over to break up as much of the ice that is still attached to it as possible. Then I shake it to get as much out as possible.

Hand warming break.

The story I’m about to tell you made me cry—really cry—hot and heavy tears for over an hour. Yet, I’m pretty sure you’ll laugh...

Life in the tent is punctuated by rituals. Unpacking and repacking your backpack every day, without forgetting anything and ensuring that everything is properly placed, requires it. Every item comes out of the bag, goes into the tent, and then back into the bag again.

I sleep on the right side. Nothing to do with the “right side of the bed” couples talk about—it’s simply that the zipper of the tent entrance, which is a mosquito net, makes it more convenient to be on this side rather than 30 cm further.

My half-full backpack, stuffed with winter clothes, is to my left, level with my thigh. It fits well in that spot, as long as it’s positioned slightly at an angle and, importantly, with the straps facing me. This way, the rounded part of the bag aligns with the tent floor without pressing against the tent wall. The goal is to avoid contact between the tent fabric and the double roof. If the two fabrics touch and it rains, capillary action will cause water to seep through. We don’t want that.

This bag position also allows easy access to the two belt pockets on the backpack and the anti-bear spray, which is fixed midway up in a pocket I designed myself on the shoulder strap. It’s pointless to keep a spray at the bottom of the bag. If needed, you can ask a bear to wait a minute—but I assure you, it’s completely useless.

Near my left arm are two waterproof (well, nearly waterproof) boxes I use to store food. These prevent any leaks from finding their way onto my sleeping bag. I’m sure you get the idea.

At the level of my head, in the corner, are my water bottles and canteens. Finally, at my feet, I store items that I won’t need in the evening: the compression sack for the sleeping bag, my hat...

In the tent’s awning, my shoes are placed at the top right. The left side has an elastic band used to roll up and secure the door when it’s open. However, when it rains, the seam of this elastic slowly lets water in. It drips—not much, but enough that if a shoe is left there overnight, it might be filled with water by morning. We definitely don’t want that.

To the left of the awning is where I place rubbish—paper, plastic, and similar items—until morning, when I collect it all to throw into the first trash bin I find.

Then there’s a large bottle with a wide neck that I’ve had for a while now. It’s placed on the opposite side of the shoes. I could explain what it’s for, but it’s not essential to the story, and some of you might not be ready for that. Let’s just say that when sleeping in a tent, such a bottle is incredibly handy for men in their fifties who don’t want to waste the hard-earned warmth in their sleeping bags by getting up multiple times during the night...

Why am I telling you all this?

Well, imagine that tonight, as I was turning to adjust my pillow (my jacket and pants rolled up in a very specific way, offering real comfort all night long), I got on all fours on my self-inflating mattress. This mattress, about 3 or 4 cm thick, inflates on its own when unrolled and only requires a few breaths to make it comfortable before closing the valve. It allows you to sleep on damp and cold ground—even on snow or ice, as often happens to me in winter.

And then disaster struck.

From that moment, everything happened quickly—a rapid succession of actions, reactions, questions, decisions, and regrets, all compressed into a few dozen seconds, probably less than a minute.

As I got into position, on all fours, ready to reshape my pillow, I heard a loud Pchiiiittttt and felt a breath of air against my thigh. My first thought was that I had just punctured my mattress with my knee pressing on a sharp stone or thorn. I assumed it was likely a cutting stone because the Pschiiit was powerful, and I felt the air escaping against my skin, indicating a large opening.

Of course, I instinctively shifted and turned around to see what was happening—my first mistake, albeit an understandable one. Anyone would have done the same.

And then everything escalated.

I inhaled, and the mist scratched my throat and lungs. Something was very wrong. That’s when I realized what had happened: I had accidentally triggered the bear spray.

Normally, the spray has a safety pin to prevent this from happening. But today, I had walked under many low-hanging branches, and it’s entirely possible that one of them dislodged the pin without me noticing.

I don’t check my gear in the evening anymore. I know it would be more logical to do so—I used to at the beginning of my pilgrimage—but for some time now, after a long day of walking, I’m too exhausted to do anything but lie down and rest. I only check my equipment in the morning. That’s when I inspect for holes in my clothes, loose straps, places that need reinforcing, and, of course, the bear spray—specifically, whether its safety pin is in place. I devote about two evenings a week to equipment maintenance. It’s not as though “we” build solid things these days...

So there I was, turning around, my face in the cloud, breathing in. I told myself, "It itches, but it’s fine... It’s not that strong. I’ll finish this breath, hold it, and go out." I even thought, This spray isn’t very effective. It’s not strong enough to repel a bear determined to steal my cheese.

That was a mistake—an important one. Looking back, I now believe that if you ever find yourself in the same situation, it doesn’t matter whether you’re inhaling or exhaling—just block your breath immediately, wherever it is... for about 40 minutes, at least. If you pass out, even better—it will spare you what’s coming next.

And remember to close your eyes. I almost forgot that part. Closed eyes are better, even if, like me, you think it’s too late. Just close them.

With my breath held, lungs full, and in a high position, I turned around as quickly as I could and felt for the zipper tab. Thankfully, I knew where it was because of practice and the way I position myself in the tent—it’s always in the same spot. That was fortunate, because by this time, my eyes were blurred with tears beginning to form. But my eyes weren’t the main issue—it was my throat.

My throat itched severely, burning like hell (if you’ll allow the comparison). It was awful. Then I started coughing, which didn’t help me open the mosquito net, let alone hold my breath. Every cough required me to inhale, which was both inevitable and excruciating.

I managed to reach the main opening in the double roof. It has two zippers that leave the upper central part of the fabric open for ventilation when desired, which I’d left open that night. I plunged my head out into the “fresh” air. I say “fresh” because, with the burning sensation in my throat, it didn’t feel refreshing at all.

Now on my knees in the awning, I found the two zippers and pulled them down simultaneously to close the opening.

The coughing became violent. It felt like I was about to cough my lungs out. I wished my throat would simply cease to exist. My face, hands, and thigh burned like I’d received four overlapping sunburns all at once.

I was terrified of vomiting. I had to concentrate hard to prevent it—not just because of the cleanup that would follow, but also because the combination of coughing, extreme difficulty in breathing, and vomiting could be dangerous, even suffocating.

I manage to extract myself from the tent, crawling on all fours. I move about ten meters away and collapse there, feeling like I might die—or almost. Suffocation... Violent coughing, my eyes streaming, my nose running, my mouth burning, and saliva dripping onto the ground. I can only breathe in short, jerky gasps, focusing intensely on calming myself enough to get some oxygen. Oxygen is important, after all...

And then the burning starts. It was already burning, but now it intensifies. It rises and rises, building even more. Touching the affected areas is a serious mistake—don’t do it. Don’t touch. Must not touch. I’m pretty sure it’s swelling too. I have to wait... 10 minutes... 20 minutes... 30 minutes...

Finally, it starts to subside. And that’s when I have the worst good idea of the year.

I’m camped next to a water source. It’s great to have running water when you’re sleeping in a tent. Spring water means freshness. Really fresh, even cold. With the burning sensation, you can imagine the allure, the temptation. It’s like a jug of lemonade with ice cubes clinking in front of someone lost in the Sahara! Or like a Coulommiers cheese on a table in front of a homesick Norman expat! Or a basket of chanterelles in front of... well, you get the point.

I stand up and walk past the tent to grab the tea towel/handkerchief/cleaning rag drying on top of it. I’m already imagining the bliss of a cloth soaked in clear, fresh water, gently placed on my burning skin. I even wonder if it will make a sizzling sound, like water hitting a hot pan, when I put it over my face.

Bitter mistake. The cold relief never comes. At first, it seems fine—good, even. But no. It’s a mirage. Placebo effect. The burning sensation returns, worse than before. But not immediately! No, it gives you just enough time to wet all the affected areas you thought would be soothed. And then, it hits. Too late, though—you’ve already committed. And now you’re crying again...

Anyway, I’ve just endured two truly painful hours, and now I’m waiting for the right moment to go back into the tent and try to sleep. Meanwhile, I’m wondering what I’ll do if I don’t find the spray’s safety pin. The chances of finding it are slim, but I have hope because one of the spots where the branches rubbed against my gear is just above the spring near where I set up camp. Maybe, with a little luck...

Et voilà.

This, along with the time my tent flooded a few months ago, will definitely remain one of the most ridiculous experiences I’ve had on this pilgrimage. Definitely. But this one is already funny, in hindsight. So everything is fine... no real harm done.

See you soon.

It’s the kind of water you drink even when you’re not thirsty, until your stomach feels tight because you’ve overdone it. Yes, I assure you... I speak from experience. Respect.

Yesterday, I told myself that I would take the road again after spending two days near this stream at the edge of the village, where the local children, Gypsies, had built a kind of small swimming pool out of odds and ends—sticks, plastics, and stones. The small water reservoir was really appealing. I relaxed in it, washed myself, and washed my clothes there. Every evening, when it was quiet, I returned to soak until I was fully satisfied or until the sun disappeared and night fell.

I had placed my tent in the hedge adjacent to the spot, where it was thick enough to shelter me from the sun, which burns strongly when it’s not raining. I was in the shade and protected from the view of passers-by on the road or the swimmers who came during the day. The spot was perfect—so hidden that even if someone walked less than five meters away, it was almost impossible to see my tent. Even I, knowing its location, had to make a real effort to spot it when heading to the river, about 50 meters downhill.

That morning, as had been the case for some time, I was torn between continuing my journey or staying another day, knowing the consequences. I had one banana and one madeleine left to eat, and three liters of water to drink. The water was enough to last until the next evening and the walk the day after to the next well or spring, whether natural or human-made.

As for food, it was tight. I decided to save the madeleine for the next evening and the banana for the departure the day after because walking on a completely empty stomach slows me down more than I already am from fatigue. So, I chose to stay and enjoy the luxury of a garden swimming pool, even if it meant fasting for a while. Fasting now and then is good...

When I finally left, my plan was to pass through the next village, 7 km away, to look for a grocery store (though none was indicated on my map) to buy food. Then, I’d walk another 2 km to find a place in the forest to pitch my tent and spend two days resting. You know the cycle I already described in my last message. I also needed to figure out how to recharge my power banks. I had no charge left in two of the three, and only 40% in the last one.

Normally, I expect to be invited to charge my batteries at the grocery stores or bars in the villages I pass through. After buying food and sipping coffee or a drink, I typically spend an hour or so charging everything.

But here’s the problem: I had no more money with me. The local currency is Lei, and I had only 15 Lei left. I had Euros and my bank card, but the small village grocery store didn’t accept either. You can see the complexity here—no way to buy food and no real excuse to ask to stay an hour to recharge my phone. This was a situation that could potentially become problematic, forcing me to walk 45 km to the next ATM.

But it’s been three years that everything has gone wonderfully well, and I am starting to get used to it—a certain confidence is becoming anchored.

When I speak of habit, I should clarify that it does not alter the quality of perception and wonder. At least, I hope not to get used to it in that way. Simply, the occurrence of these positive outcomes is such that I no longer listen to anxieties, doubts, or uncertainties. Despite what could easily become unpleasant or even difficult, I’ve reached an irrational yet very real serenity: it’s going to be fine.

So, here I am in this village. And there’s a grocery store! I find it easily. The lady running it does not accept cards or Euros, as expected and anticipated.

But she tells me to wait, grabs her phone, and calls her boss. He arrives 15 minutes later, agrees to exchange some Euros for me, and allows me to recharge my batteries in the shop.

I wait outside, sitting on the benches set up for the local regulars. Thirty minutes pass. An hour. The first battery gets recharged, but the other two, which typically take five hours, are not done yet. I tell myself that I can continue charging them partially until my phone, which is also charging, reaches 100%. Then I’ll leave. So I begin writing this message...

My bag is full of food and water. I’m ready...

And then the boss comes back with a friend and a bottle, offering me a beer and a polanta (a little plum! Or in this case, a pear). We talk.

He also has a guesthouse in the village, and after hearing my plan to pitch my tent in the next forest, he suggests I set it up in the garden of his guesthouse instead. I can shower and do laundry if I want—and of course, I will! I can also finish recharging my batteries there.

I met a couple of very nice hosts there, about to leave as they were taking the road early in the morning. I was invited by the owner of the place to share supper—a good hot vegetable soup. It was a nice change from bread, cheese, and tomatoes.

We spent the evening together discussing various things. They offered me a packet of dried fruit, a kilo of apricots, a bar of chocolate, a dozen hard-boiled eggs, and two cereal bars.

I said goodbye to them in the early morning and took the opportunity to remove my laundry from the line where it was trying to dry, because despite the lingering night, the sky was heavy with clouds. And, in fact, a few moments before I went back to sleep in the shelter of my tent, the rain began to fall.

Now, I’m seated at the patio table. Everyone has gone, and I’m alone. I’ll close the gate when I leave, as agreed. I’m finishing this message while charging my phone. In a moment, I’ll be back on the road, freshly washed, my clothes clean, my batteries and phone fully charged, my bags full of food and fruit, my water bottles full of water, and my pocket full of Lei (well, full enough 😜).

I don’t know why, but again I feel the need to explain these small, almost intangible things. Actually, it’s not so much about explaining as it is about sharing. But to share, I feel compelled to explain everything in detail: everything is fine... It went well. Kindness is a succulent spice.

It’s been like this for three years. In addition to being really pleasant to live with, it’s fortifying.

Voilà. That was a little hello from Transylvania.
